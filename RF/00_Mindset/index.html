RF & High-Speed Design · Module 0

Mindset shift: why RF is different

Everything in the EE notes still holds at radio frequencies, but several assumptions that were safe at low frequency stop being safe. Wires have delay. Current crowds into the surface of conductors and returns along paths you didn't draw. Ground stops being a single node. And the energy turns out to travel in the space between the conductors, not in the copper. This module rebuilds those assumptions from first principles before the rest of the RF notes rely on them.

Builds on: EE modules 1–4. Bench: oscilloscope, a long coax cable, NanoVNA or any VNA.

Wavelength and the size of things

The EE notes treated a circuit as a set of nodes joined by ideal wires, with Kirchhoff's laws holding at every instant. That model makes a hidden assumption: a change at one end of a wire appears at the other end instantly. In reality it travels at a finite speed, close to the speed of light in air and slower in a circuit board. Whether that matters depends on how far the signal travels compared with how fast it changes.

For a sine wave, the natural measure is the wavelength: the distance the wave travels during one cycle.

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} \qquad v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_\text{eff}}}$$
c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; εeff ≈ 3.3 for a trace on FR-4, so signals travel at about 0.55c, or 6 ns per metre

Compare the wavelengths of common radio bands with the sizes of things on a board or in a vehicle:

At mains frequency, a wire would have to be hundreds of kilometres long before the delay mattered. At 2.4 GHz, a single circuit board is longer than a wavelength. That's the whole reason RF design needs a different way of thinking: the circuit is no longer small compared with the wave travelling through it.

The lumped-to-distributed transition

A circuit is lumped when it's small enough that every point on a wire sees the same voltage at the same moment. Then resistance, capacitance and inductance can be treated as if they sit at single points, in the components, and Kirchhoff's laws hold. A circuit is distributed when a signal takes a noticeable time to cross it. Then different points on the same wire hold different voltages at the same instant, and the wire itself has to be treated as part of the circuit.

What decides it isn't the frequency of the signal but how fast it changes. A 1 kHz square wave from a fast logic chip has edges lasting a nanosecond, and during each edge the voltage is changing as fast as a gigahertz sine wave. So there's a time-domain version of the comparison: the time the signal takes to travel the connection against the time its edges take to rise.

On FR-4, with about 60 ps of delay per centimetre, a 1 ns edge makes any trace longer than about 3 cm a transmission line. Module 1 gives the equivalent rule for sine waves in terms of wavelength.

Every component is an RLC network

The same idea applies inside components. A resistor has leads or end caps and a body, so current through it builds a magnetic field (inductance), and its two ends sit close together with a dielectric between them (capacitance). These parasitics are tiny, typically a fraction of a nanohenry and a fraction of a picofarad, so at low frequency they don't matter. Their impedances scale with frequency, though, so at RF they can dominate. You met one case in EE Module 3: a capacitor's ESL makes it behave as an inductor above its self-resonant frequency. Every part does something similar.

$$X_L = 2\pi f L \qquad X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$$
0.5 nH is 3 Ω at 1 GHz; 0.05 pF is 3.2 kΩ at 1 GHz

Design exerciseIs a slow signal lumped?

A logic chip sends a 1 kHz square wave down a 30 cm ribbon cable. Its edges rise in about 1 ns.

Should the cable be treated as lumped or distributed?

  1. Lumped: at 1 kHz the wavelength is hundreds of kilometres.
    The 1 kHz repetition rate isn't what matters. Think about how fast the voltage changes during each edge.
  2. Distributed: the edges take 1 ns, while the signal takes about 1.5 to 2 ns to cross the cable, so the two ends disagree during every edge.
    What matters is the rise time against the delay, not the repetition rate.
  3. Lumped, as long as the cable is shielded.
    Shielding changes the fields outside the cable, not the delay along it.
  4. It depends only on the chip's supply voltage.
    The supply voltage sets the size of the edge, not its speed.

Between edges nothing is changing, so the cable is irrelevant. During each edge, the near end rises in 1 ns while the far end won't start rising until the signal has travelled 30 cm, which takes 1.5 to 2 ns in a ribbon cable. The cable is distributed for the edges, and if it isn't terminated, the edges will ring (Module 1). A slow repetition rate with fast edges is the classic way digital signals become RF problems, and the usual fix is to slow the edges down if the signal doesn't need them fast.

Design exerciseA pull-up resistor at 915 MHz

A 10 kΩ pull-up resistor sits on a control pin of a 915 MHz radio chip, next to the RF output, and some RF reaches it.

At 915 MHz, what does the resistor look like?

  1. 10 kΩ: resistors don't depend on frequency.
    Ideal resistors don't. Real ones have capacitance between their ends.
  2. Much less than 10 kΩ: its end-to-end capacitance of about 0.05 pF has an impedance of only a few kilohms at 915 MHz, in parallel with the resistance.
    At high frequency the parasitic capacitance provides a lower-impedance path than the resistor itself.
  3. Much more than 10 kΩ, because of its inductance.
    Its inductance adds only a few ohms at 915 MHz, negligible next to 10 kΩ.
  4. An open circuit.
    The parasitic capacitance makes it the opposite of an open circuit at high frequency.

0.05 pF at 915 MHz is about 3.5 kΩ of reactance, in parallel with the 10 kΩ, so the part looks like about 3.3 kΩ and is mostly capacitive. High-value resistors are the first to be affected, because their parasitic capacitance only has to compete with a large resistance. It's why RF designs use low-value resistors where RF is present and isolate high-value parts with inductors or ferrite beads. Check it in the figure above.

The skin effect

At DC, current spreads evenly across a wire's cross-section. At high frequency it crowds into a thin layer at the surface. The reason follows from what you already know about inductance.

Current in a wire creates a magnetic field, inside the wire as well as around it. Picture the wire as many thin parallel filaments. A filament at the centre is surrounded by the field of all the others, so it links more magnetic flux than a filament at the surface: it has more inductance. At DC that doesn't matter, because inductance only opposes changes in current. At high frequency the centre filaments' larger inductive impedance (\(2\pi f L\)) pushes the current out towards the surface, where the impedance is lowest. The result is that current flows in a layer roughly one skin depth thick:

$$\delta = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f \mu}}$$
ρ is the resistivity and μ the permeability; for copper, δ ≈ 66 µm at 1 MHz and about 2 µm at 1 GHz

Since the current only uses a thin outer layer, the effective area shrinks and the resistance rises. For a round wire much thicker than δ, the current uses a ring of area about \(\pi d\,\delta\), so the AC resistance is about \(d/4\delta\) times the DC resistance, and it rises with \(\sqrt{f}\).

Two practical consequences. First, the surface matters more than the bulk: RF cable conductors are often silver-plated, and the roughness of PCB copper adds loss because current has to follow the bumps (Module 1 mentions this). Second, the skin effect is part of why high-frequency signals lose more energy per metre than low-frequency ones.

Design exerciseSteel inside a copper wire

Some coax cables, including the common RG-316, use a centre conductor of steel with a thin copper or silver plating. The plating is several skin depths thick at the operating frequency, and steel's resistivity is about ten times copper's.

At 1 GHz, how does its resistance compare with a solid copper conductor of the same diameter?

  1. About ten times higher, because the core is steel.
    That would be true at DC. Think about where the current flows at 1 GHz.
  2. Nearly the same, because at 1 GHz the current flows in the outer few micrometres, all of it in the plating.
    The current never reaches the steel at 1 GHz, so the core's resistivity doesn't matter.
  3. Lower, because steel is magnetic.
    Steel's magnetic permeability would make the skin depth even thinner in the steel, but the current isn't in the steel anyway.
  4. Half, because the plating is a better conductor.
    The plating is no better than solid copper at the surface.

At 1 GHz the skin depth in copper is about 2 µm, so a plating several micrometres thick carries essentially all the current, and the conductor behaves like solid copper. The steel core adds strength and makes the cable cheaper, with no RF penalty. The catch is at DC and low frequency, where the current uses the whole cross-section and the steel's higher resistivity shows. That matters if the same cable carries DC power to a device, for example an active GPS antenna.

Return current

Current flows in loops (EE Module 1). Every signal current going out along a trace comes back along some return path, usually through the ground plane. At low frequency, the return current spreads out and takes the path of least resistance, which across a solid plane means a broad sweep roughly along the straight line between the two ends.

At high frequency the impedance of the loop is dominated by its inductance, \(2\pi f L\), not its resistance. Inductance is set by the area of the loop: the bigger the area enclosed between the outgoing and returning currents, the more magnetic flux they link. So at high frequency the return current takes the path of least inductance, which is the path that keeps the loop area smallest: directly underneath the signal trace, following every turn it makes. On a typical board the switch from one behaviour to the other happens somewhere between about 10 kHz and 1 MHz.

Underneath a trace at height h above the plane, the return current density falls off with the distance x to either side as \(1/\big(1 + (x/h)^2\big)\), so most of it flows within a few trace heights of the centreline.

Things to try
  1. At 1 kHz, does the return current follow the trace? At 10 MHz?
  2. At 10 MHz, add the slot. Where does the return current go now, and what has happened to the loop area?

A slot, split or gap in the ground plane under a fast trace forces the return current to detour around it. The loop area grows, and with it the inductance. A large loop also radiates and picks up interference, and the detouring current flows under other traces, coupling noise into them. It's one of the most common causes of EMC failures (Module 7), and it's easy to create by accident: a split between "analog" and "digital" ground, a row of closely spaced vias, or a cut-out for a connector.

Design exerciseA trace across a split

A board's ground plane is split into two halves, joined only at one corner. A fast clock trace runs straight across the split, from one half to the other.

What is the main problem?

  1. None: the two halves are connected, so the return current has a path.
    It has a path, but think about where the path goes.
  2. The return current can't follow under the trace, so it detours through the corner connection, forming a large loop that radiates, picks up noise and couples into other traces.
    At high frequency the return current wants to be under the trace, and the split forces a long detour.
  3. The trace's DC resistance increases.
    The trace itself is unchanged. The problem is in the return path.
  4. The clock frequency changes.
    The frequency is set by the clock source. The return path affects how cleanly the edges arrive and how much they radiate.

At high frequency, return current flows directly under its trace. Where the trace crosses the split, the current under it has nowhere to go and must travel along the split to the corner and back, enclosing a large loop. That loop is an inductor in series with the signal (slowing and distorting the edges) and an antenna (radiating, and receiving). The fixes: route fast traces only over solid plane, or if a split is unavoidable, put a stitching capacitor or a bridge in the plane right where the trace crosses.

Ground is not ground

EE Module 2 treated ground as one node, everywhere at 0 V, and noted that its resistance can shift it when large currents flow. At RF the bigger issue is inductance. Every connection to ground has some: about 0.5 nH for a via through a standard board, and roughly 1 nH per millimetre for a wire. Those numbers are small, but at RF they aren't:

$$X = 2\pi f L: \qquad 0.5\ \text{nH at 915 MHz} \approx 2.9\ \Omega \qquad 20\ \text{nH at 915 MHz} \approx 115\ \Omega$$

A 2 cm wire "to ground" has an impedance of over 100 Ω at 915 MHz, twice the impedance of the 50 Ω circuits it's supposed to be the reference for. It isn't a ground at that frequency; it's a component. This is the same effect as the oscilloscope's long ground lead in EE Module 3, at a larger scale.

It gets stranger when the connection is a noticeable fraction of a wavelength. A wire shorted to ground at its far end reflects the signal back, and when the wire is a quarter-wavelength long, the reflection arrives back at the near end exactly in phase to cancel the current: the shorted wire looks like an open circuit. So a "ground" connection can be at its worst at exactly the frequency you care about. Module 1 develops the transmission-line theory behind this.

Design exerciseA shield grounded through a pigtail

A cable's braided shield is twisted into a 5 cm pigtail and soldered to a ground pad. The cable carries 900 MHz telemetry nearby.

How well is the shield grounded at 900 MHz?

  1. Well: copper wire has only milliohms of resistance.
    Its resistance is milliohms, but its impedance at 900 MHz is dominated by inductance.
  2. Poorly: the pigtail is several hundred ohms at 900 MHz, so the shield is barely connected to ground there.
    5 cm of wire is tens of nanohenries, and at 900 MHz it's also a noticeable fraction of a wavelength.
  3. Well, as long as the solder joint is good.
    The solder joint is fine. The length of the pigtail is the problem.
  4. It depends only on the braid coverage.
    Braid coverage matters for the shield itself, not for how it's connected to ground.

At 900 MHz a 5 cm pigtail is about 0.15 λ, and its impedance is over 300 Ω. Shield current that should flow smoothly to ground instead develops a voltage along the pigtail, and the shield itself starts radiating. That's why RF and EMC-critical cables use connectors that contact the shield all the way around, with no pigtail at all. Try a 5 cm wire in the figure above.

Energy flows in the fields

In the EE notes, it was natural to think of energy flowing through the wires, like water through pipes. That picture is wrong, even at DC, and at RF the difference matters.

The voltage between two conductors means there's an electric field in the space between them. The current in them means there's a magnetic field around them. Electromagnetism says energy flows where both fields are present, in the direction at right angles to both. The rate of flow per unit area is the Poynting vector:

$$\vec S = \vec E \times \vec H$$
in watts per square metre; E in volts per metre, H in amps per metre

Inside a good conductor the electric field is almost zero (the voltage drop along a wire is tiny), so almost no energy flows inside the copper. It flows through the space around and between the conductors, and the wires guide it. Even for a battery and a resistor connected by long wires, the energy travels through the space alongside the wires and enters the resistor from the outside.

DiagramEnergy travels in the space between conductors

On a circuit board, the fields of a trace are concentrated in the dielectric between it and the ground plane, with some spilling into the air above. That's where the signal's energy travels. Three consequences follow, and the rest of the RF notes rely on them:

  • The material between the conductors matters as much as the copper. Its dielectric constant sets the speed and the impedance, and its loss tangent sets how much energy is turned into heat (Module 1).
  • Anything inside the fields becomes part of the circuit. A nearby trace, a shield can, a finger, the inside of an enclosure or a carbon-fibre panel near an antenna all change the impedance and can pick up energy.
  • Antennas are just structures that let the fields escape. Keep the conductors close together and the fields stay confined; open them out and the energy leaves as a radio wave (Module 9).
Design exerciseThe antenna that detunes in your hand

A small telemetry antenna measures perfectly on the bench. When someone holds the board near the antenna, or it's installed inside a carbon-fibre body tube, the match gets much worse.

Why?

  1. The antenna's copper heats up.
    Nothing is heating the copper. Think about where the energy actually is.
  2. The antenna's fields extend into the hand or the carbon fibre, so those materials become part of the circuit and change its impedance.
    The energy is in the fields around the antenna, and anything in those fields changes how it behaves.
  3. The ground plane has moved.
    The board's ground hasn't changed. The surroundings have.
  4. The radio's output power drops when it's held.
    The radio is the same. The load it's driving has changed.

An antenna's job is to put energy into the fields around it, so its surroundings are part of its circuit. A hand adds lossy dielectric; carbon fibre is conductive and shields and detunes the antenna. That's why an antenna is always measured in the real installation, as Module 1's exercises point out, and why antennas usually need a non-conductive window in a carbon-fibre airframe.

The RF designer's mental model

None of this replaces the EE notes. Kirchhoff's laws, Ohm's law and Thévenin all still hold, as long as they're applied to small enough pieces of the circuit and every parasitic is included. What changes is which details are safe to ignore. The table summarises the shift:

Low-frequency habitRF habit
A wireA connection with no voltage drop and no delayA transmission line with delay and an impedance, once it's longer than about a tenth of a wavelength
A componentIts marked valueA small RLC network; its behaviour depends on frequency, package and mounting
Current in a conductorSpread across the whole cross-sectionIn a thin layer at the surface, a few micrometres deep
Return currentSomewhere in the ground, by the lowest-resistance pathDirectly under the signal path, by the lowest-inductance path
GroundOne node at 0 V everywhereA conductor with inductance; points on it differ in voltage, and long connections to it may not be ground at all
Where the energy isIn the wiresIn the fields between the conductors, and in anything those fields reach
A measurementThe probe barely affects the circuitThe probe, its cable and its ground connection are part of the circuit

In practice, an RF designer looks at a schematic or a layout and asks a short set of questions before calculating anything:

  • How long is each connection compared with the wavelength, or with the rise time?
  • Where does the return current flow, and is there a continuous path for it right under the signal?
  • What impedance does each part of the path have, and does it match the next part?
  • What does each component look like at this frequency, including its package and its connection to the board?
  • Where are the fields, and what else is inside them?

Most RF problems are answered, or at least located, by those questions. The rest of the RF notes give you the tools to answer them quantitatively.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●●○Resistance of a coax centre conductor at 915 MHz

A coax cable carrying 915 MHz telemetry has a solid copper centre conductor 0.5 mm in diameter.

Given Copper resistivity1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m Permeabilityμ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m Conductor diameter0.5 mm Frequency915 MHz

(a) Skin depth?

\(\delta = \sqrt{\rho / (\pi f \mu_0)}\).

\(\delta = \sqrt{1.72\times10^{-8} / (\pi \times 915\times10^6 \times 4\pi\times10^{-7})} \approx 2.18\ \mu\text{m}\).

(b) DC resistance per metre?

\(R = \rho / (\pi r^2) = 1.72\times10^{-8} / (\pi \times (0.25\times10^{-3})^2) = 87.6\ \text{m}\Omega/\text{m}\).

(c) Approximate resistance per metre at 915 MHz?

That's the DC value. At 915 MHz only a thin surface layer carries the current.

The current flows in a ring about one skin depth thick. Compare its area with the whole cross-section.

The ring has an area of about \(2\pi r\,\delta\), against \(\pi r^2\) for the whole wire, a ratio of \(r / 2\delta \approx 57\). So the resistance is about \(57 \times 87.6\ \text{m}\Omega \approx 5\ \Omega\) per metre, sixty times the DC value.

Discussion: where does that loss show up?

5 Ω per metre in a 50 Ω cable is significant, and it's one of the two main loss mechanisms in coax, along with loss in the dielectric. It's why cable loss is quoted in dB per metre at a given frequency, rising roughly with √f at lower frequencies. Thicker cables lose less because the centre conductor's circumference is larger, which is why long runs use thicker coax.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●○○A ground connection for an RF module

A small 915 MHz radio module is grounded to the main board through a 2 cm wire. A redesign replaces it with four vias directly under the module's ground pad.

Given 2 cm wireabout 20 nH One viaabout 0.5 nH Frequency915 MHz

(a) Impedance of the wire at 915 MHz?

\(X = 2\pi \times 915\times10^6 \times 20\times10^{-9} \approx 115\ \Omega\), more than twice the 50 Ω the radio is designed around.

(b) Impedance of the four vias in parallel?

Four equal inductors in parallel have a quarter of the inductance.

0.125 nH, so \(X = 2\pi \times 915\times10^6 \times 0.125\times10^{-9} \approx 0.72\ \Omega\), 160 times better than the wire.

Discussion: why four vias and not one?

One via is 2.9 Ω at 915 MHz, already acceptable for many purposes, but parallel vias divide the inductance and also share the current, which matters for a module drawing amps when it transmits. In practice the vias aren't quite independent: closely spaced vias share some of their magnetic field, so four vias give a bit less than a fourfold improvement. Spreading them around the pad helps.

Exercise 3Analysis · ●●○A ground wire that becomes an open circuit

An antenna's ground connection is an 8 cm wire to the airframe's metal bulkhead. Treat the wire as a line in air, shorted at the bulkhead end.

(a) At what frequency is the wire a quarter-wavelength long?

λ/4 = 8 cm, so λ = 32 cm. Then f = c/λ.

\(f = c / (4 \times 0.08) = 937\ \text{MHz}\), within a few percent of the 915 MHz telemetry band.

(b) How long would a wire be to be a quarter-wavelength at 2.4 GHz?

\(c / (4 \times 2.4\times10^9) = 31.2\ \text{mm}\).

Discussion: what does it mean for the antenna?

At 937 MHz, the shorted 8 cm wire looks like an open circuit at its near end, so the antenna's "ground" is effectively disconnected, close to the frequency it was meant to work at. The antenna's behaviour then depends on whatever else its fields find to use as a return path, typically the outside of the coax shield, which then radiates. A ground connection at RF should be short compared with the wavelength: a few millimetres, made with wide straps or many vias, not centimetres of wire.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A circuit is lumped only while a signal crosses it quickly compared with how fast it changes. Compare the delay with the rise time, or the length with the wavelength.
  2. Every component is an RLC network at high frequency. High-value resistors and large capacitors are affected first.
  3. At high frequency current flows in a thin surface layer, about 2 µm thick in copper at 1 GHz, so resistance rises with √f.
  4. High-frequency return current flows directly under its signal trace, along the lowest-inductance path. Gaps in the plane force detours, large loops and radiation.
  5. Ground connections have inductance: about 0.5 nH per via and 1 nH per millimetre of wire. A connection a quarter-wavelength long looks like an open circuit.
  6. Energy travels in the fields between conductors. The dielectric and anything near the conductors are part of the circuit.
