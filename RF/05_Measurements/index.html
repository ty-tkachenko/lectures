Measurements & lab tools

RF design isn't finished until it's been measured, and an RF measurement is only as good as the engineer's understanding of what the instrument is actually doing. This module explains how each of the main instruments works inside, what limits its accuracy, and how to use it so that the number on the screen describes the device and not the setup.

Choosing the instrument

Each RF instrument answers a narrow question very well and others badly. Most measurement mistakes start with using the wrong one, or with not knowing which question it's answering.

How a vector network analyzer works

An S-parameter is a ratio of two waves (Module 2): S₁₁ is the wave reflected from port 1 divided by the wave incident on it, S₂₁ the wave emerging from port 2 divided by the same incident wave. A VNA is a machine for measuring exactly these ratios. It sends a signal it controls into the device, separates the waves travelling in each direction with directional couplers (Module 3), and measures each wave's amplitude and phase with a receiver.

DiagramInside a two-port VNA, measuring in the forward direction

Three properties of this architecture explain most of what a VNA can and can't do:

  • It measures ratios. Because every reading is a wave divided by the incident wave, the source's power doesn't need to be accurate or flat. That's why a VNA is excellent at relative measurements (S-parameters) and not designed for absolute power.
  • Each receiver is a narrowband tuned receiver, a superheterodyne like the one in Module 3, following the source as it sweeps. Its IF bandwidth sets the noise: a narrower IF bandwidth lowers the noise floor (by 10 dB for every factor of ten), improving the dynamic range for measuring deep filter rejection, at the cost of a slower sweep.
  • The hardware is imperfect: the couplers leak, the ports aren't perfectly matched, and the cables between the instrument and the device add loss and phase. Those imperfections are systematic, repeatable, and removable by calibration.

A VNA can also show its data in the time domain. Taking the inverse Fourier transform of S₁₁ over a wide frequency span gives the reflection against time, the same picture as the TDR of Module 1. It's used to locate a discontinuity along a cable or a trace, and to gate it: to mathematically remove a reflection, such as a connector's, from the frequency response.

Calibration: the error model, SOLT and TRL

Calibration starts from a model of what goes wrong. Look at a one-port measurement (S₁₁ only). Everything between the ideal receivers and the device's connector, including the coupler, the internal cables, the port connector and any test cable, is a linear two-port network. Like any two-port, it can be described by S-parameters: three error terms are enough for a reflection measurement.

DiagramThe one-port error model and the SOLT standards

Following a wave through the error box: some of the incident wave leaks straight to the reflected-wave receiver without reaching the device (e₀₀, the coupler's finite directivity). The rest reaches the device, reflects, and travels back with a gain e₁₀e₀₁; on the way, part of it re-reflects from the imperfect port match (e₁₁) and bounces again. Summing the bounces, exactly as for the quarter-wave transformer in Module 2, gives the measured reflection:

$$\Gamma_m = e_{00} + \frac{e_{10}e_{01}\,\Gamma}{1 - e_{11}\,\Gamma}$$

There are three unknown complex terms at each frequency, so three measurements of known standards determine them: a short (Γ = −1), an open (Γ = +1) and a load (Γ = 0). That's an SOL calibration. With the error terms known, the equation is simply inverted for every subsequent measurement to recover the true Γ. A full two-port calibration adds a thru to characterise the transmission paths, giving the twelve-term SOLT calibration.

The calibration is only as good as the standards are known. The kit's definition file describes each standard's small imperfections: the open's fringing capacitance, the short's inductance and the offset length of each. Using a kit with the wrong definition is a common source of error, and so is a worn or dirty standard.

TRL (thru, reflect, line) calibration uses standards that are easy to build on a PCB: a direct thru, any highly reflective termination (which doesn't need to be known precisely), and a length of line whose impedance defines the reference. It's the standard way to calibrate at the end of PCB traces, inside a test fixture, so that the measurement describes the component and not the fixture's connectors and traces. Its limitation is bandwidth: the line must differ from the thru by between about 20° and 160° of electrical length, so a single line covers about an 8:1 frequency range.

Design exerciseAn antenna that looks too good

A VNA is calibrated at its own port, then a 3 m low-cost coax cable is added to reach an antenna mounted on a vehicle. The antenna shows a return loss of 25 dB across the whole band.

How much should that result be trusted?

  1. Fully: 25 dB is an excellent match.
    The cable is outside the calibration, so its loss and mismatch are part of the reading.
  2. Very little: the reflected wave crosses the uncalibrated cable twice, so the cable's loss improves the apparent return loss by twice its loss, and its own mismatch adds errors. The calibration should be done at the end of the cable.
    A lossy cable makes any load look better matched, by twice its loss; a long enough cable makes even an open circuit look like a good match.
  3. It's accurate, but the frequencies are shifted.
    The cable shifts phase, but its loss also changes the magnitude reading.
  4. The cable only affects S₂₁, not S₁₁.
    The reflected wave travels through the cable both ways.

Return loss measured through an uncalibrated cable is the antenna's return loss plus twice the cable's loss, with ripple from the cable's own mismatch on top. A 3 m cheap cable at 915 MHz might lose 1.5–3 dB, enough to turn a mediocre 20 dB antenna into an apparent 25 dB, or a 10 dB antenna into a 14 dB one. Moving the calibration plane to the end of the cable (calibrating there with the kit) removes the cable entirely.

VNA techniques

Most poor VNA measurements come from the setup, not the instrument. The practical rules follow from the error model: anything that changes after calibration, or that lies outside the calibration planes, is an error.

PracticeWhy
Calibrate over the frequency range and with the IF bandwidth and power you'll measure withError terms are measured at specific frequencies; interpolating between calibration points adds error.
Don't move or bend the test cables after calibrating; use phase-stable cablesA flexed cable changes its phase and loss, so the error terms no longer match the hardware.
Torque SMA connectors to their specification and keep them cleanA loose or dirty connector is a different, unrepeatable error term each time.
Measure amplifiers at low drive, with an attenuator if neededThe VNA's default power can compress a high-gain amplifier, or damage the receiver at port 2.
Narrow the IF bandwidth or average to see deep filter rejectionThe noise floor limits how much isolation or rejection can be measured.
Use port extension or de-embedding for parts on a boardMoves the reference plane from the connector to the component, removing the trace's phase and loss.
Check the calibration by measuring a standard again (the load should read better than about 40 dB return loss)A quick check that catches a bad standard or a disturbed cable before it spoils an afternoon of data.
Application

Measuring an antenna mounted on a vehicle, rather than on the bench, gives the result that matters, because the body around the antenna shifts its resonance. Two details decide whether the measurement is meaningful. The calibration plane should be at the antenna's own connector, at the end of whatever cable reaches it. And the test cable should be routed away from the antenna with ferrite chokes on it, because current on the outside of the cable's shield (Module 3's balun discussion) makes the cable part of the antenna, so the result changes whenever someone moves it.

Design exerciseMeasuring a filter's stopband

A filter is specified to reject a frequency by 80 dB, but the VNA shows S₂₁ flattening out at −65 dB, with a noisy trace.

What's the most likely explanation, and what should be tried?

  1. The filter is out of specification.
    Possibly, but a noisy, flat floor points to the instrument's noise floor first.
  2. The measurement has reached the VNA's noise floor. Reduce the IF bandwidth (each factor of ten lowers the floor by 10 dB) or average, and check that the cables aren't leaking around the filter.
    A noisy, flat trace at a particular level is the signature of the noise floor or of leakage.
  3. The calibration is wrong.
    Calibration errors don't usually produce a flat, noisy floor.
  4. Increase the frequency span.
    The span doesn't change the noise floor; the IF bandwidth does.

A measurement can't show more rejection than the instrument's dynamic range: its source power minus its noise floor. The noise floor depends on the IF bandwidth, so narrowing it from 10 kHz to 100 Hz lowers it by 20 dB at the cost of a much slower sweep. If the floor doesn't move, the limit is leakage: signal getting around the filter through the cables, connectors or the fixture's ground.

Spectrum analyzers

A swept spectrum analyzer is a superheterodyne receiver whose local oscillator sweeps across the band, with a narrow IF filter. At each instant, the only input frequency that lands in the IF filter is the one that's IF away from the LO, so sweeping the LO sweeps a narrow "window" across the spectrum. The power that passes through the window is detected and plotted against frequency.

DiagramA swept spectrum analyzer

The width of the IF filter is the resolution bandwidth (RBW), and it controls three things at once, all from first principles:

  • Resolution: two signals closer together than about one RBW pass through the window together and appear as one.
  • Noise floor: the window collects noise from its whole width, so the displayed noise floor rises 10 dB for every factor of ten wider RBW. A CW signal is entirely inside the window whatever its width, so its displayed level doesn't change.
  • Sweep time: a filter of bandwidth RBW takes about 1/RBW to respond, and the window has to dwell for that long at each RBW-wide step of the span. Sweep time therefore scales as span/RBW²: a ten times narrower RBW takes a hundred times longer.

The input attenuator sets the second big trade-off. The analyzer's first mixer compresses and generates its own distortion if the input is too strong, which can show harmonics or intermodulation that don't exist in the signal. More attenuation protects the mixer but raises the displayed noise floor by the same amount. A quick test: if adding 10 dB of input attenuation changes the level of a harmonic (relative to the carrier), the harmonic was being generated inside the analyzer.

Application

Checking a transmitter's harmonics before an emissions test is a routine spectrum analyzer job, and the first step is protecting the analyzer: a 2 W (+33 dBm) transmitter connected directly can damage the input, whose maximum is typically +30 dBm. A 30 dB or 40 dB high-power attenuator goes between the transmitter and the analyzer, and its value is entered as a reference level offset so the display reads the true power. The second step is the attenuator test above: harmonics that change relative to the carrier when the analyzer's input attenuation is changed are being generated by the analyzer, not the transmitter.

Signal generators

A signal generator produces a known signal: a CW tone at a set frequency and level, or a modulated signal that imitates a real transmitter. Inside, a synthesiser (a phase-locked loop, Module 11) generates the frequency and an output stage with a calibrated attenuator sets the level. Its specifications describe how clean and how accurate that signal is:

SpecificationWhat it means in practice
Level accuracy (typically ±0.5–1 dB)The output power is only known to this accuracy; a power meter is needed for better.
Harmonics (typically −30 dBc)When testing a filter's or receiver's harmonic response, the generator's own harmonics may be larger than what's being measured; add a low-pass filter after it.
Phase noise (dBc/Hz at an offset)Sets how clean the tone is close to the carrier. It limits blocking and reciprocal-mixing tests (Module 6).
Reverse power (often +30 dBm or less)The output can be damaged by a transmitter driving back into it, for example when testing a transceiver.
Minimum level (−110 to −140 dBm)Needed for receiver sensitivity tests, where leakage around the cables can easily exceed the intended signal.

A common use is measuring a receiver's sensitivity: the generator provides a modulated signal at a known, very low level, and the level is reduced until the receiver's error rate reaches its limit. At −110 dBm and below, signal leaking from the generator's case or cables can be larger than the signal in the cable, so these tests use well-shielded cables, extra attenuators placed at the receiver, and sometimes a shielded box.

Power meters and mismatch uncertainty

A power meter measures the total power absorbed by its sensor, with an accuracy of a few percent: better than any analyzer. Thermal sensors measure the heat the RF power produces, so they respond to the true average power of any waveform, slowly. Diode sensors rectify the signal; they're fast and sensitive, and the peak-power versions can follow a pulse or a burst, but outside their square-law region their reading depends on the waveform.

Every RF power measurement has one more uncertainty that's easy to overlook. Neither the source nor the sensor is perfectly matched. The wave reflected from the sensor travels back to the source, reflects again, and adds to the incident wave with a phase that depends on the cable length and frequency, so the power delivered to the sensor differs from what a perfectly matched sensor would receive by a factor of up to

$$\text{mismatch uncertainty} = 20\log_{10}\big(1 \pm |\Gamma_s|\,|\Gamma_l|\big)\ \text{dB}$$
Γs: source reflection coefficient, Γl: sensor (load) reflection coefficient

Because the phase is usually unknown, this is a band of uncertainty, not a correction. It's the reason power sensors are designed with an excellent match, and why a 6 dB attenuator is often fitted at a poorly matched source before measuring it.

Noise figure measurement

A noise figure can't be measured by looking at the noise at an amplifier's output alone, because the output noise depends on both the amplifier's gain and the noise it adds, and the gain isn't known accurately enough. The Y-factor method separates them using a calibrated noise source: a noise diode that can be switched between "off", where it produces the thermal noise of a room-temperature 50 Ω resistor (T₀ = 290 K), and "on", where it produces much more. Its excess noise ratio (ENR) states how much more.

DiagramA Y-factor noise figure measurement

The derivation is short. Describe the amplifier's added noise as an equivalent input noise temperature Te = (F − 1)·T₀. With the source off, the output noise is proportional to G·(T₀ + Te); with it on, to G·(Thot + Te). Their ratio, the Y-factor, doesn't depend on the gain at all:

$$Y = \frac{N_\text{on}}{N_\text{off}} = \frac{T_\text{hot} + T_e}{T_0 + T_e} \quad\Rightarrow\quad F = \frac{\text{ENR}}{Y - 1}$$
ENR = (Thot − T₀)/T₀, as a ratio; F as a ratio, so NF = 10 log₁₀ F

This gives the noise figure of everything after the noise source, including the analyzer's own front end. The analyzer measures its own noise figure during calibration and removes it with the cascade formula (Module 3), which works well only when the device has enough gain to dominate. A low-gain device with a noisy analyzer leaves a small Y-factor, and the result becomes very sensitive to small errors.

Load-pull

Module 3 showed that a power amplifier's output isn't matched for maximum small-signal gain but for the load that lets the transistor use its full voltage and current swing. That optimum load can be estimated from the supply voltage and the transistor's maximum current, but the real device has output capacitance, package parasitics and nonlinear behaviour that move it. Load-pull finds it experimentally: an adjustable impedance tuner after the device presents a sequence of load impedances while the output power, efficiency and linearity are measured at each.

DiagramA load-pull setup

The shape of the contours follows from the same two limits used in Module 3's estimate. When the load impedance is low, the transistor reaches its maximum current before its maximum voltage, and the power is ½Imax²R. When the load is high, the voltage limits first and the power falls as the load rises. The optimum, Ropt ≈ Vmax/Imax, is where both limits are reached together. The transistor's output capacitance sits in parallel with the load, so the optimum load at the package terminals is Ropt with that capacitance tuned out by some inductive reactance.

Application

Few small teams own a load-pull system, but PA manufacturers publish load-pull results in their datasheets as tables or contour plots of the optimum load at several frequencies. The output matching network is then designed to transform 50 Ω to that load (the datasheet's "Zload"), exactly as in Module 2's matching examples, and a VNA confirms the network's impedance before the PA is fitted. Matching a PA's output to the conjugate of its small-signal S₂₂ instead is a common mistake that gives high gain at low power and disappointing output power.

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●○○A spectrum analyzer's noise floor

A spectrum analyzer has a noise figure of 24 dB. Thermal noise is −174 dBm/Hz at room temperature (Module 6).

(a) What's the displayed noise floor with a 10 kHz RBW?

Noise floor = −174 dBm/Hz + NF + 10 log₁₀(RBW).

−174 + 24 + 40 = −110 dBm.

(b) With a 300 Hz RBW?

10 log₁₀(300) = 24.8, so −174 + 24 + 24.8 = −125.2 dBm, 14.8 dB lower. The sweep takes about (10 000/300)² ≈ 1100 times longer.

Discussion: other ways to lower the floor

Many analyzers have a built-in preamplifier, which lowers their noise figure by 10–20 dB at the cost of strong-signal handling, and reducing the input attenuation to its minimum also helps. For signals near the noise floor, using an RMS (average) detector instead of a peak detector gives a stable reading of the noise and the signal.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Measuring an LNA's noise figure

A noise source with an ENR of 15 dB is connected to an LNA with 20 dB of gain. The analyzer measures a Y-factor of 12 dB. The analyzer's own noise figure is 8 dB.

(a) What's the noise figure of the LNA and analyzer together?

Use the ratios: ENR = 31.6 and Y = 15.85, not the decibel values.

F = ENR/(Y − 1), with both as ratios.

F = 31.6/(15.85 − 1) = 2.13, a noise figure of 3.28 dB.

(b) What's the LNA's own noise figure?

Rearrange the cascade formula: FLNA = Ftotal − (Fanalyzer − 1)/G.

FLNA = 2.13 − (6.31 − 1)/100 = 2.077, a noise figure of 3.17 dB. With 20 dB of gain the correction is only 0.11 dB.

Discussion: when the correction gets large

With only 5 dB of LNA gain the correction would be (6.31 − 1)/3.16 = 1.68, larger than the LNA's own contribution, and any error in the analyzer's noise figure or the LNA's gain would dominate the result. For low-gain devices, a low-noise preamplifier in front of the analyzer reduces the second-stage contribution.

Exercise 3Analysis · ●○○How accurate is that power reading?

A transmitter's output has a VSWR of 1.5 and is measured with a power sensor whose VSWR is 1.2.

What's the largest mismatch uncertainty (the larger of the ± values)?

|Γ| = (VSWR − 1)/(VSWR + 1). The uncertainty is 20 log₁₀(1 ± |Γs||Γl|).

|Γs| = 0.2 and |Γl| = 0.091, so |Γs||Γl| = 0.0182. The limits are +0.157 dB and −0.159 dB, about ±0.16 dB.

Discussion: in context

±0.16 dB is comparable to the sensor's own calibration uncertainty, so it matters when results are compared against a limit, such as a regulatory maximum output power. With a 3:1 source VSWR (Γ = 0.5) and the same sensor it would be ±0.4 dB.

Calibration and the instruments' limits

NanoVNA (or any VNA) and its calibration kitAn SMA test cable, 1 m or longerAn antenna and a filter to measureA spectrum analyzer and 30 dB attenuatorA low-power transmitter or signal generator
  1. Calibration. Calibrate at the end of the test cable. Measure the load standard again and record its return loss. Then flex the cable and measure it again.
  2. Reference plane. Measure the antenna with the calibration at the cable's end, then with the calibration at the VNA's port. Compare the apparent resonance and match.
  3. Dynamic range. Measure the filter's stopband with IF bandwidths of 10 kHz and 100 Hz.
  4. Spectrum analyzer. Through the attenuator, measure the transmitter's carrier and harmonics. Change the analyzer's input attenuation by 10 dB and check whether the harmonics move relative to the carrier.
What you should see: the load reading better than about 40 dB after calibration and worse after flexing the cable; the antenna looking better matched without the cable in the calibration; the stopband floor dropping by about 20 dB with the narrower IF bandwidth; and harmonics that stay put relative to the carrier when the attenuation changes.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Choose the instrument by the question: VNA for S-parameters, spectrum analyzer for what a circuit emits, power meter for accurate power, noise figure analyzer for added noise, load-pull for PA loads.
  2. A VNA measures ratios of waves with tuned receivers; IF bandwidth trades noise floor against speed.
  3. Calibration removes the systematic errors of everything up to the reference plane. Anything outside it, or anything that changes after it, is error.
  4. A spectrum analyzer's RBW sets resolution, noise floor (10 log RBW) and sweep time (∝ 1/RBW²). Too strong an input creates false distortion; check with the attenuator.
  5. Every power measurement carries a mismatch uncertainty of 20 log(1 ± |Γs||Γl|).
  6. The Y-factor method measures noise figure independently of gain: F = ENR/(Y − 1), then corrected for the analyzer.
  7. Load-pull finds a PA's optimum load, which is set by its voltage and current limits, not by its small-signal match.
