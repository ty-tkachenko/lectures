RF fundamentals

At radio frequencies a wire stops being "just a wire". Signals travel as waves, reflect off anything that doesn't match, and lose energy into the board itself. These notes build the vocabulary and intuition the rest of the RF material depends on: decibels, transmission lines, reflections, and the materials signals travel through.

Builds on: EE modules 1–4. Bench: NanoVNA or any VNA, a few SMA cables.

Decibels

A telemetry radio transmits about 100 mW. By the time the signal reaches the ground station a few kilometres away, perhaps 0.000 000 000 01 mW arrives. Numbers spanning that many orders of magnitude are awkward to multiply, so RF engineers use a logarithmic scale, the decibel:

$$\text{ratio in dB} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_2}{P_1} \qquad\qquad P\ \text{in dBm} = 10\log_{10}\frac{P}{1\ \text{mW}}$$
dB is a ratio (gain or loss). dBm is an absolute power referenced to 1 mW.

The advantage: multiplying becomes adding. An amplifier with 20 dB of gain followed by a cable with 3 dB of loss gives 17 dB overall. Most of RF system design, from transmit chains to link budgets, is adding and subtracting dB.

You can't add two dBm valuesAdding dB to dBm applies a gain to a power, which is fine. But two signals of 0 dBm (1 mW each) combine to 2 mW, which is +3 dBm, not 0 dBm and not "0 dBm + 0 dBm". To combine powers, convert to milliwatts, add, and convert back.
Mental math

Break any dB value into 10s and 3s. 13 dB = 10 + 3, so ×10 × 2 = ×20. 27 dBm = 30 − 3, so 1 W ÷ 2 = 500 mW. With practice this needs no calculator.

Check your understandingWhere should the amplifier go?

At the ground station, the antenna delivers −95 dBm into a 20 dB low-noise amplifier at the mast, followed by 4 dB of cable down to the receiver: −79 dBm arrives. Someone proposes moving the amplifier indoors, after the cable.

What happens?

  1. The received power drops by 4 dB.
    In decibels the chain is a sum, and addition does not care about order. −95 + 20 − 4 and −95 − 4 + 20 are the same −79 dBm. What moves is where the cable sits relative to the amplifier's own noise.
  2. The received power is unchanged, because gains and losses add in any order, but the noise gets worse, because the cable's loss now comes before the amplifier.
    The signal level is the same; the signal-to-noise ratio isn't. The cable now attenuates the signal before the amplifier adds its noise.
  3. The received power rises, because the amplifier sees a cleaner signal indoors.
    Moving the amplifier does not change the total gain or the total loss. The sum is still −79 dBm. Indoors or outdoors is not what sets the noise here; the order of the loss and the amplifier is.
  4. Nothing changes at all.
    The power at the receiver is the same, but the cable is now in front of the amplifier. The amplifier adds its noise to a signal that is already 4 dB weaker, so the signal-to-noise ratio falls by about that cable loss.

In decibels the chain is just a sum, and −95 + 20 − 4 = −95 − 4 + 20 = −79 dBm either way. What changes is the noise: a loss before the first amplifier weakens the signal before it's amplified, while the noise the amplifier adds stays the same, so the signal-to-noise ratio drops by about the cable's loss. That's why low-noise amplifiers go at the antenna. Module 6 makes this precise with noise figure.

For a voltage ratio, use \(20\log_{10}\) instead of \(10\log_{10}\), because power goes as voltage squared. Doubling a voltage is +6 dB, and that's the same physical change as quadrupling the power.

Transmission lines

Signals don't arrive instantly. They travel at a large fraction of the speed of light, slowed down by the material around the conductor:

$$v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_\text{eff}}} \qquad\qquad \lambda = \frac{v}{f}$$
εeff is the effective dielectric constant: 1 for air, about 2.1 for PTFE coax, about 3.3 for a microstrip trace on FR-4

If a connection is short compared with the wavelength, every point on it sees nearly the same voltage at any instant, and ordinary circuit theory (Kirchhoff's laws, EE Module 2) works. Once the connection is a noticeable fraction of a wavelength, different points on it hold different voltages at the same moment. The far end lags behind the near end, and anything that happens at the far end comes back as a reflection. Then you need transmission-line thinking.

λ
Rule of thumb: the λ/10 ruleIf a connection is longer than about one tenth of a wavelength, treat it as a transmission line: control its impedance and terminate it properly. At 915 MHz on FR-4 that's only about 2 cm.

One wavelength is one full cycle, 360° of phase. A tenth of a wavelength is therefore 36° of lag: the load end is already a tenth of a cycle behind the source end, so the two ends are not at the same voltage at the same instant. That is enough to break the lumped-circuit assumption that Kirchhoff's laws rely on (EE Module 2). λ/10 is a convenient threshold, not a law of physics. Where the signal is precious, designers often use λ/20, which is only 18°.

Phase is just time delay, measured in cycles

The electrical length of a line is its delay expressed as a fraction of one cycle, in degrees:

$$\theta = 360^\circ \cdot \frac{\ell}{\lambda} = 360^\circ \cdot f \cdot t_\text{delay}$$

A line that delays the signal by a quarter of a period has an electrical length of 90°, whatever its physical length. That's why the same 2 cm trace is electrically short for a 10 MHz clock (well under a degree) and about 105° at 2.4 GHz on FR-4, where \(\varepsilon_\text{eff} \approx 3.3\). The same 2 cm in air, with no dielectric slowing the wave, is only about 58°. Matching networks, power splitters and antenna feeds are all built from lines of precise electrical length, and Module 2 relies on this idea throughout. (A related idea, group delay, describes how long the information in a modulated signal takes to get through, and comes back with filters in Module 3.)

It isn't only radios: fast digital edges count tooA digital edge with rise time \(t_r\) contains significant energy up to roughly \(0.35/t_r\). A 1 ns edge on an SPI or Ethernet line behaves like a 350 MHz signal, so a long trace or ribbon cable starts acting like a transmission line, ringing and all. This is where RF and high-speed digital design meet.
Check your understandingA different board material

By the λ/10 rule, a 2.4 GHz trace on FR-4 can be about 7 mm long before it needs impedance control. The same design is moved to a PTFE-based board, with a lower dielectric constant.

Does the longest "lumped" trace get longer or shorter?

  1. Shorter.
    A lower dielectric constant raises the wave speed, v = c/√ε_eff. A faster wave covers more distance in one cycle, so the wavelength, and the λ/10 length, get longer.
  2. Longer, because the wave travels faster in a lower-εr material, so the wavelength on the board is longer.
    λ = c/(f√εeff): lower εeff, longer wavelength.
  3. Unchanged: the frequency is the same.
    Frequency sets how many cycles happen per second. Wavelength is speed divided by frequency, so a faster wave at the same frequency has a longer wavelength.
  4. It depends only on the trace width.
    Width sets the capacitance to the ground plane, which sets Z₀. Wavelength is set by speed and frequency, not by width.

A wave covers one wavelength per cycle. In a lower-εr material it travels faster, so it covers more distance per cycle and the wavelength is longer. On PTFE (εeff around 1.9 for microstrip) the wavelength at 2.4 GHz is about a third longer than on FR-4, so the λ/10 limit moves out to about 9 mm. Still short: at 2.4 GHz, almost any real route needs to be a controlled-impedance line.

Characteristic impedance

Every pair of conductors has inductance along its length and capacitance between them, spread evenly per metre. Picture the wave front advancing at speed \(v\). In one second it sweeps a length \(v\), and it has to charge the capacitance of that length, \(C' v\), up to the wave's voltage \(V\). The current that supplies the charge is \(I = C' v V\). The same line sets the speed, \(v = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}\): \(L'\) is the inertia and \(1/C'\) is the stiffness, just as on a string. Divide voltage by current:

$$\frac{V}{I} = \frac{1}{C' v} = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} = Z_0 \qquad\qquad Z_0^\text{coax} = \frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac{D}{d}$$
L′ and C′ are inductance and capacitance per metre. For coax, D is the dielectric (shield) diameter and d the centre conductor diameter. The logarithm is natural.

That ratio is the characteristic impedance. Two consequences follow at once, and both surprise people who think of Z₀ as a resistance they could measure:

  • Z₀ doesn't depend on length. \(L'\) and \(C'\) are both per metre, so a longer cable has more inductance and more capacitance in the same proportion. The ratio \(\sqrt{L'/C'}\) stays put. A 50 Ω cable is 50 Ω whether it is 1 cm or 100 m. What sets it is the cross-section: conductor sizes, spacing, and the dielectric.
  • An ohmmeter reads open. A multimeter applies a steady voltage and waits. Nothing is changing, so no wave is travelling, the open far end has had time to charge, and no DC current flows. The meter correctly reports an open circuit. Z₀ is the ratio a travelling wave sees, not a DC resistance.

Things to try
  1. On an air line, slide D/d until the loss curve bottoms out (about 3.6, near 77 Ω) and until power handling peaks (about 1.65, which is 30 Ω). 50 Ω sits between them.
  2. Raise εr and watch Z₀ fall as 1/√εr. The best D/d ratios do not move: the dielectric scales every impedance the same way.

Why 50 Ω?

Fix the outer diameter of an air-filled coax and vary only D/d. Conductor loss is lowest when D/d ≈ 3.6: the return path is not too far away, and the centre conductor is not so thin that its resistance dominates. The air-line formula \(Z_0 = 60\ln(D/d)\) then gives about 77 Ω. Power handling is a different optimum. Breakdown happens at the surface of the inner conductor, where the electric field is strongest, and the power that can be sent before that field is reached peaks at D/d = √e ≈ 1.65, which is 30 Ω. Fifty ohms is the compromise between those two that stuck in the 1930s, and today nearly every RF connector, cable, amplifier and instrument is built for it. (Video and cable television standardised near the loss minimum, at 75 Ω.) On a PCB, a 50 Ω microstrip is a trace of a particular width over a ground plane; Modules 2 and 4 show how to find that width.

Check your understandingFilling an air line with PTFE

An air-dielectric coax has 50 Ω characteristic impedance. Without changing the conductors, the air is replaced with solid PTFE (εr = 2.1).

What happens to Z₀?

  1. It rises.
    Filling the line with PTFE raises the capacitance per metre. Z₀ = √(L′/C′), so more capacitance lowers the impedance, it does not raise it.
  2. It falls, to about 35 Ω.
    The dielectric raises the capacitance per metre by 2.1×, and Z₀ falls by √2.1.
  3. It stays at 50 Ω, because the geometry is the same.
    The conductors set L′, which is unchanged. The dielectric multiplies C′. Geometry alone does not fix Z₀ once the material between the conductors changes.
  4. It halves.
    C′ is multiplied by εr = 2.1, but Z₀ depends on √(1/C′), so it falls by √2.1 ≈ 1.45, from 50 Ω to about 35 Ω, not by half.

Z₀ = √(L′/C′). The conductors set L′, which doesn't change. The dielectric multiplies C′ by εr, so Z₀ falls by √2.1 ≈ 1.45, from 50 Ω to about 35 Ω. To get 50 Ω back with PTFE, the conductors have to be further apart relative to their size: D/d grows from 2.3 to about 3.35. That's why PTFE cables are thicker for the same centre conductor than air lines would be. Try it with the calculator above.

Reflections, VSWR and return loss

A wave travelling down a 50 Ω line has \(V/I = 50\ \Omega\). When it reaches a load that isn't 50 Ω, it can't keep that ratio and satisfy the load at the same time. Voltage and current are continuous at the junction, so the forward and reflected waves together have to equal the voltage and the current in the load:

$$V^+ + V^- = V_L \qquad\qquad \frac{V^+ - V^-}{Z_0} = \frac{V_L}{Z_L}$$
The reflected current is V⁻/Z₀, but it flows back toward the source, so it subtracts from the forward current.

Eliminate \(V_L\) and the fraction of the voltage that comes back is the reflection coefficient Γ (gamma). The voltage in the load is \(V_L = V^+(1+\Gamma)\), so the transmission coefficient is \(T = 1+\Gamma\):

$$\Gamma = \frac{V^-}{V^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} \qquad\qquad T = 1 + \Gamma$$

The open and the short are what the current and the voltage at the end require, not extra cases to memorise. An open draws no current, so \(I = 0\), which forces \(V^- = V^+\) and \(\Gamma = +1\). The voltage at the end is \(2V^+\): the reflected wave arrives in step with the forward wave and they add. A short holds the voltage at zero, so \(V^+ + V^- = 0\) and \(\Gamma = -1\). A matched load is the one value, \(Z_L = Z_0\), for which the travelling-wave ratio already satisfies the load, so nothing comes back (\(\Gamma = 0\)).

Power splits cleanly: the fraction \(|\Gamma|^2\) reflects and \(1 - |\Gamma|^2\) is delivered. (T can exceed 1 for a high-impedance load, but that's voltage, not power. No energy is created.) The incident and reflected waves interfere to form a standing wave whose peaks and dips stay fixed in place. On the figure below, the dashed envelope is the largest and smallest total voltage a probe would see as it slid along the line. At a peak the reflected wave adds to the forward wave, so the envelope height is proportional to \(1+|\Gamma|\); at a null it subtracts, so the envelope falls to \(1-|\Gamma|\). Their ratio is the voltage standing-wave ratio. Return loss is the same fact in decibels:

$$\text{VSWR} = \frac{V_\text{max}}{V_\text{min}} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma|}{1 - |\Gamma|} \qquad\qquad \text{Return loss} = -20\log_{10}|\Gamma|\ \text{dB}$$

Check your understandingWhich mismatch is worse?

Two antennas are tested on a 50 Ω radio. One has a 75 Ω feed impedance; the other measures 33 Ω.

Which reflects more power?

  1. The 75 Ω one, because it's 25 Ω away from 50 Ω instead of 17 Ω.
    Γ = (Z_L − Z₀)/(Z_L + Z₀) depends on the ratio Z_L/Z₀, not on the difference in ohms. 75/50 and 50/33 are the same ratio.
  2. The 33 Ω one, because low impedances reflect more.
    A load below Z₀ and a load above Z₀ reflect the same fraction of the power when the ratios match. The sign of Γ flips; |Γ| does not.
  3. About the same: both are about 1.5 times away from 50 Ω, one above and one below.
    Both give |Γ| ≈ 0.2, a VSWR of about 1.5:1.
  4. Neither, as long as the cable is short.
    The reflection is set at the load, where the wave meets an impedance that is not Z₀. A short cable changes the delay, not that mismatch. On a lossless line |Γ| is the same at every distance.

Γ = (ZL − Z₀)/(ZL + Z₀) depends on how far the load is from Z₀ as a ratio. 75/50 = 1.5 and 50/33 ≈ 1.5, so both reflect about 4 % of the power, with opposite signs of Γ. Thinking in ratios is the useful habit: a 100 Ω load and a 25 Ω load are both a 2:1 mismatch.

Time-domain reflectometry (TDR)

Time-domain reflectometry launches a fast voltage step into a line and watches what comes back. Every change in impedance reflects part of the step, and the echo arrives after the round trip to the discontinuity and back. A step up means higher impedance (an open, a narrow trace, extra inductance); a step down means lower impedance (a short, a wide pad, extra capacitance). The trace becomes a map of impedance along the line:

$$x = \frac{v\,t}{2}$$
distance to the discontinuity; the factor of 2 is because the echo makes a round trip

Check your understandingA TDR set for the wrong cable

A TDR shows a fault 1.5 m along a PTFE cable, but its velocity factor was left at 1.0, the value for air.

Where is the fault really?

  1. Further than 1.5 m.
    The instrument measured a round-trip time and multiplied by the speed it was given. PTFE is slower than air, about 0.69c, so in that time the wave covered less distance. The fault is closer than the display says, not further.
  2. Closer than 1.5 m, at about 1.0 m.
    The wave travels at about 0.69c in PTFE, so it covered less distance in the measured time.
  3. At 1.5 m: the TDR measures time directly.
    It measures round-trip time. The distance on the screen is that time times the velocity factor you set. Leave the factor at 1 and the metres are wrong even though the nanoseconds are right.
  4. It can't be located without cutting the cable.
    The echo time is the location. Only the conversion from time to metres used the wrong speed. Set the velocity factor to about 0.69 and the same trace reads the right distance.

The TDR measures the echo's round-trip time and converts it to distance assuming the speed you set. Set for air, it assumes the wave went at c, but in PTFE it went at about 0.69c, so it covered only 69 % of the assumed distance: about 1.0 m. Measuring a cut length of the same cable first, as in the lab, gives you the right velocity factor.

Reflections in the frequency domain

Suppose a cable has two small mismatches: one at the near-end connector and one at the far end. On a TDR you'd see two echoes, separated by the round-trip delay \(2\tau\). On a VNA, which sweeps frequency, the two echoes add with a phase that changes with frequency. Sometimes they reinforce and sometimes they cancel, so the return loss shows a ripple:

$$\Delta f_\text{ripple} = \frac{1}{2\tau}$$
ripple period in frequency; τ is the one-way delay between the two discontinuities

The two views are the same information, related by the Fourier transform. Closely spaced bumps in a return-loss plot mean the discontinuities are far apart; widely spaced bumps mean they're close together. When you see a periodic ripple on a VNA, measure the spacing and you know how far apart the problems are.

Check your understandingShortening the cable between two mismatches

A VNA shows a return-loss ripple caused by mismatches at each end of a cable. The cable is cut to half its length and re-terminated with the same connectors.

What happens to the ripple period in frequency?

  1. It halves.
    Halving the cable halves the one-way delay τ between the echoes. The ripple period is 1/(2τ). A smaller delay makes that period larger, not smaller.
  2. It doubles.
    The echoes are closer together in time, so their phase difference changes more slowly with frequency. Period 1/(2τ) doubles when τ halves.
  3. It stays the same, because the connectors are the same.
    The two mismatches set how big the ripple is, because they set the two echo heights. The period is set by the delay between them, which is the cable length.
  4. The ripple disappears.
    Both mismatches are still there, so both echoes are still there. Only the time between them changed, which changes the spacing of the ripple, not whether it exists.

The two echoes are separated by the round-trip delay 2τ. As the frequency rises, their relative phase turns through one full cycle every 1/(2τ) hertz. Halve the cable and τ halves, so the ripple period doubles. The rule to remember: closely spaced ripple means distant mismatches, widely spaced ripple means close ones.

Dielectric constant and loss (Dk and Df)

A PCB trace carries its signal mostly in the electric field between the copper and the ground plane, so the dielectric in between matters as much as the copper. Two numbers describe it:

  • Dk (dielectric constant, εr) sets how much the material slows the wave, and so the wavelength on the board and the trace width needed for 50 Ω.
  • Df (dissipation factor, loss tangent, tan δ) is the energy lost per radian divided by the energy stored. The fraction of the stored energy turned into heat over a whole cycle is \(2\pi\tan\delta\), and \(\tan\delta = 1/Q_d\).

Loss comes from two places. Conductor loss grows with \(\sqrt{f}\). A changing current produces a changing magnetic field inside the copper, and that changing field induces an electric field which opposes the current that created it. The opposition is strongest in the interior, surrounded by changing current, so the net current crowds into a thin skin at the surface. The skin gets thinner as \(1/\sqrt{f}\), and the resistance rises with it. Dielectric loss grows in proportion to \(f \cdot \text{Df}\): each cycle throws away a fraction \(2\pi\tan\delta\) of the stored field energy, and there are \(f\) cycles per second. Above a gigahertz or so, dielectric loss usually wins on FR-4, which is why RF boards use lower-loss laminates. Module 0 derives the skin depth and why the return current hugs the trace.

Polarization

A radio wave's electric field oscillates in a particular direction, its polarization. A straight dipole or monopole radiates linear polarization along its own axis, and it only picks up the component of an incoming field that lies along that axis. Two linear antennas at angle θ to each other lose

$$L_\text{pol} = -20\log_{10}|\cos\theta|\ \text{dB}$$

That's nothing at 0°, 3 dB at 45°, and in theory infinite at 90° (in practice 20–30 dB, thanks to reflections and imperfect antennas). Circular polarization rotates as it travels; a circular antenna receives a linear signal with a constant 3 dB loss whatever the angle.

Check your understandingLinear or circular at the ground station?

The rocket carries a linearly polarized antenna, and it rolls and pitches through every angle during the flight. The ground station can use a linear antenna (no loss when aligned, a deep null at 90°) or a circular one (a constant 3 dB loss at any angle).

Which gives the more reliable link?

  1. Linear, because it has no loss when aligned.
    Alignment is the best case. When the two linear polarizations are at 90°, the received component is cos 90° = 0, a null of 20 dB or more in practice. A link has to survive that moment.
  2. Circular, because a constant 3 dB loss is easier to budget for than a fade that can exceed 20 dB.
    A circular antenna takes half the power of a linear wave at every angle, a fixed 3 dB, instead of swinging from 0 dB to a deep null.
  3. They're equivalent on average.
    The average hides the outages. Crossed linear antennas drop the link at the worst angle even when the mean over a flight looks acceptable.
  4. Neither works once the rocket changes attitude.
    A circular ground antenna still receives the linear signal. The cost is a constant 3 dB, not a null, at every angle between the polarizations.

A link budget is set by the worst case. With linear antennas at both ends, the loss swings from 0 dB to 20–30 dB as the rocket turns, and the link drops out each time it passes 90°. A circular ground antenna gives a steady 3 dB whatever the angle. That's a known cost you can budget for, which is why circular polarization at the ground station is so common.

Further checks

The questions above ask what changes in one case. These ask for the mechanism, for the flaw in a plausible claim, or for the assumption that stopped being true. They stay closed until you want them.

Explain it from the mechanism

Check your understandingWhy the voltage doubles at an open end

An open circuit at the end of a 50 Ω line reflects the whole wave and the voltage there is twice the forward voltage. Why?

  1. The open end stores the energy and then releases it as a second wave.
    Nothing at an ideal open stores energy and hands it back later. The doubling is the two travelling waves occupying the same place at the same time.
  2. An open draws no current, so the reflected current must cancel the forward current. Cancelling the current requires the reflected voltage to equal the forward voltage, and the two voltages add.
    I = 0 forces V⁻ = V⁺, so Γ = +1 and V_L = 2V⁺.
  3. The line's Z₀ doubles when the load is removed.
    Z₀ is set by the cross-section. Removing the load does not change L′ or C′.
  4. Charge piles up and the voltage rises without limit.
    The reflected wave carries the charge back toward the source. At the end the voltage is 2V⁺, not a ramp.

Continuity requires I = 0 in the open. The forward current is V⁺/Z₀ and the reflected current subtracts, so (V⁺ − V⁻)/Z₀ = 0 and V⁻ = V⁺. The voltages are in the same direction, so they add to 2V⁺. That is Γ = +1, from I = 0, not from a special rule.

Check your understandingWhy a dielectric slows the wave

The same trace is electrically longer on FR-4 than in air, at the same frequency. What actually slowed the wave?

  1. The dielectric adds series resistance, so the wave loses speed the way current loses speed in a thin wire.
    A series resistance would turn energy into heat. It is not what sets the speed. The speed is 1/√(L′C′), and the dielectric changes C′.
  2. The dielectric polarises, which raises the capacitance per metre. More capacitance means more charge to supply as the wave front advances, so the front moves more slowly.
    v = 1/√(L′C′). εr multiplies C′ and the speed falls by √εr.
  3. The copper is thinner on a PCB than in a cable, so the electrons have further to go.
    Conductor thickness sets the skin-effect loss, not the wave speed. Air-filled coax with thick copper is still at c.
  4. The frequency drops, because the board absorbs cycles.
    Frequency is set by the source. The board changes the wavelength, λ = v/f, by changing v.

A dielectric's molecules shift slightly in the electric field. That is extra charge separation, so C′ rises by about εr. The wave front has to supply I = C′vV, and the line's own L′ and C′ only agree at v = 1/√(L′C′). Higher C′, lower v. On FR-4 microstrip ε_eff ≈ 3.3, so the wave runs at about 0.55c.

Check your understandingWhy an ohmmeter reads open on 50 Ω coax

A multimeter across an unterminated 50 Ω cable reads an open circuit. Which explanation is right?

  1. The cable is faulty: 50 Ω should read 50 Ω at DC.
    Z₀ is not a resistor between the two conductors. It is the ratio V/I of a travelling wave. At DC there is no travelling wave.
  2. The shield is shorting the centre, so the meter is confused.
    A short would read about 0 Ω. The meter reads open, which is the opposite fault, and here it is not a fault.
  3. The meter applies a steady voltage. The open far end charges up and then no current flows, so the meter is right to say open. 50 Ω was never a DC resistance.
    Z₀ = √(L′/C′) is the ratio a wave sees while it is still charging the capacitance ahead of it.
  4. The meter is too slow: wait longer and it will settle at 50 Ω.
    Waiting longer only finishes the charging. After that the current is zero, not V/50.

While a wave front is moving, current flows to charge C′. Once the front has reflected from the open end and the whole cable sits at one steady voltage, that current stops. An ohmmeter only looks at the steady state. The 50 Ω appears when something launches a wave and you look at V/I before the reflection matters, which is what a VNA or a TDR does.

Check your understandingWhy conductor loss rises with frequency

The same copper trace loses more signal at 2.4 GHz than at 100 MHz, even though the copper has not changed. Why does the loss rise?

  1. The electrons have further to travel at a higher frequency.
    The path length is the trace. What changes is how much of the copper's cross-section carries the current.
  2. The dielectric gets lossier, and conductor loss is really dielectric loss.
    Dielectric loss does rise with frequency, in proportion to f·Df. Conductor loss is a separate term, and it rises because of the skin effect, roughly as √f.
  3. A faster-changing current makes a faster-changing magnetic field inside the copper. The induced electric field opposes current in the interior, so the current is squeezed into a thinner skin and the resistance rises.
    Skin depth shrinks as 1/√f. Less copper is in use, so the resistance, and the loss, rise as √f.
  4. The connector reflections get worse, and that is counted as conductor loss.
    A reflection sends power back; it does not heat the trace in proportion to √f. Conductor loss is the I²R of the current that does flow, in the skin that is left.

Faraday's law: a changing B induces an E. Lenz's law: that E opposes the change. Inside the conductor the induced field cancels current; at the surface there is less conductor outside it to produce the opposing field, so the current survives there. The layer gets thinner as frequency rises, about 6.5 µm in copper at 100 MHz and 1.3 µm at 2.4 GHz, and the loss follows √f. Module 0 draws the skin.

Find the flaw

Check your understanding"VSWR 1.0, so the antenna radiates everything"

At the radio, a VNA reads VSWR 1.0 on the cable that goes to the antenna. A teammate says the antenna is therefore radiating all of the radio's power.

What is wrong with that claim?

  1. Nothing. VSWR 1.0 means Γ = 0, so no power comes back, and the antenna must have it.
    Γ = 0 only says the radio sees Z₀. A long lossy cable, or a 50 Ω dummy load in place of the antenna, also looks like that. The power can be heat.
  2. VSWR 1.0 means everything is reflected, so the antenna gets nothing.
    That is VSWR infinite, |Γ| = 1. VSWR 1.0 is the matched case, |Γ| = 0.
  3. A match only says nothing comes back to the radio. The missing power may have heated a lossy cable, or been absorbed by a matched load that is not an antenna.
    |Γ| = 0 at the radio does not locate where the power was absorbed.
  4. VSWR ignores the cable, so the reading is the antenna's VSWR whatever the cable does.
    On a lossy cable the round trip attenuates the echo. The VSWR at the radio can look better than the VSWR at the antenna.

VSWR 1.0 means the impedance at the place you measured is Z₀, so the reflected wave there is zero. That happens for a good antenna, and also for a 50 Ω terminator, and also for a long enough lossy cable with almost anything on the end, because the echo dies on the way back. To know the antenna radiates, you need the match measured at the antenna, and a reason to believe the power was not lost in the feed.

Check your understanding"T = 1.33, so the load gets more power"

A 100 Ω load on a 50 Ω line has T = 1 + Γ = 1.33. Someone concludes the load receives more power than the line was carrying.

Where does that go wrong?

  1. T cannot exceed 1, so the calculation of Γ is wrong.
    For Z_L = 100 Ω, Γ = (100−50)/(100+50) = 1/3, and T = 4/3. The voltage ratio really is bigger than 1.
  2. T is a voltage ratio. Power goes as voltage times current, and the current into a higher impedance is smaller. The power delivered is 1 − |Γ|², about 89 %, not 133 %.
    No passive load can receive more power than the incident wave carries.
  3. The extra voltage comes from the generator raising its output.
    The generator launched one forward wave. The larger voltage at the load is that wave plus the reflection, standing in the same place.
  4. 133 % is right, because the reflected wave adds energy.
    The reflected wave carries energy back toward the source. It does not add a second supply of power at the load. |Γ|² of the incident power goes back.

V_L = T V⁺ with T = 1.33, so the voltage is higher than the forward voltage. The current is V_L/100 Ω, and the power into the load is |I|²·100 Ω, which equals (1 − |Γ|²) of the incident power: 1 − (1/3)² ≈ 0.89. Voltage added; power did not. The rest is in the reflected wave.

Check your understanding"Doubling the voltage is +3 dB"

A note says the antenna voltage doubled, "so that is +3 dB". What is wrong?

  1. Doubling anything is +6 dB, voltage or power.
    +3 dB is a doubling of power, because dB of power uses 10 log₁₀. Voltage uses 20 log₁₀, since power goes as voltage squared.
  2. Decibels only apply to power, so a voltage ratio has no dB value.
    A voltage ratio is 20 log₁₀(V₂/V₁). It is the same physical dB as the power ratio it corresponds to.
  3. Doubling the voltage quadruples the power if the impedance is unchanged, and 10 log₁₀(4) = 6 dB. The 3 dB figure is the power rule, used on a voltage.
    20 log₁₀(2) = 6.02 dB. The same change is +6 dB either way you look at it.
  4. It is +3 dB of voltage and +6 dB of power, and both wordings are used.
    The decibel is defined so that one physical change has one number. A doubled voltage across the same impedance is +6 dB, not +3 dB of one kind and +6 dB of another.

Power ratios use 10 log₁₀ and voltage ratios use 20 log₁₀, because P ∝ V² and log(V²) = 2 log V. Doubling power is about +3 dB. Doubling voltage is about +6 dB, and it is the same fact as quadrupling the power. The note used the power rule on a voltage.

Check your understanding"18 mm is short, so the GPS trace is lumped"

The GPS antenna trace is 18 mm on FR-4 (ε_eff ≈ 3.3) at 1575 MHz. Someone calls that short compared with a hand, so ordinary circuit theory is fine and the trace need not be 50 Ω.

What was missed?

  1. 18 mm is more than a wavelength, so it is an antenna.
    The wavelength on the board is about 105 mm. 18 mm is a fraction of that, not several wavelengths. The fraction is what matters, and it is already too big.
  2. "Short" means short compared with the wavelength, not compared with a hand. 18 mm is about 62° at L1, and the lumped rule of thumb is λ/10, which is 36°. The ends of the trace are not at the same voltage.
    λ = c/(f√ε_eff) ≈ 105 mm, and 360° × 18/105 ≈ 62°.
  3. GPS is a digital signal, so transmission-line rules do not apply.
    The antenna input is a 1575 MHz sine wave. The lumped-or-distributed test is the electrical length at that frequency.
  4. FR-4 is slower, so the electrical length is shorter than in air and 18 mm is safe.
    Slower means a shorter wavelength, so the same millimetres are a larger fraction of a cycle. The dielectric makes the trace electrically longer, not shorter.

λ on the board is c/(1.57542 GHz × √3.3) ≈ 105 mm. The trace is 18/105 of a cycle, about 62°, against a 36° (λ/10) threshold. It has to be a controlled-impedance line. "Short" was judged by eye instead of by λ.

What assumption broke?

Check your understandingA TDR whose source is not 50 Ω

The TDR figures in this module assume the instrument launches its step from a 50 Ω source into a 50 Ω cable. Suppose the source is 25 Ω instead, and the far end is open.

What new feature appears on the trace?

  1. The open disappears, because a 25 Ω source cannot launch a step onto 50 Ω cable.
    A step still launches. The launch is a voltage divider between 25 Ω and 50 Ω, so the step height changes. The open is still there.
  2. The echo arrives sooner, because a lower source impedance means a faster wave.
    The speed is 1/√(L′C′) of the cable, not a property of the source. The echo time does not move.
  3. The echo that returns does not get absorbed at the instrument. It reflects again, so later ghosts appear at multiples of the round-trip time.
    A matched source is what swallows the returning wave. Γ at a 25 Ω source is not zero.
  4. The trace shows the open as a downward step, because the source is below 50 Ω.
    The sign of the echo is set by the far-end load. An open is still Γ = +1. The source impedance sets what happens to that echo when it gets home.

The usual reading assumes the near end is matched, so each discontinuity sends one echo and the echo stops there. With a 25 Ω source, Γ at the source is (25−50)/(25+50) = −1/3. The open's echo reflects again and makes another round trip, and you see a train of ghosts. A real TDR is built to be matched for that reason. The simulator on this page launches from 50 Ω; it will not show those ghosts.

Check your understandingDoes the cable length matter?

Two antennas are on 50 Ω cable. One is 50 Ω, the other is 100 Ω. Someone says a longer cable changes what the radio sees in both cases, so you should keep both jumpers short.

For which load does the length actually change the impedance at the radio?

  1. Both. Any extra cable adds impedance in proportion to its length.
    That would be true of the DC resistance of the conductors, which is tiny. Z₀ does not add with length. A matched line looks like Z₀ at every length.
  2. Only the 50 Ω antenna, because that is the one the cable was built for.
    The matched case is the one where length drops out. Z_in = Z₀ for any length when Z_L = Z₀.
  3. Neither, on a lossless cable, because |Γ| is the same everywhere.
    |Γ| is the same everywhere on a lossless line, so the VSWR does not depend on length. The impedance does: a 100 Ω load seen through a different length is a different R + jX, even though |Γ| is unchanged.
  4. Only the 100 Ω antenna. The 50 Ω load already matches the cable, so the input stays 50 Ω at any length. The 100 Ω load is transformed, and the impedance at the radio repeats only every half wavelength.
    The assumption that broke is "cable length always changes the match". It changes the match only when there is a reflection to transform.

On a lossless line, Z_in = Z₀ when Z_L = Z₀, however long the line is. When Z_L = 100 Ω, Γ is not zero, and moving along the line rotates Γ by −2βℓ. The radio sees 100 Ω only at multiples of λ/2, and something else in between, including a pure 25 Ω at λ/4. Keeping the matched jumper short does not fix a match that was already there. Keeping the mismatched one short avoids a transformation you did not design. Module 2 draws that rotation.

Check your understandingAn open cable at 10 MHz

A 1 m length of 50 Ω PTFE coax (velocity factor about 0.69) is left open at the far end and measured at 10 MHz.

What does the near end look like?

  1. 50 Ω, because that is the cable's impedance at every frequency.
    Z₀ is the ratio of a travelling wave when the far end has not had time to answer. At 10 MHz this cable is only about 17° long, so the open end does answer within the cycle.
  2. An open circuit, exactly, because the far end is open.
    A short open stub does not look like a perfect open. It has capacitance: the centre conductor and the shield, over 1 m, are about 100 pF.
  3. A capacitor of about 100 pF. The line is electrically short, the far end is open, and an open line much shorter than λ/4 is the capacitance C′ℓ.
    βℓ ≈ 17°. Z_in = −j Z₀ cot(βℓ) ≈ −j160 Ω, which is about 100 pF at 10 MHz.
  4. A short circuit, because a short cable has negligible impedance.
    A short at the far end would look inductive when the line is short. An open looks capacitive. "Negligible" would be 0 Ω, which is neither.

The assumption that broke is "a 50 Ω cable looks like 50 Ω". That is true for a matched load, or for a wave you observe before the reflection returns. Here the load is open and the round trip is a small fraction of the 100 ns cycle, so the input is the open-stub formula. For a short line that is Z_in ≈ 1/(jω C′ℓ) with C′ ≈ 97 pF/m, about 100 pF. At 10 MHz that is roughly −j160 Ω, not 50 Ω.

Check your understandingReturn loss through a lossy cable

An antenna with 10 dB return loss is measured through 3 m of coax that loses 1 dB one way at this frequency. The VNA is calibrated at the instrument end of the cable, not at the antenna.

What return loss does the VNA report, and why is it flattering?

  1. 10 dB. Return loss is a property of the antenna, and the cable is just a wire.
    The echo has to travel to the antenna and back. Each way costs 1 dB, so the echo that arrives is weaker than the one the antenna made.
  2. 9 dB, worse by the one-way loss, because the cable ate part of the transmitted power.
    The one-way loss does reduce the power that reaches the antenna. The reflection measurement compares the echo with the outgoing wave at the VNA, and the echo has been attenuated twice.
  3. 11 dB, better by the one-way loss.
    The outgoing wave is attenuated once on the way to the antenna, and the echo is attenuated again on the way back. The round trip is twice the one-way loss.
  4. About 12 dB. The displayed return loss is the antenna's 10 dB plus twice the cable loss, because the echo is attenuated on the way out and on the way back.
    A lossy cable hides mismatch. The match looks better by 2 × 1 dB.

The assumption that broke is "the number on the VNA is the antenna". Calibrated at the near end, the instrument sees |Γ_meas| = |Γ_load| × (the round-trip voltage gain of the cable). In decibels that adds twice the one-way loss to the return loss: 10 + 2×1 = 12 dB. Bigger return loss means a smaller echo, so the antenna looks better than it is. Calibrating at the antenna end, or at least accounting for 2αℓ, is what removes the flattery. Module 5 is where calibration is done properly.

Exercise 1Budget · ●●○How much power actually leaves the rocket?

The telemetry radio sits on the avionics sled and feeds an antenna in the nosecone through a short coax jumper.

Given Radio output+27 dBm CoaxRG-316, 1.2 m, 0.75 dB/m at 915 MHz Connectors2 × SMA, 0.1 dB each Antenna2 dBi gain, return loss 10 dB as installed

(a) Mismatch loss at the antenna, the power lost to reflection, \(-10\log_{10}(1-|\Gamma|^2)\)?

Return loss 10 dB means \(|\Gamma| = 10^{-10/20} \approx 0.316\), so \(|\Gamma|^2 = 0.1\).

\(|\Gamma|^2 = 0.1\), so the antenna accepts 90 % of the power: \(-10\log_{10}(0.9) = 0.46\ \text{dB}\).

(b) EIRP (effective radiated power, including antenna gain)?

You've got the losses right. Now add the antenna's +2 dBi of gain.

27 − 0.9 (cable) − 0.2 (connectors) − 0.46 (mismatch) + 2 (antenna) = 27.44 dBm.

(c) …and in milliwatts?

\(10^{27.44/10} \approx 555\ \text{mW}\). Without the antenna gain and losses it would be the radio's 500 mW. The 2 dBi of gain more than makes up for 1.56 dB of losses.

Discussion: where would you look to improve it?

The cable is the biggest loss. Moving the radio closer to the antenna, or using a lower-loss cable, buys back most of a dB. Re-tuning the antenna in the real nosecone to 15 dB return loss cuts the mismatch loss from 0.46 to 0.14 dB. Neither is large on its own, but each dB is about 12 % more range in free space, and the margins add up across the whole link budget (Module 6).

Exercise 2Fault-finding · ●●○Finding the damage in the RF harness

After a hard landing, the telemetry link is weak. A TDR on the coax from the radio to the antenna shows a dip to about 35 Ω, 4.2 ns after the launch edge, followed by an otherwise normal trace.

Given CablePTFE coax, velocity factor 0.69 Echo time4.2 ns (round trip) Dip35 Ω on a 50 Ω line

(a) How far from the radio end is the fault?

That's the round-trip distance. The echo travelled there and back, so halve it.

\(x = v t / 2 = 0.69 \times 3\times10^8 \times 4.2\times10^{-9} / 2 \approx 0.435\ \text{m}\).

(b) Reflection coefficient Γ of the 35 Ω section?

Check the sign: 35 Ω is below 50 Ω, so Γ is negative and the TDR steps down.

\(\Gamma = (35 - 50)/(35 + 50) = -0.176\).

Discussion: what physically causes a low-impedance dip?

Lower impedance means more capacitance per unit length: the centre conductor has been pushed closer to the shield. That's typical of a cable crushed or kinked too tightly, here probably where it passes the bulkhead 43 cm from the radio. A high-impedance bump would instead suggest a cracked solder joint or partly pulled-out connector pin (extra series inductance).

Exercise 3Layout judgement · ●○○Does the GPS trace need to be a controlled-impedance line?

The GPS module's antenna input connects to a u.FL connector through an 18 mm trace on the flight computer's FR-4 board.

Given GPS L1 frequency1575.42 MHz Trace length18 mm Effective dielectric constant3.3 (FR-4 microstrip)

(a) Wavelength on the board?

That's the free-space wavelength. On the board it's shorter by \(\sqrt{\varepsilon_\text{eff}}\).

\(\lambda = c / (f\sqrt{\varepsilon_\text{eff}}) = 3\times10^8 / (1.575\times10^9 \times 1.817) \approx 104.8\ \text{mm}\).

(b) Electrical length of the trace?

\(\theta = 360° \times 18/104.8 \approx 62°\), well beyond the λ/10 (36°) threshold. Yes: it must be a 50 Ω line, with a solid ground plane underneath.

Discussion: what goes wrong if it isn't?

An uncontrolled trace of that length, say a thin 0.15 mm signal trace at ~85 Ω, transforms the 50 Ω input of the GPS into something else. The resulting mismatch costs signal, and GPS signals arrive about 20 dB below the noise floor, so there's no margin to spare. Better still, put the connector right next to the module.

  2. Look at the three standards as return loss and as a Smith chart. Open and short reflect everything (0 dB return loss); the load reflects almost nothing.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Decibels turn multiplication into addition. dB is a ratio; dBm is an absolute power. Combine powers in milliwatts, not dBm.
  2. Once a connection is longer than about λ/10, it's a transmission line. Electrical length is just delay measured in cycles.
  3. Z₀ is set by a line's cross-section, not its length, and you can't measure it with an ohmmeter.
  4. A mismatch reflects \(|\Gamma|^2\) of the power. VSWR, return loss and Γ all describe the same thing.
  5. TDR shows reflections in time; a VNA shows them as ripple in frequency. They're two views of the same physics.
  6. Dielectric loss grows with f·Df and dominates on FR-4 at GHz frequencies. Polarization mismatch can cost more than any cable.
