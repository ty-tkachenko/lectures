System-level performance

A radio link works if the wanted signal arrives stronger than everything competing with it: thermal noise, the receiver's own noise, distortion products, and other signals. This module builds the numbers that decide that, from first principles: how much noise there is, how much signal survives the path, how much distortion the electronics add, and how strong interference can get before the link fails.

Builds on: RF modules 1–5, especially the cascades section of Module 3. Bench: a signal generator, a spectrum analyzer, a combiner and an amplifier for a two-tone test.

Thermal noise and sensitivity

Every resistor at a temperature above absolute zero generates a random voltage, because its electrons are in constant thermal motion. The mean-square noise voltage across a resistor R, measured in a bandwidth B, is v² = 4kTRB (k is Boltzmann's constant, 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K). Connect that resistor to a matched load of the same value, and half the voltage appears across the load, so the noise power delivered is

$$P_n = \frac{(v/2)^2}{R} = \frac{4kTRB}{4R} = kTB$$
independent of the resistance; at T₀ = 290 K, kT₀ = 4.0 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz = −174 dBm/Hz

The resistance cancels: any matched source at room temperature, including a 50 Ω antenna looking at warm surroundings, delivers −174 dBm in every hertz of bandwidth. That's the floor every receiver starts from. The receiver then adds its own noise, by its noise figure, and the detector needs the signal to be some amount above the total, the required signal-to-noise ratio, which depends on the modulation and the acceptable error rate (Module 10). Together these give the receiver's sensitivity, the weakest signal it can use:

$$P_\text{sens} = -174\ \text{dBm/Hz} + 10\log_{10}B + \text{NF} + \text{SNR}_\text{min}$$

Each term is a design lever. Halving the bandwidth gains 3 dB, which is why slow, narrowband links reach so far. A better noise figure gains decibel for decibel. And spread-spectrum modulations such as LoRa can work with a negative SNR, because the receiver correlates the signal over many chips and recovers it from below the noise.

The noise that reaches the antenna isn't always at 290 K. An antenna pointing at the sky sees mostly cold space, a few tens of kelvin at gigahertz frequencies, while one pointing at the warm ground sees close to 290 K, and man-made noise at lower frequencies can be far higher. The antenna's noise temperature replaces T₀ in the first term, and it's why satellite ground receivers, which look at cold sky, benefit from noise figures well below 1 dB while a terrestrial link barely notices the difference between 1 and 2 dB.

Design exerciseFaster data, shorter range

A telemetry link is reconfigured from 10 kbit/s to 100 kbit/s, with the receiver bandwidth widened to match. Nothing else changes.

What happens to the sensitivity, and to the range in free space?

  1. Nothing: the transmit power hasn't changed.
    The receiver now collects noise over ten times the bandwidth.
  2. The noise in the receiver's bandwidth rises by 10 dB, so the sensitivity worsens by 10 dB. In free space, received power falls 6 dB per doubling of distance, so the range falls by a factor of about 3.2.
    10 log 10 = 10 dB of extra noise; free-space loss changes by 20 dB per decade of distance, so 10 dB is a factor of √10 in range.
  3. The sensitivity worsens by 20 dB, and the range halves.
    Bandwidth enters as 10 log B, not 20 log B.
  4. The sensitivity improves, because the data gets through faster.
    Faster data needs more bandwidth, which admits more noise.

Ten times the bandwidth admits ten times the noise power: 10 dB. The required SNR is about the same for the same modulation, so the sensitivity is 10 dB worse. Because free-space path loss grows as 20 log₁₀(d), losing 10 dB cuts the range by 10^(10/20) ≈ 3.2. Data rate, bandwidth and range trade directly against one another, which is why long-range links run slowly.

Noise temperature and the Friis cascade

Module 3 derived the cascade formula in noise factors. The same result is often written in noise temperatures, which are easier to add. A stage's equivalent noise temperature Te is the temperature a matched resistor at its input would need to produce the noise the stage adds: Te = (F − 1)·T₀. A 1 dB noise figure is 75 K; a 3 dB noise figure is 290 K. In those terms the cascade is

$$T_\text{sys} = T_\text{ant} + T_1 + \frac{T_2}{G_1} + \frac{T_3}{G_1G_2} + \cdots$$

which reads naturally: each stage's noise, referred to the antenna, divided by all the gain in front of it. A passive loss L at physical temperature T₀ has a noise temperature of (L − 1)·T₀, so a 3 dB cable in front of the receiver adds 290 K by itself, and then divides everything after it by 2.

DiagramThe LNA at the receiver, or at the antenna

The numbers come straight from the cascade. With the cable first, F = 3.16 (the cable) + (1.26 − 1)·3.16 + (3.98 − 1)·3.16/100 ≈ 4.13, a noise figure of 6.2 dB. With the LNA first, F = 1.26 + (3.16 − 1)/100 + (3.98 − 1)/(100/3.16) ≈ 1.37, or 1.4 dB. Nearly 5 dB of sensitivity, equivalent to 75 % more range, comes purely from where the LNA is placed.

Application

This is why GPS antennas on vehicles are almost always active antennas, with an LNA built into the antenna housing and powered through the coax by a bias tee (Module 3). The receiver can then be a metre or more away through thin, lossy cable without losing sensitivity. The same reasoning puts LNAs at the top of ground-station masts, right at the antenna feed.

Free-space path loss and link budgets

A transmitter radiating a power Pt equally in all directions spreads it over a sphere, so at a distance d the power density is Pt/(4πd²). A transmit antenna with gain Gt concentrates it in the receiver's direction by that factor. The receive antenna collects the power falling on its effective aperture Ae, and a fundamental antenna result (Module 9) relates aperture to gain: Ae = Grλ²/(4π). Multiplying these together gives the Friis transmission equation:

$$P_r = P_t\,G_t\,G_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2 \qquad \text{FSPL} = 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda} = 20\log_{10}d_\text{km} + 20\log_{10}f_\text{MHz} + 32.44\ \text{dB}$$

Two consequences are worth remembering. Path loss grows by 6 dB every time the distance doubles, so each extra 6 dB in the link doubles the free-space range. And path loss grows with frequency, not because higher frequencies are absorbed by free space, but because an antenna of a given gain has a smaller aperture at a shorter wavelength.

A link budget adds up every gain and loss between the transmitter and the receiver and compares the result with the sensitivity. The difference is the link margin, which has to cover everything the free-space model ignores: fading from reflections off the ground, antenna pattern nulls as the vehicle turns, polarization mismatch (Module 1), and manufacturing spread. Margins of 10 to 20 dB are typical for a link that must not fail.

Design exerciseDoubling the range

A link works reliably to 20 km with its required margin, and the next mission needs 40 km.

Which change achieves that on its own?

  1. Doubling the transmit power.
    Doubling the power is 3 dB; doubling the distance costs 6 dB.
  2. Replacing the ground antenna with one that has 6 dB more gain, such as a small directional antenna that tracks the vehicle.
    Free-space path loss rises 6 dB per doubling of distance, so the link needs 6 dB more from somewhere.
  3. Halving the receiver's noise figure from 6 dB to 3 dB.
    That's 3 dB of sensitivity, half of what's needed.
  4. Moving from 915 MHz to 2.4 GHz.
    With the same antenna gains, path loss rises by about 8 dB, making it worse.

Doubling the distance adds 20 log₁₀ 2 = 6 dB of path loss. That 6 dB can come from four times the transmit power, from antenna gain at either end, from 6 dB better sensitivity (a quarter of the bandwidth, for example), or from a combination. Antenna gain at the ground station is usually the cheapest, because the ground station can be large, directional and pointed, while the vehicle's antenna has to work in every direction.

Compression, harmonics and intermodulation

A real amplifier's output isn't exactly proportional to its input. Any smooth nonlinearity can be approximated near its operating point by a power series, and three terms explain almost everything:

$$y = a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3$$
a₁ is the gain; a₂ and a₃ describe the curvature, with a₃ negative for an amplifier that compresses

Feed in a single tone, x = A cos ωt, and expand with the identities cos² = ½(1 + cos 2ωt) and cos³ = ¾cos ωt + ¼cos 3ωt:

  • The fundamental's amplitude becomes a₁A + ¾a₃A³. With a₃ negative, the gain falls as A grows: this is compression, and P1dB is where it reaches 1 dB (Module 3).
  • A second harmonic at 2ω appears with amplitude ½a₂A², and a third harmonic at 3ω with ¼a₃A³. In decibels, the 2nd harmonic rises 2 dB for every 1 dB of input, the 3rd harmonic 3 dB per dB.

Feed in two tones, at ω₁ and ω₂, and the x³ term produces products at every combination of three frequencies. Most fall far away and are easily filtered, but two land right next to the wanted signals, at 2ω₁ − ω₂ and 2ω₂ − ω₁:

DiagramWhere the products of a two-tone signal land

These third-order intermodulation products (IM3) can't be removed by filtering, because they sit among the wanted channels. Each has amplitude ¾a₃A³, so like the third harmonic it grows 3 dB for every 1 dB of input, three times faster than the wanted signal. On a plot of output against input in decibels, the fundamental is a line of slope 1 and IM3 a line of slope 3; extended, they cross at the third-order intercept point, IP3. The amplifier never actually reaches it (it compresses first), but it's a single number that predicts the IM3 level at any lower drive:

$$P_\text{IM3} = 3P_\text{out} - 2\,\text{OIP3} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{IM3 (dBc)} = -2(\text{OIP3} - P_\text{out})$$
per-tone powers in dBm; OIP3 is the output-referred intercept, IIP3 = OIP3 − gain

For a pure cubic nonlinearity, the same a₃ sets both compression and IP3, and the intercept comes out 9.6 dB above the input P1dB. For real amplifiers, OIP3 ≈ P1dB + 10 dB is a useful rule of thumb when only one is specified.

Harmonics matter most in transmitters: a PA's 2nd and 3rd harmonics land in other services' bands, and regulations limit them to an absolute level (Module 7), which is why every PA is followed by a harmonic filter (Module 3). Intermodulation matters most in receivers, where two strong signals outside the wanted channel, but inside the front-end filter, can produce an IM3 product exactly on the wanted channel.

Transmitted signals whose amplitude varies also suffer from intermodulation: the different frequency components of the modulated signal intermodulate with each other, spreading the spectrum into the neighbouring channels (spectral regrowth). The PA has to be run below its P1dB by at least the signal's peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR):

Design exerciseTwo strangers make a third

A receiver tuned to 433.92 MHz picks up interference whenever two other transmitters are active: one at 434.42 MHz and one at 434.92 MHz. Neither is on the receiver's channel, and both are inside its front-end filter.

What's happening?

  1. One of the transmitters has a harmonic at 433.92 MHz.
    Harmonics fall at multiples of a transmitter's frequency, far from 433.92 MHz.
  2. The two signals produce a third-order intermodulation product in the receiver's front end at 2 × 434.42 − 434.92 = 433.92 MHz, exactly on the wanted channel.
    Equally spaced signals put their IM3 product one spacing further along: right on the receiver's channel.
  3. The image frequency of the receiver's mixer.
    The image is twice the IF away, and only needs one signal.
  4. Thermal noise from the two transmitters.
    The interference only appears when both are on together, the signature of intermodulation.

Two signals at f₁ and f₂ generate IM3 at 2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁. Here 2(434.42) − 434.92 = 433.92 MHz. Because the product grows 3 dB for every 1 dB of the interferers, a modest increase in their level makes it much worse. The defences are a higher-IIP3 front end, less gain before the most nonlinear stage, a narrower front-end filter that attenuates the interferers before they meet a nonlinearity, or an attenuator switched in when signals are strong.

Phase noise and reciprocal mixing

An ideal oscillator produces a single, infinitely narrow spectral line. A real one has noise inside its loop that randomly modulates its phase, spreading a little power into "skirts" either side of the carrier. Phase noise is the power in a 1 Hz bandwidth at a given offset from the carrier, relative to the carrier: for example, −110 dBc/Hz at 100 kHz offset. It typically falls by about 20 dB per decade of offset, down to a flat floor far from the carrier. In the time domain, the same phase noise appears as jitter on the oscillator's edges.

In a receiver, the LO's phase noise causes a problem that has nothing to do with the wanted signal being weak. The mixer multiplies the whole input spectrum by the whole LO spectrum. A strong unwanted signal at an offset Δf from the wanted channel mixes with the main LO line to an IF Δf away from the wanted IF, where the IF filter rejects it. But it also mixes with the LO's phase-noise skirt at offset Δf, which lands it exactly at the wanted IF. In effect the strong signal is copied into the wanted channel with the LO's phase-noise profile, reduced by the phase noise at that offset. This is reciprocal mixing:

$$N_\text{RM} = P_\text{blocker} + \mathcal{L}(\Delta f) + 10\log_{10}B$$
𝓛(Δf): LO phase noise in dBc/Hz at the blocker's offset; B: channel bandwidth

Desense and dynamic range

A receiver is desensitised when something other than thermal noise raises its effective noise floor, so its sensitivity gets worse even though nothing is wrong with the wanted signal. The causes all appear earlier in this module:

  • Blocking: a strong signal compresses the front end, reducing gain for everything including the wanted signal.
  • Reciprocal mixing: a strong nearby signal is copied into the channel by the LO's phase noise.
  • Intermodulation: two strong signals produce a product on the channel.
  • Transmitter noise: a nearby transmitter's own wideband noise and spurious output falls in the receive band directly, and no receiver filter can remove it, because it's at the receive frequency.

The range of signal levels a receiver handles, from the weakest it can detect to the strongest before distortion appears, is its dynamic range. The most useful single measure is the spurious-free dynamic range: from the noise floor up to the input level at which IM3 products just reach the noise floor. Because IM3 rises at three times the slope of the signal, it works out to two-thirds of the distance from the noise floor to the IIP3:

$$\text{SFDR} = \tfrac{2}{3}\big(\text{IIP3} - N_\text{floor}\big)$$
Nfloor = −174 + 10 log B + NF, referred to the input

Worked example

A vehicle carries a 1 W, 915 MHz telemetry transmitter and a GPS receiver at 1575 MHz, with antennas a short distance apart and 20 dB of isolation between them. The GPS receiver's noise floor density is about −172 dBm/Hz (a 2 dB noise figure). A typical PA's own broadband noise output, far from its carrier, might be −135 dBm/Hz.

At 1575 MHz, that noise reaches the GPS input at −135 − 20 = −155 dBm/Hz, 17 dB above the GPS receiver's own floor, so its sensitivity falls by about 17 dB, enough to lose satellite lock. The GPS receiver's filters can't help, because the noise is in the GPS band. The fix is at the transmitter: a filter after the PA that attenuates 1575 MHz by at least 30 dB, which brings the noise below the GPS floor. This is the general rule for co-located radios: a transmitter's noise in a neighbour's receive band must be filtered at the transmitter.

Design exerciseMore gain, less range?

A receiver's range is worse than expected near a busy site with several strong transmitters. A colleague suggests adding a second 20 dB LNA to improve sensitivity.

What's the likely effect?

  1. A large improvement: more gain always helps a weak signal.
    The first LNA already sets the noise figure; more gain mainly raises the signal levels at later stages.
  2. Little improvement in noise figure, and probably worse performance: the strong signals are amplified by another 20 dB, so the later stages compress and generate intermodulation sooner, raising the effective noise floor.
    The cascade shows diminishing returns on noise figure, while the input-referred IIP3 falls by the added gain.
  3. No change at all.
    The added gain changes the strong-signal behaviour considerably.
  4. It improves only the receiver's selectivity.
    An LNA isn't selective; it amplifies the interferers too.

Near strong transmitters, the limit is dynamic range, not thermal noise. Adding gain in front of the existing chain lowers the whole receiver's input-referred IIP3 and P1dB by up to 20 dB, so desense from intermodulation and blocking gets worse. The useful changes are the opposite: better filtering in front of the LNA (accepting a little noise figure), a higher-IIP3 LNA, or less gain.

Channel interference

Radios share spectrum in channels, and interference between channels comes from both ends of the link. A receiver's selectivity is its ability to reject a signal in a neighbouring channel; a transmitter's adjacent channel leakage is how much of its power spills into the neighbouring channel. Either can limit two systems operating near each other.

Frequency planning is the cheapest defence. Before choosing channels, IFs and clock frequencies for a system, list every oscillator and transmitter on the vehicle and check where their harmonics, intermodulation products and mixer spurs land relative to every receiver. A spreadsheet that does this takes an afternoon; finding the problem in a flight test takes much longer.

Amplifier stability

An amplifier is a transistor with feedback, whether or not anyone designed it in: the device's internal capacitance between output and input couples some of the output back (S₁₂ ≠ 0). Combine that with reflections from the source and load, and a loop forms. If the loop's gain reaches one with the right phase, the amplifier oscillates.

The condition can be stated in the terms of Module 2. With a load ΓL on the output, the input reflection coefficient is

$$\Gamma_\text{in} = S_{11} + \frac{S_{12}S_{21}\,\Gamma_L}{1 - S_{22}\,\Gamma_L}$$

If |Γin| > 1 for some passive load, the input reflects more power than arrives: it behaves as a negative resistance, a source of power. Connect a source impedance that completes the loop, and the circuit generates its own signal. An amplifier is unconditionally stable if no passive source or load can do this. The Rollett test checks it from the S-parameters alone:

$$K = \frac{1 - |S_{11}|^2 - |S_{22}|^2 + |\Delta|^2}{2\,|S_{12}S_{21}|} > 1 \quad\text{and}\quad |\Delta| = |S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}S_{21}| < 1$$

A transistor with K < 1 is only conditionally stable: stable for some loads and unstable for others. The loads that cause trouble form a region on the Smith chart bounded by a stability circle, which the figure computes directly.

Things to try
  1. At 1 GHz, which kinds of load are dangerous: low or high resistance, inductive or capacitive?
  2. Switch to 500 MHz, far below the band the amplifier might be designed for. Is it more or less stable? Why does this matter for an amplifier only meant to work at 2 GHz?
  3. Find the smallest series resistor that removes the unstable region at all three frequencies.

The figure's second lesson is that stability has to hold at every frequency where the transistor has gain, not just in the operating band. Transistors have the most gain at low frequencies, which is where they're usually least stable, and an amplifier designed for 2 GHz can happily oscillate at 300 MHz through its bias network. Practical design follows from that:

  • Check K and |Δ| from near DC to well above the band, using the device's full S-parameter file.
  • Add loss where it costs least: a small series resistor at the gate or base, a resistor across the bias choke, or a ferrite bead on the bias line, all of which reduce low-frequency gain without much effect in band.
  • Decouple bias lines at both low and high frequencies (EE Module 3), so the supply can't close a loop.
  • Keep source and emitter connections short with several ground vias (Module 4): a little inductance there is series feedback that can make a stable transistor unstable.
Design exerciseAn LNA that oscillates with no antenna

An LNA works perfectly with its antenna connected, but on the bench with its input connector left open, the current draw rises and a spectrum analyzer on its output shows a strong signal at an unrelated frequency.

Why does disconnecting the antenna make it oscillate?

  1. The open input lets noise in.
    An open input lets in less noise, if anything. The current rise and the strong tone point to oscillation.
  2. An open circuit has |Γ| = 1. Somewhere in frequency, with the phase of the open seen through the input network, the source reflection falls in the amplifier's unstable region and closes the loop; the antenna's 50 Ω kept it out of that region.
    A conditionally stable amplifier is stable only for some source and load impedances; an open or a short is the most extreme.
  3. The DC bias depends on the antenna.
    The antenna doesn't usually set the DC bias.
  4. The open connector radiates the LNA's output back into its input.
    Possible in principle, but the main mechanism is the input impedance itself.

A conditionally stable amplifier is stable only for source and load impedances outside its stability circles. A well-matched antenna sits near the centre of the Smith chart, usually safely away from them. An open or shorted connector puts |Γ| = 1, on the edge of the chart, and the transmission line between the connector and the transistor rotates it through every phase as frequency changes, so at some frequency it lands in the unstable region. Designing for unconditional stability, or at least for stability with any passive termination, avoids this; it's also why amplifiers should be tested with their ports open and shorted as well as matched.

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●●○A telemetry link budget

A vehicle transmits 1 W (30 dBm) at 915 MHz from a 0 dBi antenna. The ground station is 30 km away, with a 6 dBi antenna. Cable and connector losses total 2 dB. The receiver's sensitivity is −110 dBm.

(a) What's the free-space path loss?

FSPL = 20 log₁₀(dkm) + 20 log₁₀(fMHz) + 32.44.

20 log₁₀ 30 = 29.5, 20 log₁₀ 915 = 59.2, so FSPL = 29.5 + 59.2 + 32.44 = 121.2 dB.

(b) What's the link margin?

Received power = 30 + 0 + 6 − 2 − 121.2 = −87.2 dBm. Margin = −87.2 − (−110) = 22.8 dB.

Discussion: what eats the margin

22.8 dB looks generous, but a linear vehicle antenna to a circular ground antenna costs 3 dB, the vehicle antenna's pattern may have nulls 10 dB or more deep at some attitudes, and ground reflections can cause fades of 10 dB or more at long range. The margin is what lets the link survive those combined.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●○○Receiver sensitivity

A receiver has a 50 kHz channel bandwidth, a 6 dB noise figure, and its demodulator needs 10 dB of SNR.

What's its sensitivity?

−174 + 10 log₁₀ B + NF + SNR.

10 log₁₀(50 000) = 47.0, so −174 + 47 + 6 + 10 = −111 dBm.

Discussion: datasheet sensitivity

Radio datasheets quote sensitivity at a specific data rate, bandwidth and error rate, measured on their evaluation board. Comparing two radios means comparing at the same conditions, and in a real product the antenna's noise temperature and any loss in front of the radio chip subtract from the datasheet figure.

Exercise 3Analysis · ●○○Intermodulation from the intercept

An amplifier has an OIP3 of +30 dBm. In a two-tone test, each tone at its output is 0 dBm.

(a) What's the level of each IM3 product?

PIM3 = 3Pout − 2·OIP3.

3 × 0 − 2 × 30 = −60 dBm, 60 dB below the tones.

(b) If both tones rise to +10 dBm, where is the IM3 product?

3 × 10 − 60 = −30 dBm: the tones rose 10 dB, the products 30 dB, so they're now only 40 dB below the tones.

Discussion: when the rule stops holding

The 3:1 slope holds well below compression. Near P1dB, higher-order terms start to matter and the products grow less predictably, so IP3 should be measured at levels where the slope is verified to be 3.

Exercise 4Analysis · ●●○A blocker and reciprocal mixing

A receiver with a 100 kHz channel and a 6 dB noise figure has an LO with −120 dBc/Hz of phase noise at 1 MHz offset. A −30 dBm signal appears 1 MHz from the wanted channel.

(a) How much noise does reciprocal mixing put in the channel?

−30 − 120 + 10 log₁₀(100 000) = −30 − 120 + 50 = −100 dBm.

(b) By how much does that desensitise the receiver?

Thermal floor = −174 + 50 + 6 = −118 dBm. Add the two noise powers in milliwatts, then compare with the thermal floor.

−100 dBm and −118 dBm add to −99.9 dBm, 18.1 dB above the thermal floor. The receiver loses 18 dB of sensitivity, most of its link margin, to a signal in a different channel.

Discussion: which to fix

The phase noise would need to be about 28 dB better at 1 MHz to make this blocker negligible. Alternatively, an RF filter that attenuates the blocker before the mixer helps if the blocker is far enough away for a filter to separate; at 1 MHz from a 900 MHz signal, it usually isn't.

Two signal generators and a 2-way combinerA spectrum analyzerA small amplifier on an evaluation boardA radio module pairStep attenuators
  1. Two-tone test. Combine two tones 1 MHz apart and drive the amplifier. Measure the tones and IM3 products at the output at several input levels; confirm the 3:1 slope and extrapolate to OIP3. Check that the IM3 doesn't come from the analyzer (change its attenuation).
  2. Sensitivity. Connect one radio's transmitter to the other's receiver through enough attenuation to reach the noise floor. Increase the attenuation until packets start to fail and record the received level.
  3. Blocking. At 3 dB above sensitivity, add a second generator's signal at a nearby frequency through the combiner and raise it until the link fails. Repeat at several offsets.
What you should see: IM3 products rising 3 dB per dB with an intercept roughly 10 dB above P1dB; a measured sensitivity close to the datasheet value; and a blocking level that improves rapidly with offset, following the IF filter's shape and the LO's phase noise.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Any matched source at room temperature delivers kTB of noise: −174 dBm/Hz. Sensitivity adds bandwidth, noise figure and required SNR to that.
  2. In the Friis cascade each stage's noise is divided by the gain before it; loss in front of the LNA counts in full. Put the LNA at the antenna.
  3. Free-space path loss rises 6 dB per doubling of distance; a link budget compares received power with sensitivity, and the margin covers what the model ignores.
  4. Third-order products grow 3 dB per dB and land in band; IP3 predicts them. Harmonics are filtered; IM3 can't be.
  5. LO phase noise copies strong nearby signals into the channel (reciprocal mixing).
  6. Desense comes from blocking, reciprocal mixing, intermodulation and other transmitters' noise; SFDR = ⅔(IIP3 − noise floor).
  7. An amplifier is stable for all passive terminations only if K > 1 and |Δ| < 1, at every frequency where it has gain.
