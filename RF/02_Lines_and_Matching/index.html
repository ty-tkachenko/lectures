Transmission lines, propagation & matching

Module 1 showed that a mismatch reflects power. This module is about controlling impedance: building lines with a known impedance on a circuit board, seeing what a line does to the impedance at its far end, and designing networks that make a load look like 50 Ω. Along the way it introduces the two tools RF engineers use to think about all of this, the Smith chart and S-parameters.

Builds on: RF modules 0–1, EE modules 3–4. Bench: NanoVNA or any VNA, a few chip capacitors and inductors, a test board.

Propagation modes

A transmission line guides an electromagnetic wave, and the pattern the fields take across the line's cross-section is called a mode. Different structures support different modes, and that decides what a line can and can't do.

The simplest is the TEM (transverse electromagnetic) mode, where both the electric and magnetic fields lie entirely in the cross-section, at right angles to the direction of travel. Coax carries a TEM wave: the electric field runs radially from the centre conductor to the shield, and the magnetic field circles the centre conductor. TEM needs at least two separate conductors, because the electric field has to start on one and end on the other. Its useful properties follow from that: it works all the way down to DC, its speed doesn't depend on frequency, and its characteristic impedance is a constant.

A microstrip trace has two conductors too, but its fields are partly in the board and partly in the air above, two materials with different speeds. The wave can't be purely TEM, but at frequencies where the trace is small compared with the wavelength it behaves almost exactly like one. That's called quasi-TEM, and it's why microstrip uses an effective dielectric constant somewhere between air's and the board's.

A hollow metal pipe, a waveguide, has only one conductor, so it can't carry TEM at all. It carries TE or TM modes instead, field patterns that "fit" between the walls, and each has a cutoff frequency below which it can't propagate. A waveguide is a high-pass structure: nothing gets through below the cutoff of its lowest mode.

DiagramField patterns in coax, microstrip and a rectangular waveguide

Lines that are meant to carry TEM can also carry higher-order modes once the frequency is high enough for a TE or TM pattern to fit inside them. For coax, the first one appears when the circumference is roughly a wavelength in the dielectric:

$$f_c \approx \frac{c}{\pi\,(a + b)\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$$
a and b are the inner and outer conductor radii

Above that frequency, energy can travel in two field patterns at two different speeds, and the line no longer behaves predictably. That, not loss, is what sets the top frequency of a cable or connector, and it's why higher-frequency connectors are physically smaller.

Design exerciseWhy an SMA connector has a frequency limit

SMA connectors are rated to about 18 GHz. Smaller 3.5 mm and 2.92 mm connectors go to 26 GHz and 40 GHz.

What mainly sets an SMA's upper frequency limit?

  1. Loss in the PTFE dielectric becomes too high.
    Loss does rise with frequency, but it rises gradually. Something more abrupt sets the limit.
  2. Above a certain frequency its dimensions let a second field pattern (a higher-order mode) propagate, so the connector stops behaving like a simple 50 Ω line.
    The limit comes from the size of the structure compared with the wavelength, which is why smaller connectors go higher.
  3. The threads can't be tightened enough.
    Torque matters for repeatability, but it isn't what sets the rating.
  4. The centre pin's inductance.
    The connector is designed as a 50 Ω line, so its inductance is balanced by its capacitance.

Coax carries TEM at any frequency, but once the wavelength in the dielectric becomes comparable to the circumference, a TE mode can also propagate. Energy split between two modes travelling at different speeds produces unpredictable reflections and resonances. Shrinking the connector pushes that frequency up, which is why each step up in frequency rating comes with a smaller connector.

Microstrip, stripline and coplanar waveguide

On a circuit board, a transmission line is a trace with a defined geometry relative to one or more ground conductors. Module 1 showed that \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}\), the square root of inductance per metre over capacitance per metre. That's enough to reason about every board line from first principles: anything that adds capacitance per metre (a wider trace, a closer plane, a higher dielectric constant, extra ground nearby) lowers the impedance, and anything that adds inductance per metre (a narrower trace, more distance to the return path) raises it.

Design exerciseFrom the outer layer to an inner layer

A 50 Ω microstrip trace sits on 0.2 mm of FR-4 above a ground plane. The same signal has to be rerouted on an inner layer as stripline, with 0.2 mm of the same FR-4 to the plane above and 0.2 mm to the plane below.

To stay at 50 Ω, how must the trace width change?

  1. It stays the same: the distance to the nearest plane is the same.
    Think about what the second plane does to the capacitance per metre.
  2. It must be narrower, because the second plane adds capacitance and all the field is now in the FR-4, so less width gives the same Z₀.
    More capacitance per metre lowers Z₀, and a narrower trace brings it back up.
  3. It must be wider, because the trace is now buried.
    Burying the trace adds capacitance, which lowers Z₀. A wider trace would lower it further.
  4. It must be wider, to compensate for slower propagation.
    Propagation speed and impedance are separate. Look at the capacitance.

From \(Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}\): the stripline has a plane on both sides, roughly doubling the capacitance per metre, and all its field is in FR-4 instead of partly in air. Both lower Z₀, so the trace has to be narrower: about 0.19 mm instead of 0.40 mm here. Try it in the calculator above. It's a common layout surprise: 50 Ω on an inner layer is noticeably thinner than on the outside.

The Smith chart

Module 1 introduced the reflection coefficient, \(\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)\). For a passive load it's a complex number with magnitude at most 1, so every passive impedance in the universe corresponds to a point inside a circle of radius 1 on the complex plane. A matched load (\(\Gamma = 0\)) is the centre; a short (\(\Gamma = -1\)) is the left edge; an open (\(\Gamma = +1\)) is the right edge. The Smith chart is just that circle, with impedances written on it.

To make the grid, take each resistance and reactance and work out where it lands. Normalise first, \(z = Z_L/Z_0\), then \(\Gamma = (z - 1)/(z + 1)\). All the impedances with the same resistance (say z = 1 + jx for every x) turn out to lie on a circle, and so do all those with the same reactance. Those two families of circles are the chart's grid:

  • Constant-resistance circles all touch the right edge. The r = 1 circle passes through the centre: every point on it has exactly 50 Ω of resistance.
  • Constant-reactance arcs curve out from the right edge. The upper half is inductive (positive reactance), the lower half capacitive. The horizontal line through the middle is pure resistance.

The chart earns its place through one more fact. On a lossless line, a wave reflected from the load travels back to you, so at a distance d from the load the reflection has picked up an extra round-trip phase of \(2\beta d\):

$$\Gamma(d) = \Gamma_L\,e^{-j2\beta d} \qquad \beta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$$
moving towards the generator rotates Γ clockwise at constant |Γ|; half a wavelength is one full turn

So adding line in front of a load rotates its point clockwise around the centre. The distance from the centre, |Γ|, doesn't change, which is why VSWR is the same everywhere on a lossless line. Every matching problem on the chart becomes geometry: move the point to the centre.

Design exerciseAdding cable to a mismatched antenna

An antenna has a VSWR of 2:1 measured at its connector. Someone adds 30 cm of good, low-loss 50 Ω cable between it and the radio.

What does the radio see?

  1. A better match, because the cable absorbs the reflection.
    A low-loss cable absorbs almost nothing. Think about what the cable does on the Smith chart.
  2. A different impedance, but still a 2:1 VSWR: the point rotates around the chart at the same distance from the centre.
    A lossless line rotates Γ without changing its magnitude, so VSWR is unchanged.
  3. A worse VSWR, because the cable adds its own mismatch.
    A good 50 Ω cable is matched to the system. It moves the impedance but doesn't change |Γ|.
  4. Exactly the same impedance.
    The impedance does change, unless the cable happens to be a multiple of half a wavelength long.

The cable rotates the antenna's point around the Smith chart by twice its electrical length, so the impedance the radio sees changes, but its distance from the centre doesn't, so the VSWR stays at 2:1. Changing cable lengths can't fix a mismatch; it only moves it. (A lossy cable does make the VSWR look better at the radio, by absorbing part of the reflection on its way back. Section 7 returns to that.)

Lumped-element matching

Matching means adding a network between a source and a load so that the source sees 50 Ω. It has to be lossless, otherwise it wastes the power it's meant to deliver, so it's built from inductors and capacitors. The simplest is the L-network: one component in series and one in parallel (shunt).

The reasoning from first principles goes like this. Suppose the load is 10 Ω and the source wants 50 Ω. A series reactance can't change resistance, only reactance. A shunt reactance, though, can transform resistance: put a reactance across a resistor and the combination has a different resistance and a reactance. So the recipe is:

  1. Add a series reactance to the low-resistance side, so that the combination, viewed as a parallel circuit, has exactly 50 Ω of resistance in parallel with some reactance.
  2. Add a shunt reactance that cancels that parallel reactance, leaving 50 Ω.

Working it through gives a single number that sets both components, the loaded Q of the network:

$$Q = \sqrt{\frac{R_\text{high}}{R_\text{low}} - 1} \qquad X_\text{series} = Q\,R_\text{low} \qquad X_\text{shunt} = \frac{R_\text{high}}{Q}$$
for purely resistive source and load

On the Smith chart, a series element moves the point along a circle of constant resistance, and a shunt element moves it along a circle of constant conductance. An L-network is two such moves that end at the centre. There are always two solutions (one moves up first, the other down), typically a low-pass and a high-pass version. Q also sets the bandwidth: a large impedance ratio means a high Q and a narrow match.

Design exerciseA harder match

Two power amplifiers need their outputs matched to 50 Ω with an L-network. One has an output resistance of 25 Ω, the other 5 Ω.

Which match has the wider bandwidth?

  1. The 5 Ω one, because its components are larger.
    Larger reactances relative to the resistance mean more stored energy, which narrows the bandwidth.
  2. The 25 Ω one, because the smaller impedance ratio means a lower Q.
    Q = √(50/25 − 1) = 1 against √(50/5 − 1) = 3.
  3. They're the same: both are matched perfectly at the design frequency.
    Both are perfect at one frequency. Bandwidth is about how quickly the match degrades away from it.
  4. Neither has a bandwidth: an L-network is broadband.
    Every reactive match has a bandwidth, set by its Q.

The loaded Q is set by the impedance ratio: Q = 1 for 25 Ω and Q = 3 for 5 Ω. A higher Q means the network stores more energy relative to what it passes, so its impedance changes faster with frequency and the match is narrower. Low-impedance transistor outputs are hard to match broadband for exactly this reason, and Section 8 shows the standard way round it: match in several smaller steps.

Input impedance and quarter-wave transformers

The Smith chart rotation can be written as a formula. At distance ℓ from the load, the voltage and current are each the sum of a forward wave and a reflected wave. Dividing one by the other, and using \(\Gamma(\ell) = \Gamma_L e^{-j2\beta\ell}\), gives the impedance seen looking into the line:

$$Z_\text{in} = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta\ell}{Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta\ell}$$

Three special lengths are worth knowing by heart, because each one turns the formula into something simple:

  • Half a wavelength: \(\tan\beta\ell = 0\), so \(Z_\text{in} = Z_L\). A λ/2 line repeats the load, whatever Z₀ is (one full turn on the Smith chart).
  • A quarter-wavelength: \(\tan\beta\ell \to \infty\), so \(Z_\text{in} = Z_0^2 / Z_L\). A λ/4 line inverts the load: a short becomes an open, a low impedance becomes a high one.
  • A short length shorted at the end looks like an inductor; open at the end, a capacitor. These stubs are how transmission lines replace lumped components at high frequency.

The quarter-wave inversion gives a matching network made of nothing but a line. To match a real load \(R_L\) to \(Z_0\), insert a quarter-wavelength of line with impedance

$$Z_1 = \sqrt{Z_0\,R_L}$$
the geometric mean; to match 100 Ω to 50 Ω, a 70.7 Ω line

Then \(Z_\text{in} = Z_1^2/R_L = Z_0\). It's exact at one frequency, where the line is precisely λ/4, and degrades away from it.

Design exerciseA quarter-wave transformer at twice its frequency

A 70.7 Ω quarter-wave transformer matches a 100 Ω load to 50 Ω at 915 MHz. The same board is used for a second product at 1830 MHz.

What does the 50 Ω source see at 1830 MHz?

  1. 50 Ω: the transformer still matches.
    The transformer only works where the line is a quarter-wavelength long.
  2. About 100 Ω: at twice the frequency the line is half a wavelength, which repeats the load, so the source sees the mismatch as if the transformer weren't there.
    A λ/2 line returns the load impedance unchanged.
  3. About 25 Ω: the inversion doubles.
    Inversion only happens at odd multiples of λ/4.
  4. An open circuit.
    That would need a short at the load and a quarter-wave line.

The line's electrical length is proportional to frequency. At 1830 MHz the quarter-wave line is a half-wave line, and a half-wave line repeats its load: the source sees 100 Ω again, a 2:1 VSWR. The transformer matches again at 3 × 915 MHz (three quarter-wavelengths). Every matching network built from lines is periodic in frequency like this.

S-parameters

At low frequency you describe a circuit by voltages and currents at its terminals, measured with opens and shorts. At RF that doesn't work: open and short circuits are hard to make at the end of a line (it's never a perfect open), and many devices oscillate or break when you try. Instead, RF engineers describe a network by what it does to waves, with every port terminated in Z₀.

Send a wave into port 1 of a two-port network. Some of it reflects back out of port 1, some comes out of port 2, and the rest is absorbed or radiated. The scattering parameters are the ratios:

$$S_{11} = \frac{\text{wave out of port 1}}{\text{wave into port 1}} \qquad S_{21} = \frac{\text{wave out of port 2}}{\text{wave into port 1}}$$
with port 2 terminated in Z₀; S₂₂ and S₁₂ are the same measured from port 2

S₁₁ is exactly the reflection coefficient Γ seen at port 1, and S₂₁ is the transmission: the gain of an amplifier, the loss of a cable. They're complex (magnitude and phase) and depend on frequency. Since power goes as the square of a wave's amplitude, energy conservation gives a useful check for any passive network:

$$|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{21}|^2 \le 1$$
equal to 1 for a lossless network; the shortfall is power absorbed or radiated

Two more properties make S-parameters easy to sanity-check. A network made of ordinary passive parts is reciprocal: S₂₁ = S₁₂. And a symmetrical network has S₁₁ = S₂₂. A VNA measures all four S-parameters directly, which is why it's the central instrument of RF work (Module 5).

Design exerciseWhere does the power go?

A VNA measures a two-port at 1 GHz: S₂₁ = −6 dB and S₁₁ = −30 dB.

Of the power sent into port 1, roughly where does it end up?

  1. Half is passed and half is reflected.
    −6 dB is a quarter of the power, and −30 dB is a thousandth.
  2. About a quarter is passed, almost none is reflected, and about three quarters is absorbed or radiated inside the network.
    |S₂₁|² = 0.25 and |S₁₁|² = 0.001, so about 75 % is lost in the network.
  3. About three quarters is reflected, because it isn't passed.
    S₁₁ tells you directly how much is reflected, and it's very little.
  4. All of it is passed: S-parameters in dB don't describe power.
    The square of an S-parameter's magnitude is a power ratio.

Convert to power: −6 dB is 0.25, −30 dB is 0.001. Energy conservation leaves 1 − 0.25 − 0.001 ≈ 0.75 absorbed or radiated. A network that's well matched but passes little is lossy: an attenuator, a long cable, or a filter's resistive loss. A reactive filter in its stop band does the opposite: it passes little because it reflects almost everything. S₁₁ is what tells the two cases apart.

Return loss and insertion loss

Return loss and insertion loss are S₁₁ and S₂₁ expressed as positive numbers of decibels, the way they appear on datasheets:

$$\text{RL} = -20\log_{10}|S_{11}| \qquad \text{IL} = -20\log_{10}|S_{21}|$$
a good match has a high return loss; a good cable has a low insertion loss

Insertion loss includes everything that stops power getting through: resistive loss, dielectric loss, radiation and reflection. When a component is mismatched, part of its insertion loss is just the reflected power, called the mismatch loss, \(-10\log_{10}(1 - |S_{11}|^2)\). For a return loss of 10 dB it's only 0.46 dB, which is why a 10 dB return loss is often good enough.

!
Loss makes a bad match look goodMeasured through a lossy cable, any load's return loss improves by twice the cable's loss, because the reflection is attenuated on the way to the load and again on the way back. Put a 3 dB cable in front of an open circuit, which reflects everything, and you measure a 6 dB return loss. Always measure a match at the point that matters, with the VNA calibrated there, not at the far end of a cable.
Design exerciseAn antenna that measures too well

A ground-station antenna is checked from the shack, through 20 m of cable with 4 dB of loss. The VNA shows a return loss of 18 dB, which looks excellent.

What's the return loss at the antenna itself?

  1. 18 dB: the cable is 50 Ω, so it doesn't change the match.
    A matched cable doesn't change |Γ| if it's lossless. This one isn't.
  2. 22 dB, since the cable's loss hides 4 dB.
    The reflection passes through the cable twice.
  3. About 10 dB: the reflection was attenuated by 4 dB on the way up and 4 dB on the way back.
    18 − 2 × 4 = 10 dB.
  4. It can't be worse than what's measured.
    Cable loss can only make a match look better than it is.

The VNA's signal loses 4 dB reaching the antenna, and whatever reflects loses another 4 dB on the way back, so the measured return loss is 8 dB better than the real one: 18 − 8 = 10 dB at the antenna. That's acceptable, but nowhere near what the measurement suggested. A cable with high enough loss makes even an open circuit look like a decent match.

Bandwidth

Every match made from reactive parts or line sections is exact at one frequency and degrades away from it. The bandwidth of a match is the range of frequencies over which the return loss stays above some limit, often 10 dB (VSWR below about 2:1) or 15 dB for more demanding parts.

The reason a match has a bandwidth at all is energy storage. A network that transforms a large impedance ratio has to store a lot of energy in its inductors and capacitors relative to the power passing through (a high Q), and stored energy makes the impedance change quickly with frequency. Two consequences follow:

  • Large impedance ratios are inherently narrowband. There's a theoretical limit, the Bode–Fano limit, on how wide a match any network can achieve for a given load.
  • Matching in steps widens the bandwidth. Going from 5 Ω to 50 Ω in two steps (5 to 15.8 to 50 Ω) uses two networks each with a lower Q, and the combination is broader than a single high-Q step. The same applies to quarter-wave transformers: two or more sections in cascade are broader than one.

Design exerciseOne step or two?

A 10 Ω load can be matched to 50 Ω with one L-network, or with two L-networks in cascade through an intermediate impedance of about 22 Ω.

Which gives the wider bandwidth?

  1. One L-network, because it has fewer parts to go wrong.
    Fewer parts is simpler, but think about the Q of each step.
  2. Two L-networks, because each step covers a smaller ratio (about 2.2:1), so each has a lower Q.
    Q ≈ √(2.2 − 1) ≈ 1.1 for each step, against Q = 2 for the single step.
  3. They're identical, since both match perfectly at f₀.
    Both are perfect at f₀; they differ in how fast the match degrades away from it.
  4. One L-network, because two networks add their mismatches.
    At f₀ each step is matched to the next, so nothing adds up there. Away from f₀, the lower Q of each step wins.

The single step has Q = √(50/10 − 1) = 2. Splitting the 5:1 ratio into two steps of √5 ≈ 2.24:1 gives each a Q of about 1.1, and the cascade is noticeably broader. Choosing the intermediate impedance as the geometric mean of the two ends (√(10 × 50) ≈ 22 Ω) shares the ratio equally. The same idea is behind multi-section transformers and tapered lines, and the Bode–Fano limit says how far it can go.

Exercise 1Layout · ●●○A 50 Ω trace on a four-layer board

A telemetry board uses a standard four-layer stackup. The RF trace from the radio to the antenna connector runs on the top layer over the ground plane on layer 2.

Given Dielectric, top layer to layer 20.2 mm FR-4, εr = 4.2 Frequency915 MHz

(a) What trace width gives 50 Ω?

Use the calculator above, or the microstrip formula: try w/h around 2.

About 0.40 mm (w/h ≈ 2.0), with εeff ≈ 3.2.

(b) Wavelength on this trace at 915 MHz?

\(\lambda = c/(f\sqrt{\varepsilon_\text{eff}}) = 3\times10^8/(915\times10^6 \times \sqrt{3.2}) \approx 18.3\ \text{cm}\).

Discussion: what changes it?

Solder mask over the trace adds a little dielectric above it, lowering Z₀ by a few ohms. Copper thickness does too. Fabricators control impedance to about ±10 % by default, tighter on request. For a 915 MHz trace a few centimetres long, those effects cost very little; at higher frequencies, or for long runs, specify a controlled-impedance build and give the fabricator the target, letting them adjust the width for their exact materials.

That's a quarter-wavelength in free space. On the board the wave is slower, so the wavelength is shorter.

Exercise 3Design · ●●●Matching an amplifier's 10 Ω output

A small power amplifier has an output resistance of 10 Ω at 915 MHz and drives a 50 Ω antenna. Design a low-pass L-network: a series inductor on the 10 Ω side and a shunt capacitor on the 50 Ω side.

(a) Loaded Q of the network?

\(Q = \sqrt{50/10 - 1} = 2\).

(b) Series inductance?

The series reactance is \(Q R_\text{low}\); then \(L = X/2\pi f\).

\(X_s = 2 \times 10 = 20\ \Omega\), so \(L = 20/(2\pi \times 915\times10^6) \approx 3.48\ \text{nH}\).

(c) Shunt capacitance?

The shunt reactance is \(R_\text{high}/Q\); then \(C = 1/(2\pi f X)\).

\(X_p = 50/2 = 25\ \Omega\), so \(C = 1/(2\pi \times 915\times10^6 \times 25) \approx 6.96\ \text{pF}\).

Discussion: why low-pass?

Both L-network solutions match at 915 MHz, but the low-pass one also attenuates the amplifier's harmonics at 1830 and 2745 MHz, which helps meet emission limits (Module 7). In practice you'd use the nearest standard values (3.3 nH and 6.8 pF), tune on the bench with a VNA, and account for the parts' own parasitics and the pads they sit on, which at 915 MHz are no longer negligible (Module 0).

Matching on the Smith chart

  1. Calibrate and read loads. Calibrate at the end of a cable. Connect the 100 Ω resistor board and find its point on the Smith chart. Compare it with where it should be, and look at how it moves across frequency (the pads and parts aren't ideal).
  2. Rotation. Add a second cable between the calibration plane and the 100 Ω board. Watch the point rotate around the chart at constant |Γ| as the frequency sweeps.
  3. L-network. Design a low-pass L-network for the 10 Ω resistor at a frequency of your choice, build it with the nearest standard values, and measure it. Adjust one part at a time and watch which way the point moves.
  4. S-parameters. Measure S₁₁ and S₂₁ of the attenuator and work out where the power goes.
What you should see: resistive loads on the horizontal axis of the chart, a load that circles the centre as cable is added, an L-network that brings the 10 Ω point close to the centre at the design frequency, and an attenuator with low S₁₁ and an S₂₁ close to its rating.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Two-conductor lines carry TEM (coax, stripline) or quasi-TEM (microstrip) waves. Higher-order modes set the upper frequency limit of cables and connectors.
  2. Z₀ = √(L′/C′): anything that adds capacitance per metre lowers Z₀. A 50 Ω stripline is narrower than a 50 Ω microstrip on the same dielectric.
  3. The Smith chart maps every passive impedance inside a unit circle. A lossless line rotates a load clockwise at constant |Γ|; half a wavelength is a full turn.
  4. An L-network matches with two reactances; its Q is set by the impedance ratio, and its bandwidth falls as Q rises.
  5. A λ/4 line inverts impedances (Z₀²/ZL); a λ/2 line repeats them. A quarter-wave transformer of √(Z₀RL) matches a real load at one frequency.
  6. S₁₁ is reflection, S₂₁ is transmission. |S₁₁|² + |S₂₁|² ≤ 1 for passive networks; the shortfall is loss.
  7. Cable loss improves measured return loss by twice the loss. Match in several low-Q steps for more bandwidth.
