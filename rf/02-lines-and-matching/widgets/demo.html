Design challenge:

A 2.45 GHz PCB antenna measures ZL = 30 + j40 Ω at its feed. The plan is to add 50 Ω line until the impedance looking in is purely resistive, then finish with a λ/4 transformer (Section 5). The board is 4-layer FR-4 with 0.20 mm to ground (εr 4.4) and a 0.15 mm minimum trace width. Its 50 Ω line is 0.38 mm wide with εeff 3.33.

  • Zin purely resistive: |Xin| ≤ 2 Ω.
  • The λ/4 transformer that follows, Z1 = √(50 Rin), has to be buildable: 0.15–1.5 mm wide on this stackup.
  • Use the shortest line that meets both.

Produce: the line length (in λ and in mm), Rin, Z1 and Z1's width. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Slide the line length and watch the purple dot: Zin is real wherever it crosses the horizontal axis, and that happens twice per half turn. Is the first crossing buildable? (The microstrip widget in Module 1 gives widths.)

Check your design

Pass if: the load is 30 + j40 Ω in a 50 Ω system, |Xin| ≤ 2 Ω, and the transformer width for that Rin is between 0.15 and 1.5 mm.

Worked answer.

ΓL = (ZL − 50) / (ZL + 50) = (−20 + j40) / (80 + j40) = 0.5 ∠ 90°, VSWR = 3

Moving toward the generator rotates Γ clockwise by 720° × ℓ/λ. The first crossing is on the right after 90°: ℓ = 0.125 λ, Rin = 50 × VSWR = 150 Ω, Z1 = √(50 × 150) = 86.6 Ω, which is 0.13 mm wide: too thin for the fab. The second crossing is on the left after 270°: ℓ = 0.375 λ, Rin = 50 / VSWR = 16.7 Ω, Z1 = √(50 × 16.7) = 28.9 Ω, which is 0.87 mm wide and buildable.

λg = c / (f √3.33) = 67.1 mm → 0.375 λ = 25.2 mm of 50 Ω line; the 28.9 Ω section (εeff 3.58) is 16.2 mm long

Other answers can be valid if they meet every requirement.

Examples:

Try this: start with the 25 Ω → 50 Ω example: two answers, one low-pass (series L, shunt C) and one high-pass (series C, shunt L). Then pick "2 Ω PA output". The node Q jumps to about 5 and the 10 dB-return-loss bandwidth collapses. That's why big impedance ratios are usually matched in two or more smaller steps.
Design challenge:

A 915 MHz chip antenna measures 18 − j22 Ω at its feed on your board. Match it to the radio's 50 Ω output with one series part and one shunt part from the E12 series (…3.9, 4.7, 5.6, 6.8, 8.2, 10…). The radio's 2nd and 3rd harmonics, at 1.83 and 2.75 GHz, must not get an easy path to the antenna.

  • Return loss ≥ 15 dB at 902, 915 and 928 MHz with your rounded E12 parts.
  • E12 values only.
  • A topology chosen for a reason (harmonics or DC block).

Produce: the topology, the two part values, the return loss at the band edges, and a one-sentence justification. Enter your parts below and the live check computes their return loss. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Load the antenna into the designer (RS 50, 915 MHz, RL 18, XL −22). It gives two exact answers. Which one has a series inductor and a shunt capacitor, and what does that do to 2.75 GHz? Then round each part to the nearest E12 value.

Check your design

Pass if: the designer holds the antenna case, the network is low-pass, both parts are E12 values, and return loss is ≥ 15 dB at 902, 915 and 928 MHz.

Worked answer. RL < 50 Ω, so the series part goes next to the load (ω = 2π × 915 MHz = 5.749×10⁹ rad/s):

Xseries = ±√(RL(50 − RL)) − XL = ±24 + 22 → +46 Ω (L = 8.0 nH) or −2 Ω (C = 87 pF)Bshunt = ±√((50 − RL)/RL) / 50 = ±26.7 mS → C = B/ω = 4.64 pF or L = 1/(ωB) = 6.52 nH

Low-pass: shunt 4.7 pF, series 8.2 nH (E12). Return loss is 37.8 / 29.0 / 24.6 dB at 902 / 915 / 928 MHz. The series L and shunt C roll off the harmonics. The high-pass answer would let them through. Its 87 pF series cap works as a DC block, which is only worth having if the antenna feed carries DC. The node Q is 1.33, so the band is easy.

Design challenge:

An S-band ground-station feed has to cover 2.2–2.7 GHz (satellite telemetry plus the 2.4 GHz band). It feeds four 50 Ω elements in parallel, so the feed point is 12.5 Ω. You'll match it to the 50 Ω cable with λ/4 microstrip sections on RO4350B, 0.508 mm thick (εr 3.66).

  • RL = 12.5 Ω, f0 = 2.45 GHz.
  • Return loss ≥ 20 dB from 2.2 to 2.7 GHz.
  • Use the fewest sections that meet it.

Produce: the number of sections, the section impedances, and each section's width and physical λ/4 length on RO4350B 0.508 mm (use the microstrip widget in Module 1 for widths and εeff). Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Fractional bandwidth = (fhigh − flow) / f0. Compare it with the widget's “RL ≥ 20 dB” readout for one and two sections. Each section has a different width, so each has its own εeff.

Check your design

Pass if: the widget is set to RL = 12.5 Ω and f0 = 2450 MHz with εeff between 2.9 and 3.3, and the section count you choose is the fewest that covers 20.4%.

Worked answer. The required fractional bandwidth is (2.7 − 2.2)/2.45 = 20.4%.

1 section: Z1 = √(50 × 12.5) = 25 Ω; FBW = 2 − (4/π) acos[ (Γm/√(1 − Γm²)) · 2√(Z0RL) / |RL − Z0| ] = 17.1%

One section reaches only 18.5 dB return loss at the band edges, so it fails.

2-section binomial: Z1 = 503/4 · 12.51/4 = 35.4 Ω, Z2 = 501/4 · 12.53/4 = 17.7 Ω → FBW 47.7%, RL 34.4 dB at 2.2 and 2.7 GHz

Two sections: 50 Ω cable → 35.4 Ω → 17.7 Ω → 12.5 Ω load. On RO4350B 0.508 mm, the 35.4 Ω section is 1.88 mm wide with εeff 2.98, so λ/4 = c/(4f0√εeff) = 17.7 mm. The 17.7 Ω section is 4.56 mm wide with εeff 3.20, so λ/4 = 17.1 mm. The widget uses one εeff for both, so set it to about 3.1 for a 17.4 mm average.

Try this: a filter datasheet says S11 = −15 dB and S21 = −0.8 dB. Where did the missing power go? Now set S11 to −3 dB and S21 to −1 dB: is that possible for a passive part?
Design challenge:

A harmonic filter sits between a 1 W (+30 dBm) 915 MHz PA and the antenna. You have three candidates: A S11 −12 dB, S21 −0.6 dB; B S11 −20 dB, S21 −0.9 dB; C S11 −18 dB, S21 −0.5 dB.

  • At most 2% of the power reflected back to the PA.
  • At least 80% delivered to the antenna.
  • No more than 150 mW of heat in the filter, its power rating with margin.

Produce: the spec limits for S11 and S21 in dB, the power each candidate dissipates, and your choice. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Turn each dB value into a power fraction with 10dB/10. Whatever is neither reflected nor transmitted turns into heat, since a filter radiates almost nothing.

Check your design

Pass if: at the S-parameters you've set, reflected ≤ 2%, transmitted ≥ 80%, and the heat at 1 W input is ≤ 150 mW.

Worked answer.

S11 ≤ 10 log 0.02 = −17.0 dB ; S21 ≥ 10 log 0.80 = −0.97 dB ; heat = (1 − |S11|² − |S21|²) × 1 W

A reflects 6.3%, so it fails. B reflects 1.0% and passes 81.3%, but it turns 177 mW into heat and fails even though both of its S-parameters look fine. C reflects 1.6%, passes 89.1% and dissipates 93 mW: choose C.
