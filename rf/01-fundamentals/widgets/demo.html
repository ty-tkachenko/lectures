Design challenge:

You're buying a 915 MHz antenna for a telemetry ground station. The radio puts out 1 W. Your link budget assumes at least 90% of that reaches the antenna, and the radio's datasheet says its PA is safe up to VSWR 2:1. A sample antenna measures 70 + j20 Ω on a VNA at 915 MHz.

  • Power reaching the antenna ≥ 90% (mismatch loss ≤ 0.46 dB).
  • VSWR ≤ 2:1 for PA safety.
  • System impedance Z0 = 50 Ω.

Produce: the maximum VSWR (and the matching return loss) to write on the purchase spec, and a pass or fail for the sample with its VSWR and delivered power. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Turn “90% delivered” into |Γ| first: delivered fraction = 1 − |Γ|². Then VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). Which of the two requirements is stricter?

Check your design

Pass if: the load is the sample (70 + j20 Ω), at least 90% of the power reaches it, and VSWR ≤ 2:1.

Worked answer.

|Γ|max = √(1 − 0.90) = 0.316 → VSWRmax = 1.316 / 0.684 = 1.92 (RL ≥ 10.0 dB)

That's stricter than the PA's 2:1, so write VSWR ≤ 1.9:1 across 902–928 MHz.

Γ = (70 + j20 − 50) / (70 + j20 + 50) = (20 + j20) / (120 + j20) → |Γ| = 28.3 / 121.7 = 0.232

Sample: VSWR 1.61, return loss 12.7 dB, 1 − 0.054 = 94.6% delivered, so it passes.

Try this: (1) Pick Open end, then change εeff. The cut stays at the same distance, but the time it takes the echo to come back (top axis) changes. A real TDR only measures time, so if you guess εeff wrong you will look for the fault in the wrong place. (2) Pick the capacitive bump and slow the rise time to 200 ps. The dip gets shallower: a slow TDR can't see small things. (3) Pick Impedance section at 60 Ω and make it longer. In the lower plot the S11 ripples get closer together: ripple period = vp/(2 × length).
Design challenge:

A 2.45 GHz board has worse return loss than expected. You TDR its 50 Ω microstrip feed (FR-4, εeff ≈ 3.4) with a 35 ps step. The trace shows one capacitive dip. Its echo arrives 1.23 ns after launch (round trip), and fitting the dip gives a time constant Z0C/2 = 25 ps.

  • Locate the fault to ±0.5 cm and size its extra capacitance to ±0.1 pF.
  • Reproduce the reading in the widget: round trip 1.23 ns, time constant 25 ps, εeff 3.4, 35 ps rise time.
  • Propose a fix that brings this discontinuity to return loss ≥ 20 dB at 2.45 GHz.

Produce: the distance, the capacitance, the largest capacitance you can live with after the fix, and a one-sentence fix. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

A TDR measures time, and the echo travels there and back. The speed comes from εeff. For the fix: a shunt C on a matched line reflects |Γ| = ωCZ0 / √(4 + (ωCZ0)²).

Check your design

Pass if: the widget shows a capacitive bump with εeff 3.4, a round trip of 1.23 ± 0.03 ns and a time constant of 25 ± 1.5 ps.

Worked answer.

vp = c / √3.4 = 1.626×10⁸ m/s → d = vp t / 2 = 1.626×10⁸ × 1.23×10⁻⁹ / 2 = 10.0 cmC = 2τ / Z0 = 2 × 25 ps / 50 Ω = 1.0 pF

At 2.45 GHz, ωCZ0 = 0.77, so |Γ| = 0.36 and RL = 8.9 dB: that's your problem. For RL ≥ 20 dB you need |Γ| ≤ 0.1:

ωCZ0 ≤ √(0.04 / 0.99) = 0.201 → C ≤ 0.201 / (2π × 2.45×10⁹ × 50) = 0.26 pF

Fix: something 10 cm down the line, such as an oversized pad, a test point or a via, adds about 1 pF. Shrink it, or cut the ground out under the pad, to remove at least 0.74 pF. Then TDR it again.

Design challenge:

Your 2.45 GHz radio has 0.5 mm wide RF pads. On a 2-layer, 1.6 mm board a 50 Ω trace is about 3 mm wide, and that won't fit. You're moving to a 4-layer FR-4 board (εr 4.4) with 0.20 mm of prepreg between the top copper and the ground plane. The fab's minimum trace width is 0.15 mm.

  • Z0 = 50 ± 2 Ω.
  • Width between 0.15 mm (fab minimum) and 0.60 mm (fits the pad without a big taper).
  • FR-4, εr 4.4, h = 0.20 mm. Report εeff and the λ/4 length at 2.45 GHz.

Produce: the width, Z0, εeff, the λ/4 length on this trace at 2.45 GHz, and one sentence on why a thin dielectric helps. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

For a given εr, Z0 depends on the ratio w/h, not on w and h separately. Set the frequency box to 2450 MHz so the wavelength readout is useful.

Check your design

Pass if: the widget is set to FR-4, h = 0.20 mm and 2450 MHz, with Z0 between 48 and 52 Ω and a width between 0.15 and 0.60 mm.

Worked answer. 50 Ω on FR-4 needs w/h ≈ 1.92 (3.08 mm on 1.6 mm), so on 0.20 mm: w ≈ 0.385 mm. Take w = 0.38 mm: Z0 = 50.4 Ω, εeff = 3.33.

λg = c / (f √εeff) = 3×10⁸ / (2.45×10⁹ × √3.33) = 67.1 mm → λ/4 = 16.8 mm

A thin dielectric gives a narrow 50 Ω line that lands straight on the pad. It also holds the field tight to the ground plane, so the line radiates and couples less.

Try this: set every block to 0 dB, then make one block −3 dB. The power halves. Two −3 dB blocks give a quarter. Now make the source −30 dBm (1 µW) and add a +30 dB amplifier: what comes out, in dBm and in mW?
Design challenge:

A 915 MHz uplink from a ground station to a drone or rocket. The chain is: radio chip (programmable output) → short jumper, −0.5 dB (Cable block) → PA, +20 dB gain, rated for up to +30 dBm out (Amplifier) → harmonic low-pass filter, −1.0 dB (Filter) → 5 m feedline up the mast (Attenuator block): thin coax at −2.5 dB or thick coax at −0.5 dB → antenna, +6 dBi.

  • EIRP ≤ +36 dBm (US 902–928 MHz limit for digital-modulation radios: 1 W conducted with up to 6 dBi of antenna gain).
  • EIRP ≥ +33 dBm for link margin.
  • PA output ≤ +30 dBm.

Produce: the radio's power setting, the feedline choice, the PA output, the power at the antenna port, the EIRP and your margin to the legal limit. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Work backwards: antenna port = EIRP − 6 dBi. Try the thin coax first with the PA at its rated +30 dBm. Can it reach 33 dBm EIRP?

Check your design

Pass if: the blocks are set to −0.5, +20, −1.0 and either −0.5 or −2.5 dB, the PA output is ≤ +30 dBm, and EIRP = output + 6 dBi is between +33 and +36 dBm.

Worked answer.

EIRP = Pradio − 0.5 + 20 − 1.0 − Lcoax + 6 dBi

Thin coax: even with the PA at +30 dBm, EIRP = 30 − 1.0 − 2.5 + 6 = 32.5 dBm, which is too low. Thick coax: EIRP = PA output + 4.5 dB, so the PA output must be 28.5–30 dBm and the radio 9.0–10.5 dBm. Reference: thick coax, radio at +10 dBm → PA 29.5 dBm → antenna port 28.0 dBm (631 mW) → EIRP 34.0 dBm (2.5 W), 2 dB under the limit.

Polarization mismatch

Presets:
Fraction of power received
Polarization loss

Try this: with two linear antennas, set the tilts 90° apart. In theory nothing gets through. Now make one of them circular: the loss is 3 dB at every tilt. That's why a spinning rocket often carries a circular antenna, or why the ground station uses one.
Design challenge:

Your rocket carries a simple linear antenna. Seen from the ground station near the pad, looking up at the tail, its E-field rotates as the airframe rolls, often several turns a second. You get to choose the ground antenna.

  • The rocket antenna stays linear: it's all that fits.
  • Polarization loss ≤ 3.5 dB at every roll angle.
  • Loss changes by no more than 1 dB over a full roll, so the signal doesn't fade as the rocket spins.

Produce: the ground-antenna polarization, the worst-case loss, how much it varies over a roll, and one sentence explaining why. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Set both antennas, then sweep the transmit tilt from 0° to 180° the way the roll would. Look at the worst value, not the best.

Check your design

Pass if: the transmit antenna is linear and, over every transmit tilt from 0° to 180°, the loss stays ≤ 3.5 dB and varies by ≤ 1 dB.

Worked answer. With a linear ground antenna the loss is −10 log(cos²ψ): 0 dB aligned, 3 dB at 45°, 6 dB at 60°, and a null at 90° (20–30 dB in practice). That fails both requirements.

linear → circular: received fraction = ½ at every angle → 10 log 2 = 3.01 dB, variation 0 dB

Choose a circular ground antenna (RHCP or LHCP, for example a helix) and budget 3 dB for it. You trade 3 dB on the best case for a link that never drops into a null.

Try this: on FR-4, find where the green line crosses the copper line. Switch to RO4350B: the crossover moves up a lot. Then make the dielectric thinner. The trace gets narrower and the copper loss rises. (This model uses smooth copper. Real rough copper adds more, which Module 3 covers.)
Design challenge:

A 5.8 GHz FPV video transmitter. The 50 Ω line from the transmitter chip to the edge-launch SMA has to be 10 cm long, and the SMA footprint takes a center trace no wider than 1.2 mm.

  • Loss ≤ 1.0 dB over the 10 cm at 5.8 GHz.
  • 50 Ω trace width ≤ 1.2 mm.
  • Use FR-4 if it works (cheapest). Otherwise use a low-loss laminate.

Produce: the material, the dielectric height, the trace width, the predicted loss, and one sentence on why FR-4 does or doesn't work. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

In this model dielectric loss doesn't depend on thickness, but copper loss does: a thinner dielectric means a narrower trace and more copper loss. Check FR-4's dielectric loss on its own first.

Check your design

Pass if: the length is 10 cm, the frequency is within 3% of 5.8 GHz, the loss over the trace is ≤ 1.0 dB, and the 50 Ω width is ≤ 1.2 mm.

Worked answer.

αd = 8.686 · k₀ εr (εeff − 1) tanδ / (2 √εeff (εr − 1)) dB/m ; αc = 8.686 · Rs / (Z0 w)

FR-4 (Df 0.020): the dielectric alone costs 0.174 dB/cm, which is 1.74 dB over 10 cm at any thickness. It fails. RO4350B (Df 0.0037) gives 0.030 dB/cm dielectric. At h = 0.51 mm (20 mil) the 50 Ω width is 1.13 mm and the copper loss is 0.030 dB/cm, so the total is 0.60 dB over 10 cm. A 0.76 mm board would lose even less, but its 1.68 mm trace won't fit the SMA. Real copper is rough and adds loss, so keep the margin.
