Design challenge:

You're laying out a small 2.4 GHz telemetry board for a drone or model rocket on cheap 2-layer FR-4 (microstrip, εeff ≈ 3.4). The fab won't promise controlled impedance, so you'd like to keep RF traces short enough to count as plain wires. The radio's RF pin runs 4 mm to its matching network, then 12 mm on to the chip antenna.

  • Frequency 2.45 GHz, FR-4 microstrip (εeff 3.4).
  • A trace counts as a plain wire only if it is shorter than λ/10.
  • Find the longest uncontrolled trace you may use, rounded down to a whole millimetre.

Produce: which of the two traces must be designed as a 50 Ω line, the longest allowed uncontrolled length, and one sentence on what you'd change in the layout. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

Find λ on the board first: the board slows the wave by √εeff, so λ is shorter than in air. Then put each trace length on the slider and read the verdict.

Check your design

Pass if: the widget is at about 2.45 GHz on FR-4 microstrip and the trace slider sits at the longest whole-millimetre length that still shows ✅.

Worked answer.

λ = c / (f √εeff) = 3×10⁸ / (2.45×10⁹ × √3.4) = 66.4 mm → λ/10 = 6.64 mm

So the longest plain-wire trace is 6 mm. The 4 mm run is 0.060 λ: a plain wire is fine. The 12 mm run is 0.181 λ: it must be a 50 Ω line. Either move the antenna so its feed is within 6 mm of the matching network, or pay for a controlled-impedance stackup (see the microstrip widget in Module 1). For comparison, at 915 MHz λ/10 is 17.8 mm, so the same 12 mm trace would be fine there.

Other answers can be valid if they meet every requirement.

Design challenge:

A 433 MHz beacon board (70 cm band). Heavier copper costs more: ½ oz is cheapest, 2 oz the most expensive. A teammate says switching the RF trace to 2 oz copper will cut its loss in half.

  • Design at 433 MHz, the lowest frequency on the board.
  • Copper at least 5 skin depths thick, so the current density has fallen below 1% (e−5 = 0.7%) inside the metal.
  • Choose the cheapest copper weight that meets it.

Produce: the copper weight, the skin depth δ, the ratio t/δ, and one sentence answering your teammate. Check it in the widget above.

Hint

δ shrinks like 1/√f. Compare 5δ at 433 MHz with 17.5, 35 and 70 µm. Then ask yourself: once the copper is many δ thick, does adding more copper add any more surface for the current to flow on?

Check your design

Pass if: the frequency is near 433 MHz (420–450 MHz), t ≥ 5δ, and no thinner copper option would also pass.

Worked answer.

δ = √(2ρ / (ωμ₀)) = √(2 × 1.68×10⁻⁸ / (2π × 433×10⁶ × 4π×10⁻⁷)) = 3.13 µm → 5δ = 15.7 µm

½ oz copper is 17.5 µm, so t/δ = 5.6 and ½ oz passes. It meets 5δ at every frequency above about 347 MHz. Your teammate is wrong: once t ≫ δ, the RF resistance per square is about ρ/δ = 5.4 mΩ whatever the copper weight. Thicker copper only lowers the DC resistance. If you want less loss, pay for smooth copper and a low-loss surface finish, because the current lives in the top few micrometres.
