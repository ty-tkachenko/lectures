Power systems

Every electronic system starts with energy from somewhere, usually a battery, and has to deliver it to many loads at the voltages they need, efficiently, safely, and without interruption. This module follows that energy: from a lithium cell and its limits, through linear and switching regulators, to protection against faults, the budget that sizes the battery, the switchover from an external supply, and the order in which the rails come up.

Builds on: EE modules 1–6, especially internal resistance (Module 1), inductors (Module 3), diodes (Module 5) and MOSFETs (Module 6). Bench: bench supply, electronic load if available, oscilloscope, a buck converter module, a linear regulator.

From battery to loads: a power tree

A system's power architecture is usually drawn as a power tree: the source at the root, then protection, then the converters that make each supply rail, then the loads on each rail. Drawing it first makes the questions of this module concrete: how much current each branch draws, how much is lost converting it, what happens if a branch shorts, and in what order the branches come alive.

Lithium polymer cells

A lithium-ion or lithium-polymer (LiPo) cell stores energy chemically and delivers it at a voltage set by its chemistry: 4.2 V when fully charged, about 3.7 V for most of its discharge, falling steeply below about 3.3 V as it empties. Cells are combined into packs: cells in series add voltage, cells in parallel add capacity. A "3S2P" pack is three groups in series, each of two cells in parallel: 11.1 V nominal, twice one cell's capacity.

DiagramInside a 3S LiPo pack

Two numbers describe how much a pack can deliver:

  • Capacity, in milliamp-hours: a 1500 mAh pack can deliver 1.5 A for one hour, or 150 mA for ten. In practice the usable capacity is less at high current and at low temperature.
  • C-rating: the maximum continuous discharge current as a multiple of the capacity. 1C for a 1500 mAh pack is 1.5 A; a "25C" rating claims 37.5 A. Manufacturers' C-ratings are often optimistic, and what limits a pack in practice is sag and heating.

Module 1 introduced internal resistance: a real cell behaves like an ideal voltage source in series with a small resistance, so its terminal voltage sags under load by I × Rint. For a small LiPo that's 10–50 mΩ per cell. Sag matters twice: the load sees less voltage (a regulator may drop out, or a brown-out reset may trigger), and the power I²Rint heats the cell.

Handling LiPo cells safely

A lithium cell stores a lot of energy in a flammable package. Abuse causes internal shorts, heating, and in the worst case thermal runaway, a fire that sustains itself. The rules follow from what damages the chemistry:

RuleWhy
Never charge above 4.2 V per cell; always use a balance chargerOvercharging plates lithium metal inside the cell, a direct route to internal shorts. A balance charger stops each cell at 4.2 V, even if the cells have drifted apart.
Never discharge below about 3.0 V per cell (under load, 3.3 V is a common cut-off)Deep discharge damages the electrodes and makes the cell unsafe to recharge.
Don't puncture, crush or bend packs; retire swollen ("puffy") onesPhysical damage shorts the layers inside; swelling means gas has formed from degradation.
Charge on a non-flammable surface or in a LiPo bag, attendedIf a cell does fail, it's contained and someone is there.
Store at about 3.8 V per cell, coolFull cells degrade faster in storage, especially when warm.
Insulate the leads; never let the main or balance leads shortA short draws hundreds of amps, limited only by the internal resistance (Module 1).
Design exerciseA reset whenever the valves open

A board's 3.3 V regulator runs from a 2S LiPo (6.0–8.4 V). It works on the bench supply, but on the battery, the microcontroller resets whenever two valves open together, drawing 4 A, late in a test when the battery is partly discharged.

What's the most likely cause?

  1. The valves generate noise that resets the microcontroller.
    Possible, but then it would also happen on the bench supply and with a full battery.
  2. Sag: 4 A through the pack's internal resistance and the wiring drops the battery's terminal voltage, which is already lower late in the test, below what the regulator chain needs, triggering a brown-out reset.
    The dependence on load current and state of charge, and not on the supply type, points to sag.
  3. The battery's capacity is too small.
    Capacity sets run time; this happens instantly, when the current steps up.
  4. The valves' flyback diodes are reversed.
    A reversed flyback diode would short the supply every time a valve opens, on the bench supply too.

A 2S pack with 40 mΩ per cell plus 50 mΩ of wiring and connectors drops 4 × 0.13 ≈ 0.5 V at 4 A. Late in the test the cells sit around 3.5 V each, and the regulator input dips toward 6 V and below, while a bench supply holds its voltage regardless. Fixes include a pack with lower internal resistance, shorter and thicker wiring, a bulk capacitor near the valves, a regulator with lower dropout or a buck-boost, and a brown-out threshold chosen with this in mind.

Linear regulators and dropout

A linear regulator makes a lower, steady voltage from a higher, varying one. Inside is a transistor in series between input and output (the pass element), and an amplifier that compares the output with a reference and adjusts the transistor continuously. In effect it's a variable resistor that adjusts itself to drop exactly the difference between input and output.

That description gives its efficiency from first principles. The same current flows in and out (plus a small quiescent current for the regulator itself), so the input power is Vin × I and the output power Vout × I. Everything else becomes heat in the pass element:

$$P_\text{heat} = (V_\text{in} - V_\text{out})\,I \qquad \eta = \frac{V_\text{out}}{V_\text{in}}$$
a linear regulator's efficiency is just the voltage ratio, whatever the part

The pass element needs some minimum voltage across it to work: the dropout voltage. Below Vout + Vdropout at the input, the regulator can no longer regulate and the output follows the input down. Older regulators drop out at about 2 V; low-dropout (LDO) regulators at 0.1–0.5 V.

Linear regulators aren't obsolete: they're simple, cheap, small and, unlike switching regulators, generate no switching noise, which makes them the right choice for a small drop or a small current, and for supplying noise-sensitive circuits such as ADC references and radio front ends. A common pattern is a switching regulator to get close to the target efficiently, followed by an LDO to clean up the result.

Buck and boost converters from first principles

A linear regulator wastes the voltage it drops because it carries the full current through a resistance. A switching converter avoids that by never operating as a resistor: its switch is either fully on (no voltage across it) or fully off (no current through it), so ideally it dissipates nothing. The energy is shuttled in packets through an inductor, which stores it during one part of each cycle and releases it during the other. Everything about a buck converter follows from one fact about inductors.

The key fact: an inductor's average voltage is zero

From Module 3, v = L di/dt. If a circuit is in steady state, the inductor's current at the start of each switching cycle is the same as at the end. Integrating over one cycle, ∫v dt = L × (change in current) = 0. So in steady state, the average voltage across an inductor is zero: the volt-seconds applied in one direction during part of the cycle must be cancelled by the volt-seconds in the other direction during the rest. This is called volt-second balance.

DiagramA buck converter in its two switch states

Apply volt-second balance to the buck converter. While the switch is on, for a time DT, the inductor's left end is at Vin and its right end at Vout: it sees Vin − Vout. While the switch is off, for (1 − D)T, the inductor's current carries on through the diode, its left end is at ground, and it sees −Vout. Balancing the two:

$$(V_\text{in} - V_\text{out})\,DT = V_\text{out}\,(1 - D)\,T \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_\text{out} = D\,V_\text{in}$$
the buck converter: the output is the input times the fraction of time the switch is on

No component values appear in the result. The output voltage is set by timing alone, which is why a controller only needs to adjust the duty cycle to regulate. The same reasoning gives the inductor's ripple current: during the on-time the current rises at (Vin − Vout)/L, so it rises by

$$\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_\text{in} - V_\text{out})\,D}{L\,f}$$
f = 1/T, the switching frequency; a larger inductor or a faster switch gives less ripple

The average inductor current equals the load current, since the capacitor's average current is zero by the same argument as the inductor's voltage. Energy is conserved too: ideally VinIin = VoutIout, so a buck converter stepping 12 V down to 3 V at 2 A draws only 0.5 A from the input, where a linear regulator would draw the full 2 A.

The boost converter

Rearrange the same three parts and the output rises above the input. In a boost converter the inductor connects to the input, the switch goes from the inductor to ground, and the diode leads to the output. With the switch on, the inductor sees Vin and its current builds up; with it off, the inductor's current is forced through the diode into the output, and the inductor sees Vin − Vout, which is negative. Volt-second balance again:

$$V_\text{in}\,DT = (V_\text{out} - V_\text{in})(1 - D)T \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_\text{out} = \frac{V_\text{in}}{1 - D}$$
DiagramThe boost converter

Combining the two gives a buck-boost converter, which can produce a fixed output from an input that may be above or below it: useful for making 3.3 V from a single lithium cell over its whole 3.0–4.2 V range. Modern converters usually replace the diode with a second MOSFET switched in step (a synchronous converter), saving the diode's drop. Typical efficiencies are 85–95 %.

Application

The inductor's peak current, not its average, decides whether it saturates (Module 3), so an inductor for a converter is chosen with a saturation current above the load current plus half the ripple, with margin. And the switching node is a fast, large voltage step: the loop from the input capacitor through the switch and diode back to ground should be as small as possible on the PCB, because that loop carries pulses of current with very fast edges and is the main source of a switching regulator's noise (Module 4's high harmonics in practice).

Design exerciseWhy not run at a very low duty cycle?

A buck converter must make 1 V from 24 V, which needs D ≈ 4 %. At 2 MHz, the switch would be on for 21 ns per cycle.

Why is this difficult?

  1. The formula Vout = DVin doesn't hold at low duty cycle.
    Volt-second balance holds at any duty cycle; the problem is practical.
  2. 21 ns is comparable to the switch's own transition time and the controller's minimum on-time, so the converter can't produce such short pulses accurately, and the switching losses (which happen every cycle) become large compared with the energy delivered. Use a lower frequency, or two stages (24 V → 5 V → 1 V).
    Timing and switching loss, not the ideal equation, limit extreme ratios.
  3. The inductor would saturate.
    That depends on the current and ripple, which a suitable inductor handles.
  4. Buck converters can only halve the voltage.
    Any ratio below one is possible in principle.

Every controller has a minimum on-time, typically 50–150 ns, and every transition costs energy (Module 6). At a 4 % duty cycle the switch spends much of its on-time in transition. Datasheets state the minimum on-time; the usual solutions are a lower switching frequency, or two conversion stages so that each operates at a moderate duty cycle.

Efficiency and heat

Every watt lost in conversion has two costs: battery capacity that doesn't reach the loads, and heat that has to leave the board. The choice between linear and switching regulators usually comes down to a quick calculation of both:

CaseLinear regulatorBuck converter at 90 %Choice
3.3 V at 50 mA from 5 Vη = 66 %, 85 mW of heat18 mW of heatLinear: simpler, quieter, heat is trivial
3.3 V at 500 mA from 3S (12.6 V)η = 26 %, 4.7 W of heat0.18 W of heatBuck: a linear part would need a heatsink and waste three-quarters of the battery
3.3 V at 200 mA from one cell (3.6 V)η = 92 %, 60 mW of heat73 mW of heatLinear (LDO): the drop is small, so it's as efficient as a converter
Analog sensor supply, 5 V at 20 mAClean outputSwitching ripple and noiseLinear, or a buck followed by an LDO

The third row is worth remembering: a linear regulator's efficiency is Vout/Vin, so when the drop is small it's as efficient as any converter, with none of the noise. Switching converters, for their part, are least efficient at very light loads, where their fixed losses (driving the switch, the controller's own current) are large compared with the power delivered; many have a light-load mode that switches in bursts to reduce this.

Power budgets

A power budget lists every load with its current, its supply rail and how much of the time it's drawing that current. It answers three questions: the average current, which with the mission duration sizes the battery; the peak current, which sizes the battery's C-rating, the wiring and the protection, and decides whether sag is a problem; and how much the converters lose along the way. Loads are referred back to the battery through the converters: a 5 V load drawing 1 A through a 90 %-efficient buck from 11.1 V draws 5/(11.1 × 0.9) = 0.5 A from the battery.

Fuses, eFuses and current limiting

A lithium pack shorted through a few tens of milliohms of internal resistance and wiring can deliver a hundred amps or more (Module 1). Something must interrupt a fault before that current melts a trace, burns a wire's insulation or damages the battery. Three kinds of device do it, at very different speeds:

  • A fuse is a wire designed to melt. It's characterised by its rating (the current it carries indefinitely) and its I²t, the heating energy needed to melt it. At a large fault current it opens in milliseconds; at a modest overload it can take seconds or minutes. "Fast" and "slow-blow" fuses trade speed against tolerance of brief surges such as inrush (Module 3). A blown fuse must be replaced.
  • A resettable fuse (PTC) is a polymer whose resistance rises sharply when it heats, limiting the current to a small value until the fault is removed and it cools. It's slow (tens of milliseconds to seconds) and has some resistance in normal use, but resets itself.
  • An eFuse or load switch with current limit is a MOSFET with a controller that measures the current and limits it within microseconds, then turns off if the fault persists. It can also control inrush by turning on slowly, block reverse current, and report faults to the microcontroller. It's the most capable and the most expensive.

Design exerciseA fuse that blows at switch-on

A board protected by a 2 A fast fuse draws 1.2 A in normal operation, but the fuse sometimes blows the instant the battery is connected.

What's happening?

  1. The board has a short circuit.
    With a short it would blow every time and the board would never work.
  2. Inrush: the board's input capacitors are empty at connection and draw a large current spike (Module 3); its I²t occasionally exceeds the fast fuse's melting I²t. A slow-blow fuse, an inrush limiter or an eFuse with soft start fixes it.
    The fault is intermittent and happens only at connection, the signature of inrush.
  3. The fuse is rated too high.
    A higher rating would blow less often, not more.
  4. The battery voltage is too high.
    Voltage alone doesn't blow a fuse; current does.

Hundreds of microfarads of input capacitance connected to a low-resistance battery draw a spike of tens of amps for a few hundred microseconds. Whether that melts a fast fuse depends on the exact resistance and the moment of contact, so it happens sometimes. The fuse's datasheet gives its melting I²t; compare it with the inrush pulse's I²t, and either choose a slower fuse or limit the inrush.

Umbilical-to-internal power switchover

A vehicle waiting on the pad is often powered through an umbilical cable from ground equipment, saving its internal battery for flight, and the umbilical disconnects at or just before launch. The power system must hand over to the internal battery at that moment without the bus dropping far enough to reset anything. Three common approaches:

  • Diode-OR (Module 5): the umbilical supply is set a little above the battery, so it carries the load while connected. At disconnection the battery's diode simply starts conducting: no gap at all, at the cost of a diode drop and its heat. An ideal-diode controller does the same with a MOSFET and almost no loss.
  • Relay switchover: a relay held by the umbilical's supply selects the source. When the umbilical drops, the relay takes a few milliseconds to release and transfer, and a hold-up capacitor must carry the load through the gap: ΔV = I × tgap/C (Module 3).
  • Power-path controllers combine priority selection, ideal diodes and often battery charging in one chip, so the battery is topped up from the umbilical while waiting.

Power sequencing

When power is applied, the rails don't all appear at once: each converter takes some time to start, and they start in an order set by the design. That order matters for three reasons:

  • Some chips require one supply before another (a processor's core before its input/output supply, for example), and are damaged or latch up if powered in the wrong order. Their datasheets state the requirement.
  • A signal driven into a chip that isn't powered yet flows through its input protection diodes into its supply rail (Module 5), partially powering it in an undefined state.
  • The microcontroller must not start running, and drive outputs, until its supply is stable: a reset supervisor or the microcontroller's internal brown-out reset holds it in reset until the voltage is high enough, and resets it if the voltage later sags (the valve exercise above).

Converters provide two signals for this: an enable input that starts them, and a power-good output that goes high when their output is in regulation. Chaining each converter's power-good to the next one's enable sequences the rails in a defined order.

Design exerciseA sensor that powers the microcontroller

A sensor board is powered directly from the battery and starts sending data on a UART line immediately. The microcontroller's 3.3 V rail comes up 50 ms later. Occasionally the microcontroller starts in a strange state or doesn't start at all.

What's the likely mechanism?

  1. The UART data is corrupted.
    Lost data at start-up wouldn't stop the microcontroller starting.
  2. The sensor's 3.3 V UART signal drives current through the unpowered microcontroller's input protection diodes into its 3.3 V rail, partly powering it before its own supply starts, so its reset and power-up circuits see a rail that rises in an undefined way.
    "Back-powering" through I/O protection diodes is a classic sequencing failure.
  3. The battery voltage is too low.
    The sensor works, and the problem is tied to start-up order.
  4. The microcontroller's crystal starts slowly.
    A slow crystal delays start-up but doesn't cause an undefined state.

An I/O pin's protection diode to VDD conducts whenever the pin is driven above the chip's supply. With the supply off, a driven pin can lift the whole rail by a volt or two through that diode, leaving the chip partly powered and its brown-out circuit confused. Fixes: power the sensor from a rail that comes up with or after the microcontroller's, add series resistors on the signal lines to limit the current, or use buffers that isolate their outputs when unpowered.

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●○○C-rating and sag

A 3S, 1500 mAh, 25C pack has an internal resistance of 25 mΩ per cell.

(a) What continuous current does its C-rating claim?

25 × 1.5 A = 37.5 A.

(b) How much does its voltage sag at 20 A?

That's one cell. The three cells' resistances are in series: 75 mΩ.

20 A × 3 × 0.025 Ω = 1.5 V, and the pack dissipates 20² × 0.075 = 30 W internally.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●○○A linear regulator's heat

An LDO makes 3.3 V at 200 mA from a fully charged 2S pack (8.4 V). Its package has θJA = 100 K/W, and the ambient is 40 °C.

(a) How much power does it dissipate?

(8.4 − 3.3) × 0.2 = 1.02 W. Efficiency is 3.3/8.4 = 39 %.

(b) What's its junction temperature?

40 + 1.02 × 100 = 142 °C: too hot, and many LDOs would shut down thermally. A buck converter (or a buck followed by an LDO) is the answer.

Exercise 3Design · ●●○A buck converter's duty cycle and ripple

A buck converter makes 5 V from 12 V, switching at 500 kHz with a 10 µH inductor.

(a) What duty cycle does it run at (ideal components)?

D = 5/12 = 0.417.

(b) What's the inductor's peak-to-peak ripple current?

ΔI = (Vin − Vout)D/(Lf).

ΔI = 7 × 0.417/(10×10⁻⁶ × 500×10³) = 2.92/5 = 0.583 A.

Discussion: choosing the inductor

A common target is a ripple of 20–40 % of the full load current, which balances inductor size against output ripple and the peak current the switch and inductor must carry. At 2 A load, 0.58 A is 29 %, and the inductor must be rated for at least 2 + 0.29 = 2.3 A peak without saturating.

Exercise 4Design · ●●○Sizing a hold-up capacitor

A relay switchover leaves the bus unsupplied for 5 ms while the load draws 0.5 A. The bus can fall by at most 3 V before the downstream converter drops out.

What hold-up capacitance is needed, at minimum?

C = I × t / ΔV.

C = 0.5 × 0.005/3 = 833 µF. Choose 1000 µF or more, allowing for capacitor tolerance, ageing, and cold temperatures, which reduce an electrolytic's capacitance.

Discussion: or avoid the gap

An ideal-diode OR removes the gap altogether, at the cost of an extra controller IC. Hold-up capacitors are still worth having for brief interruptions, such as connector bounce under vibration.

  2. Linear regulator. Load the LDO at 200 mA and step its input from 5 V to 12 V. Measure the input current and feel (carefully) or measure the temperature.

  4. Switchover. Build a Schottky diode-OR between the bench supply and the battery, load it, disconnect the bench supply, and capture the bus voltage.
What you should see: tens of milliohms per cell; an LDO input current that doesn't change with input voltage while its temperature climbs; a switch-node duty cycle close to Vout/Vin and efficiencies around 85–95 %; and a switchover with no gap, the bus stepping down by the difference between the two sources.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A LiPo cell spans 4.2 V to about 3.0 V. Capacity sets run time; the C-rating and internal resistance set peak current and sag. Follow the handling rules.
  2. A linear regulator's efficiency is Vout/Vin, and it needs at least its dropout voltage across it. Good for small drops, small currents and quiet supplies.
  3. Volt-second balance on the inductor gives the buck converter, Vout = DVin, and the boost, Vout = Vin/(1 − D). Ripple falls with larger L and higher f.
  4. A power budget gives average current (battery capacity), peak current (C-rating, wiring, protection) and conversion losses.
  5. Fuses are cheap and slow; PTCs reset; eFuses limit in microseconds, control inrush and report faults.
  6. Umbilical switchover: diode-OR or ideal diode for no gap; a relay needs a hold-up capacitor sized by C = I t/ΔV.
  7. Sequence rails with enable and power-good; hold the microcontroller in reset until its supply is good, and keep outputs off by default.
