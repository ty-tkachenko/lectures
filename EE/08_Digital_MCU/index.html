Digital logic & microcontrollers

A microcontroller reads the world as ones and zeros and acts on it through its pins. Underneath, those ones and zeros are analog voltages with thresholds, margins and timing, and most digital hardware problems are analog problems in disguise: a floating input that reads random values, a switch that bounces, an output that can't drive what it's connected to, two chips that disagree on what "high" means. This module covers the electrical side of a microcontroller's pins, and the hardware features (timers, interrupts, watchdogs, brown-out detection) that make firmware reliable.

Builds on: EE modules 1–7, especially RC time constants (Module 3) and transistors as switches (Module 6). Bench: a microcontroller development board, oscilloscope, a push-button, resistors, and a logic analyzer if available.

Logic levels and noise margins

A digital signal is a voltage that's meant to be either near the supply (a 1, high) or near ground (a 0, low). Real voltages are never exactly at either, so every logic family specifies four guaranteed limits:

  • VOH(min): the lowest voltage an output produces when driving high.
  • VOL(max): the highest voltage an output produces when driving low.
  • VIH(min): the lowest voltage an input is guaranteed to read as high.
  • VIL(max): the highest voltage an input is guaranteed to read as low.

Between VIL and VIH is a forbidden zone where the input may read either value. The gaps between what an output guarantees and what an input needs are the noise margins: how much noise, ground shift or voltage drop the signal can pick up on its way and still be read correctly.

$$\text{NM}_H = V_{OH(\min)} - V_{IH(\min)} \qquad \text{NM}_L = V_{IL(\max)} - V_{OL(\max)}$$

CMOS inputs and floating pins

A CMOS input is the gates of two MOSFETs, a P-channel to the supply and an N-channel to ground, connected together (Module 6). Like any MOSFET gate, it's a small capacitor, a few picofarads, with almost no leakage: its input resistance is in the gigaohms. Protection diodes from the pin to the supply and to ground clamp static discharge and overvoltages.

DiagramInside a CMOS input pin

Three consequences follow, each a common source of trouble:

  • An unconnected input floats. With nothing driving it, its voltage is set by leakage and by whatever couples to it capacitively: nearby signals, a hand near the board, mains hum. It drifts and wanders across the threshold, and the firmware reads random values.
  • An input held near mid-supply wastes power. Both transistors partly conduct, so current flows straight from supply to ground through them; a floating input can raise a sleeping microcontroller's current from microamps to milliamps.
  • An input driven above the supply, or driven while the chip is unpowered, conducts through the protection diode into the supply rail: the back-powering problem of Module 7.

Unused pins should therefore be configured as outputs, or as inputs with the internal pull-up or pull-down enabled, never left as floating inputs.

Pull-ups, pull-downs and switch bounce

A pull-up resistor connects an input to the supply, so it reads high whenever nothing else drives it; a pull-down does the same to ground. A push-button between the pin and ground, with a pull-up, then reads high when released and low when pressed. Most microcontrollers have built-in pull-ups of 20–50 kΩ that firmware can enable.

The value is a trade-off, and both sides come from earlier modules. A small resistor wastes current whenever the button is pressed (I = V/R) and is harder for an open-drain output to overcome. A large resistor is weak: with the pin's capacitance and the wiring's, it forms an RC time constant that slows the rising edge (Module 3), and it's more easily overcome by noise coupled onto a long wire.

Switch bounce and debouncing

Mechanical contacts don't close cleanly: they bounce several times over a millisecond or more before settling. A microcontroller sampling every microsecond sees each bounce as a separate press. Two cures, often used together:

  • Hardware: an RC low-pass filter on the input (Module 4), with a time constant longer than the bounce, ideally into a Schmitt-trigger input whose hysteresis prevents a slow edge from being read as several transitions.
  • Firmware: accept a change only when the input has stayed in its new state for some time, typically 10–50 ms.
Design exerciseA sleeping board that draws too much

A board's microcontroller is specified to draw 5 µA in sleep, but the whole board draws 1.2 mA. Several unused pins are configured as inputs with nothing connected.

What's the likely cause?

  1. The datasheet is wrong.
    Sleep currents are usually measured carefully; the board's configuration is the suspect.
  2. The floating inputs drift to mid-supply, where both transistors in each input partly conduct, passing current from supply to ground. Configuring unused pins as outputs, or enabling their pull-ups or pull-downs, removes it.
    A CMOS input held near the threshold draws "shoot-through" current.
  3. The pull-up resistors on the used pins draw current.
    A pull-up only draws current when its line is held low.
  4. The board's capacitors leak.
    Ceramic capacitor leakage is far below a milliamp.

Each floating CMOS input settles somewhere near mid-supply, where its P- and N-channel transistors are both partly on, passing tens to hundreds of microamps each. Defining every pin's state is part of every low-power design.

Push-pull and open-drain outputs

A push-pull output has two transistors: one connects the pin to the supply to drive it high, the other to ground to drive it low. It drives actively in both directions, with fast edges. An open-drain output (open-collector for BJTs) has only the transistor to ground: it can pull the pin low, and when "high" it simply lets go, relying on a pull-up resistor to raise the line.

DiagramPush-pull and open-drain outputs

Open-drain looks like a weaker version of push-pull, but it has three uses push-pull can't match:

  • Sharing a line. Several open-drain outputs can connect to one line: any of them can pull it low, and none can fight another. Two push-pull outputs driving opposite levels would short the supply through their transistors. I²C (Module 9), interrupt lines and reset lines are open-drain for this reason.
  • Translating levels. The pull-up can go to a different supply voltage from the chip's own, within the pin's rating, so a 3.3 V chip can signal to a 5 V input.
  • Driving loads. An open-drain pin sinking current from a higher supply is the same circuit as Module 6's low-side switch, in miniature.

The cost is speed: the falling edge is driven hard, but the rising edge is only the pull-up charging the line's capacitance, the same RC curve as the button figure above.

Design exerciseTwo outputs on one reset line

A board has a reset line that a supervisor chip and a debug connector should both be able to pull low. Both drive it with push-pull outputs.

What's wrong?

  1. Nothing: the line goes low whenever either drives it low.
    When one drives low and the other drives high, they fight.
  2. When one output drives high while the other drives low, the two transistors short the supply to ground through the line: a large current, an undefined voltage, and possible damage. The line should be open-drain with a single pull-up.
    Push-pull outputs must never share a line.
  3. Reset lines must be active-high.
    The polarity isn't the problem; the output type is.
  4. It needs a pull-down resistor.
    A resistor doesn't stop two active outputs fighting.

Shared lines use open-drain outputs and one pull-up: each device can only pull low, so there's never a conflict, and the line is low if any device asserts it (a "wired-AND" of the high states). Most reset and interrupt lines are designed this way.

Timers and PWM

A timer is a hardware counter that increments on every tick of a clock, independently of the firmware. Its clock is the microcontroller's clock divided by a prescaler, and it counts up to a period value, then restarts. That simple structure does a lot: periodic interrupts for a control loop, timestamps for events, measuring pulse widths, and generating PWM.

For PWM (pulse-width modulation), the timer compares its count with a compare value: the output is high while the count is below it and low afterwards. The period value sets the frequency, and the compare value the duty cycle, with no firmware involvement once configured:

$$f_\text{PWM} = \frac{f_\text{clk}}{\text{prescaler} \times (\text{period} + 1)} \qquad D = \frac{\text{compare}}{\text{period} + 1} \qquad \text{resolution} = \log_2(\text{period} + 1)\ \text{bits}$$

Application

A common use of PWM is reducing a solenoid valve's power once it has opened. The coil needs its full current to pull the plunger in, but much less to hold it. Driving the valve's MOSFET (Module 6) at full duty for the first 50 ms and then at 30–40 % PWM at 20 kHz, above the audible range, cuts the coil's heating several-fold, while the coil's inductance smooths the current. The flyback diode carries the current during each off-time.

Interrupts

A microcontroller can watch for events in two ways. Polling means the main loop checks a flag or a pin each time round; simple, but the response time is a whole loop iteration and the loop has to keep checking. An interrupt lets the hardware stop the main program as soon as an event happens, run a short interrupt service routine (ISR), and return to where it left off.

DiagramAn interrupt arriving during the main loop

Three rules keep interrupts from causing more problems than they solve:

  • Keep ISRs short. While one runs, other events wait. Record what happened (a flag, a timestamp, a byte) and do the processing in the main loop.
  • Protect shared data. A variable that both the ISR and the main loop use can be changed halfway through the main loop reading it, for example between the two halves of a 32-bit value on an 8-bit processor. Mark it volatile, and briefly disable interrupts when reading or writing it as a whole.
  • Don't interrupt on a bouncing input without debouncing, or each bounce triggers the ISR.
Design exerciseA timestamp that's occasionally wildly wrong

An ISR updates a 32-bit millisecond counter. The main loop on an 8-bit microcontroller occasionally reads a value that's off by 256 or 65 536, but only once in thousands of reads.

Why?

  1. The timer is inaccurate.
    A timing error wouldn't produce jumps of exactly 256 or 65 536.
  2. The 8-bit processor reads the 32-bit value one byte at a time. If the ISR increments it between two of those byte reads, and the increment carries into the next byte, the main loop combines bytes from before and after the change.
    Jumps by powers of 256 are the signature of a torn multi-byte read.
  3. The variable isn't declared as an integer.
    The type is fine; the access isn't atomic.
  4. The ISR runs too slowly.
    The ISR's speed doesn't matter; what matters is that it can run in the middle of the read.

Disable interrupts while copying the counter into a local variable, then re-enable them, or read it twice and repeat until both reads agree. Declaring it volatile is also necessary, so the compiler reads it from memory every time, but on its own doesn't make the read atomic.

Watchdogs and brown-out resets

Embedded systems have to recover by themselves from two kinds of fault: firmware that stops making progress, and a supply that sags.

A watchdog timer is a hardware timer, running from its own clock, that resets the microcontroller if it ever counts to the end. Healthy firmware "kicks" (restarts) it regularly, so it never expires. If the firmware hangs, in an infinite loop, a deadlock, or waiting forever for a peripheral that has stopped responding, the kicks stop, the watchdog expires, and the system restarts in a known state.

A brown-out reset (BOR) circuit watches the supply. Below a threshold, the processor's logic and memory can't be trusted to work correctly; rather than run unpredictably, the BOR holds the microcontroller in reset until the supply recovers, with some hysteresis so it doesn't chatter. Module 7's valve example shows the classic trigger: a battery sagging under a heavy load.

DiagramA watchdog catching a hang, and a brown-out reset

A watchdog only helps if it's kicked from the right place: kicking it from a timer interrupt keeps it happy even while the main loop is stuck. Good practice is to kick it once per main loop iteration, after confirming that each important task has run. And after any reset, the firmware should check the reset cause register and record whether it was a watchdog, a brown-out or a power-up, which is invaluable when diagnosing a failure afterwards.

Design exerciseA watchdog that never fires

Firmware kicks the watchdog from a 1 ms timer interrupt. During a test, the main loop locks up waiting for a sensor that has stopped responding, and the board sits frozen for minutes without resetting.

Why didn't the watchdog help?

  1. The watchdog's timeout was too long.
    It would never have expired: something kept kicking it.
  2. The timer interrupt keeps running and kicking the watchdog even though the main loop is stuck, so the watchdog sees a healthy system. Kick it from the main loop, after checking that each task has made progress.
    The watchdog only knows what the code that kicks it knows.
  3. Watchdogs don't work while waiting for a peripheral.
    A watchdog runs independently of the processor's activity.
  4. The sensor's failure disabled the watchdog.
    A peripheral can't disable the watchdog.

Interrupts run regardless of what the main loop is doing, so a kick from an interrupt proves only that interrupts work. The kick belongs in the main loop, ideally conditioned on each task having reported progress since the last kick. Waits for peripherals should also have timeouts of their own.

Level shifting

Many systems mix logic voltages: a 3.3 V microcontroller, a 5 V sensor or display, a 1.8 V radio or memory. The logic-level figure above shows when a direct connection works; when it doesn't, one of these is used:

DiagramThree ways to connect different logic voltages
  • A resistor divider scales a higher-voltage output down for a lower-voltage input (Module 2's divider). It's one-directional, and the resistors and the input's capacitance slow the edges, so it suits slow signals such as a UART at modest speed.
  • A single N-channel MOSFET with pull-ups on each side shifts open-drain lines in both directions, and is the standard shifter for I²C (Module 9).
  • A translator IC, with a separate supply pin for each side, handles fast push-pull signals such as SPI. Some are direction-controlled, some auto-sensing; their datasheets state which kinds of signal they suit.

Some microcontroller pins are 5 V tolerant: their protection is designed so they can accept a 5 V input while running at 3.3 V. The datasheet's pin table says which; it's common for most pins but not all, and the feature usually only applies while the chip is powered.

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●○○Noise margins

A driver guarantees VOH(min) = 2.4 V and VOL(max) = 0.4 V. The receiver needs VIH(min) = 2.0 V and VIL(max) = 0.8 V.

(a) High noise margin?

2.4 − 2.0 = 0.4 V.

(b) Low noise margin?

0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4 V. These are the classic TTL levels, which is why a 3.3 V CMOS output (VOH near 3 V) can drive a TTL-compatible 5 V input.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○A pull-up's rise time

An input with a 10 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V has 50 pF of pin and wiring capacitance. It's read as high above 0.7 VDD.

(a) What's the time constant?

τ = RC = 10 000 × 50×10⁻¹² = 0.5 µs.

(b) How long after the line is released does it read high?

v = V(1 − e−t/τ) reaches 0.7V when e−t/τ = 0.3.

t = −τ ln 0.3 = 1.2τ = 0.60 µs.

Exercise 3Design · ●●○PWM for a valve

A timer runs from a 48 MHz clock with a prescaler of 1, generating 20 kHz PWM.

(a) What value goes in the period register?

48 000 000/20 000 = 2400 counts per period, so the register holds 2399 (counting 0 to 2399).

(b) What's the duty-cycle resolution?

log₂(2400) = 11.2 bits: steps of 1/2400 of the period, about 0.04 %.

Exercise 4Design · ●○○A divider for a 5 V signal

A 5 V output drives a 3.3 V input through a divider. The bottom resistor is 3.3 kΩ.

What top resistor gives 3.3 V from 5 V?

Vout = Vin × R2/(R1 + R2).

3.3 = 5 × 3.3/(R₁ + 3.3) gives R₁ + 3.3 = 5 kΩ, so R₁ = 1.7 kΩ; 1.8 kΩ (standard) gives 3.24 V.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Logic levels are guaranteed ranges; noise margins are the gaps between what an output guarantees and what an input needs.
  2. CMOS inputs are tiny capacitors: never leave one floating. Use pull-ups or pull-downs, or configure unused pins as outputs.
  3. A pull-up's value trades current against rise time (τ = RC). Debounce mechanical contacts in hardware, firmware or both.
  4. Push-pull drives both ways and must never share a line; open-drain only pulls low, shares lines safely, and rises slowly through its pull-up.
  5. Timers generate PWM in hardware: frequency from the period, duty from the compare value, resolution log₂(period + 1).
  6. Keep ISRs short and protect data shared with the main loop.
  7. A watchdog recovers from hangs only if kicked from the main loop; a brown-out reset holds the chip in reset while the supply is too low.
  8. Shift levels with a divider (slow, one-way), a MOSFET (open-drain, bidirectional) or a translator IC (fast).
