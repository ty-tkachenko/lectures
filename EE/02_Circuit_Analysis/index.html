Circuit analysis

Module 1 dealt with one battery and one resistor. Real boards have dozens of parts sharing supplies, grounds and signals. Two conservation laws and a handful of shortcuts let you work out what any of them is doing, and tell you why a battery monitor or a pressure reading can come out wrong.

Nodes, branches and loops

Circuit analysis needs three words, and it's worth getting them exactly right:

  • A node is a set of points connected by conductors: wires, PCB traces, copper pours, connector pins. Every point on a node is at the same voltage, because an ideal conductor drops no voltage. A node can be as small as one solder joint or as large as a board's whole ground plane (as long as you ignore the plane's resistance, which Section 3 won't).
  • A branch is a single path between two nodes: one component, or several in series. The same current flows all the way along a branch.
  • A loop is any closed path you can trace through branches, starting and ending at the same node.
DiagramTwo nodes, three branches, two loops

The whole top wire from R1 to R3 is node A, however long it's drawn. The whole bottom wire is node B. There's a third loop too, around the outside through the battery, R1 and R3. Counting nodes properly is the first step of every analysis, and the most common beginner error is treating two ends of the same conductor as different nodes.

Kirchhoff's current law

Module 1 said that current isn't used up. Applied to a node, that becomes Kirchhoff's current law (KCL): charge can't pile up on a wire, so the total current flowing into any node equals the total flowing out.

$$\sum I_\text{in} = \sum I_\text{out}$$
at every node, at every instant

The avionics power bus is the textbook example. The battery feeds one wire into the bus, and the flight computer, radio and GPS each take their share from it. KCL says the battery wire carries the sum. And every one of those currents has to get back to the battery, so the ground wire carries the sum too. That second fact is easy to forget, and it matters in the next section.

Worked example

In Module 1's power budget the flight computer drew 180 mA, the radio 120 mA and the GPS 30 mA. At the bus node:

\(I_\text{battery} = 180 + 120 + 30 = 330\ \text{mA}\)

If you measure 330 mA in the battery lead and only 300 mA in the flight computer and radio leads together, KCL tells you the missing 30 mA is going somewhere. That's a useful debugging tool: currents that don't add up mean there's a path you didn't know about, often a leaky or shorted part.

The pad-box bus is fed through a 5 A fuse. On it are the flight computer (180 mA), the radio while transmitting (600 mA), a camera (900 mA) and two valve drivers that each draw 1.5 A when open. Everything can be on at once.

How much current is left before the fuse's rating?

There are two valve drivers, and both can be open at once.

\(0.18 + 0.6 + 0.9 + 2 \times 1.5 = 4.68\ \text{A}\), leaving 0.32 A. That's far too close: fuses are typically run at no more than about 75 % of their rating, and valves draw more than their holding current for a moment as they open. Either a bigger fuse and wiring to match, or a separate fused feed for the valves.

Kirchhoff's voltage law

Go around any loop, adding up the voltage across every component on the way. You always get back to zero, because you're back at the same node you started from. That's Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL):

$$\sum_\text{loop} V = 0 \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad \text{sum of rises} = \text{sum of drops}$$
count a voltage as positive going − to + and negative going + to −; any consistent convention works

In plain terms, the battery's voltage gets shared out between everything in the loop, and every volt is accounted for. Module 1's igniter cable was KVL already: the 12 V was split between the outgoing wire, the igniter and the return wire.

Design exerciseFind the bad crimpoptional

A valve on the test stand opens sluggishly. Its supply is 12.0 V at the battery, and with the valve energised (2.0 A) you measure 10.9 V across the valve itself, 0.3 V along the feed wire and 0.3 V along the return wire. The only other thing in the loop is a connector.

What's the connector's resistance?

0.5 is the voltage across the connector. Ohm's law turns it into a resistance.

KVL first: the drops around the loop add up to 12.0 V. What's left for the connector?

KVL: \(12.0 - 10.9 - 0.3 - 0.3 = 0.5\ \text{V}\) across the connector. At 2.0 A that's \(0.5 / 2 = 0.25\ \Omega\). A good crimped contact is a few milliohms, so this one is a hundred times too high. It's also dissipating \(0.5 \times 2 = 1\ \text{W}\) inside a connector body, which is how connectors melt.

Series and parallel

Two shortcuts follow directly from Kirchhoff's laws, and you'll use them constantly.

In series, components share one branch, so they carry the same current (KCL), and their voltages add up (KVL). The resistances add:

$$R_\text{series} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$$

In parallel, components connect between the same two nodes, so they have the same voltage across them, and their currents add up. The conductances (1/R) add:

$$\frac{1}{R_\text{par}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots \qquad\text{for two:}\qquad R_\text{par} = R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$
"product over sum"; the ∥ symbol means "in parallel with"

Two quick checks catch most mistakes: series is always larger than the largest resistor, and parallel is always smaller than the smallest. Two equal resistors in parallel give exactly half.

In parallel the current splits, and more of it takes the easier path. That's the current divider:

$$I_1 = I\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \qquad I_2 = I\,\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}$$
note the "wrong" resistor on top: the current through R1 depends on R2

Design exerciseDual igniters on one cableoptional

For redundancy, two igniters are wired in parallel at the end of the launch cable. Each is 1.6 Ω with a 1.0 A all-fire current. The cable is Module 1's 30 m of 22 AWG twin (3.17 Ω round trip), and the firing box supplies 12 V. Ignore the battery's and relay's resistance.

How much current goes through each igniter?

That's the total current in the cable. It splits between the two igniters.

That's the current for a single igniter. With two in parallel, the load resistance halves.

Two equal resistors in parallel give half the resistance. Then it's one series loop: cable plus the parallel pair.

The pair is \(1.6 \parallel 1.6 = 0.8\ \Omega\). The loop is \(3.17 + 0.8 = 3.97\ \Omega\), so the cable carries \(12 / 3.97 = 3.02\ \text{A}\), which splits equally: 1.51 A each, a 1.5× margin. Compare a single igniter at 2.46 A: adding the second one roughly doubled the load, so the cable's share of the voltage went up and each igniter got less. Redundancy here costs margin.

The voltage divider

The flight computer should know its own battery voltage: to log it, to refuse to arm on a flat pack, and to warn the ground station during a long hold. But a 2S LiPo reaches 8.4 V and the microcontroller's ADC pin only accepts 0 to 3.3 V. Putting 8.4 V on that pin damages it.

The fix is two resistors in series across the battery. The same current flows through both, so the voltage splits in proportion to their resistances, and the junction between them sits at a fixed fraction of the input:

$$V_\text{out} = V_\text{in}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$
R1 from the input to the output, R2 from the output to ground

That fraction is the divider ratio. The firmware reads \(V_\text{out}\) and multiplies by \((R_1 + R_2)/R_2\) to get the battery voltage back.

Picking the ratio is easy. Picking the size of the resistors is the real design decision, because the divider is always connected, always drawing current from the battery:

  • Too small (a few kΩ in total) and the divider is a constant load. A few milliamps doesn't sound like much, but over a week on the shelf it flattens a small pack, and LiPos are damaged by being run flat.
  • Too large (megohms) and the divider becomes fragile: anything connected to its output disturbs it, which is the subject of the next section.

Tens of kilohms in total is the usual compromise for a battery monitor, often with a 100 nF capacitor from the output to ground to steady the voltage while the ADC samples it (Module 3 explains why that works).

Worked example

A 2S pack (8.4 V full) with R1 = 22 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ. The ratio is \(10 / 32 = 0.3125\), so at full charge the ADC sees \(8.4 \times 0.3125 = 2.63\ \text{V}\), comfortably below 3.3 V.

The divider draws \(8.4\ \text{V} / 32\ \text{k}\Omega = 0.26\ \text{mA}\). That's about 190 mAh a month, so a 2200 mAh pack left connected in a cupboard for a few months would be flat. Many flight computers switch the divider off with a small MOSFET when they aren't measuring (Module 6).

The flight computer uses the 22 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider above. During a pad hold the ADC reads 2.10 V.

What's the battery voltage?

That's 2.10 × 22/10. The scale factor is the total resistance over R2: (22 + 10)/10.

You multiplied by the ratio instead of dividing by it. The battery is bigger than what the ADC sees.

\(V_\text{bat} = 2.10 \times (22 + 10)/10 = 6.72\ \text{V}\), or 3.36 V per cell. That's close to empty: time to stop holding and swap the pack.

Loading

The divider equation assumes nothing is connected to the output. In reality something always is: the ADC pin, a multimeter, a data logger. Whatever you connect draws some current, and it sits in parallel with R2. That lowers the bottom half of the divider, so the output drops. This is called loading.

$$V_\text{out} = V_\text{in}\,\frac{R_2 \parallel R_L}{R_1 + R_2 \parallel R_L}$$
RL is the load: the input resistance of whatever reads the divider

If \(R_L\) is huge compared with R2, then \(R_2 \parallel R_L \approx R_2\) and nothing changes. You saw that in the series and parallel figure: a big resistor in parallel barely matters. The trouble starts when the load is within a factor of a hundred or so of the divider's resistors.

"The meter is lying"When a high-value divider reads low on your multimeter, the meter is reporting the voltage correctly. It's the circuit that changed when you connected the meter. Every measurement loads the thing it measures a little; the skill is knowing when "a little" is too much. Probing a megohm node with a 10 MΩ meter, or a 1 MΩ scope probe, is a classic way to chase a fault that isn't there.

Thévenin equivalents

Loading problems get much easier with one of the most useful results in circuit theory. Thévenin's theorem says that any circuit made of voltage sources, current sources and resistors, looked at from two terminals, behaves exactly like one voltage source \(V_\text{th}\) in series with one resistor \(R_\text{th}\). Whatever you connect to those terminals can't tell the difference.

You've already met one. Module 1's battery model, an ideal source with an internal resistance, is a Thévenin equivalent. Finding one takes two steps:

  1. \(V_\text{th}\) is the open-circuit voltage: what the terminals show with nothing connected.
  2. \(R_\text{th}\) is the resistance you see looking back into the terminals with every source switched off. Switching a voltage source off means replacing it with a wire (0 V); a current source becomes an open circuit (0 A).

For a divider, switching the source off joins the top of R1 to ground, so looking back from the output you see R1 and R2 in parallel:

$$V_\text{th} = V_\text{in}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} \qquad R_\text{th} = R_1 \parallel R_2$$
DiagramA divider and its Thévenin equivalent are indistinguishable from the outside

Now loading is just one more divider, made of \(R_\text{th}\) and the load:

$$V_\text{load} = V_\text{th}\,\frac{R_L}{R_\text{th} + R_L}$$

The fractional error is \(R_\text{th}/(R_\text{th} + R_L)\), which gives the rule of thumb behind the loading figure:

✓
Rule of thumb: the 100× ruleKeep the load at least 100 times the source's Thévenin resistance, and loading costs you less than 1 %. At 10× it costs about 9 %. When you can't meet the rule, put a buffer amplifier in between (Module 10), or calibrate the error out.
Worked example

The 1 MΩ / 1 MΩ divider from 10 V, measured with a 10 MΩ meter. Thévenin: \(V_\text{th} = 5\ \text{V}\), \(R_\text{th} = 1\ \text{M}\Omega \parallel 1\ \text{M}\Omega = 500\ \text{k}\Omega\).

The meter reads \(5 \times 10 / (0.5 + 10) = 4.76\ \text{V}\), a 4.8 % error. The meter is only 20× the source resistance, short of the 100× rule.

Thévenin also works the other way round, as a measurement. Measure the open-circuit voltage, then connect a known load and measure again. The drop tells you \(R_\text{th}\) without opening the box. It's how you find a battery's internal resistance, or the output resistance of a sensor with no datasheet. Equivalently, \(R_\text{th} = V_\text{oc}/I_\text{sc}\), the open-circuit voltage over the short-circuit current, though deliberately shorting a source is rarely a good idea.

Design exerciseWhat's inside the sensor?optional

A cheap temperature sensor board has an analog output and no useful datasheet. With nothing connected, the output reads 3.00 V on your meter. With a 10 kΩ resistor from the output to ground, it reads 2.50 V.

What's the sensor's output (Thévenin) resistance?

That's the total resistance in the loop. Subtract the 10 kΩ load.

The 10 kΩ carries 2.50 V / 10 kΩ = 0.25 mA. The missing 0.50 V is dropped inside the sensor at that same current.

\(I = 2.50 / 10\,\text{k} = 0.25\ \text{mA}\), and the 0.50 V drop inside the sensor gives \(R_\text{th} = 0.50 / 0.25\,\text{mA} = 2\ \text{k}\Omega\). By the 100× rule, anything reading this sensor should have an input resistance of at least 200 kΩ. A 10 MΩ meter is fine; some logger inputs are not.

Superposition

Circuits made of resistors and sources are linear: double every source and every voltage and current doubles. That gives you superposition. With several sources, the voltage at any point is the sum of what each source would produce on its own, with all the others switched off (voltage sources replaced by wires, current sources by open circuits).

A common use on the test stand: a load-cell amplifier puts out a signal between −2.5 V and +2.5 V, but the ADC only accepts 0 to 3.3 V. Two equal resistors, one from the signal and one from the 3.3 V supply, meet at the ADC pin. Each source sees a simple divider when the other is switched off:

$$V_\text{out} = \underbrace{V_\text{sig}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}}_\text{signal alone} + \underbrace{V_\text{ref}\,\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}}_\text{reference alone} = \frac{V_\text{sig}}{2} + 1.65\ \text{V}$$
with R1 = R2; each term is the other source switched off

The signal is halved and shifted up by 1.65 V, so −2.5 to +2.5 V lands on 0.4 to 2.9 V, safely inside the ADC's range.

Where superposition stops workingIt only applies to linear circuits, and only to voltages and currents. Power goes as \(V^2/R\), so you can't add up the power from each source separately. And anything nonlinear, like a diode or a transistor switching on and off, breaks it. Use it on the resistor networks around those parts, not on the parts themselves.

Reading schematics and datasheets

A schematic shows how parts are connected, not where they sit on the board. Here is the battery monitor from Section 5 as it would appear in the flight computer's schematic, with the conventions that trip people up most often marked:

DiagramThe battery monitor as drawn in a real schematic
  1. Power labels. The VBAT flag connects this wire to every other VBAT flag in the whole schematic, possibly on another page. No wire is drawn between them.
  2. Reference designators and values. R7 is "the seventh resistor". The letter tells you the kind of part: R resistor, C capacitor, L inductor, D diode, Q transistor, U integrated circuit, J connector, F fuse. Values are written without the unit and with the multiplier in place of the decimal point: 4k7 is 4.7 kΩ, 100n is 100 nF.
  3. Junction dots. A dot means the wires are connected. Here three wires meet at the top of R8, and the capacitor joins at a second dot.
  4. Crossings without a dot are not connected. The SPI clock passes straight over the sense net. Good schematics avoid four-way junctions entirely, so a crossing is never ambiguous.
  5. Ground symbols are all one node, like power labels. Boards often have several named grounds (GND, AGND, PGND, chassis), and which ones are joined, and where, is a deliberate design decision (Module 12).
  6. Net labels. VBAT_SENSE names the node, so you can find it in the layout and in the firmware's pin map. Two labels with the same name are connected, even if they're drawn pages apart.

Datasheets

Every part has a datasheet, and the numbers in it come in different kinds that mean very different things:

SectionWhat it meansHow to use it
Absolute maximum ratingsExceed these and the part may be damaged, even brieflyNever a place to operate. Stay well inside, with margin for transients
Recommended operating conditionsThe range over which the part is designed to workDesign to these: supply voltage, temperature, input range
Electrical characteristicsGuaranteed min and max values, plus a "typical", under the stated test conditionsDesign to the min or max. Check the conditions column: they may not match your circuit
Typical performance graphsHow a typical part behavesGood for intuition, not a guarantee

!
"Typical" is not a specificationA typical value is what the median part does at room temperature. Your part could be anywhere between the min and max, and at −20 °C on a cold pad it might sit at the edge. If a design only works with the typical value, it doesn't work.
✓
Where loading hides in a datasheetMicrocontroller ADCs list a maximum source impedance (sometimes called external input impedance), often a few tens of kilohms for slow sampling and much less for fast. That's the 100× rule from the ADC's side: the Thévenin resistance of your divider has to stay below it, or the readings come out low. The 100 nF capacitor on the battery monitor gets around it by holding the voltage steady while the ADC samples, so slow signals like battery voltage can use larger resistors.

Exercise 1Design · ●●○A battery monitor for a 3S pack

The new flight computer runs from a 3S LiPo. Its ADC accepts 0 to 3.3 V, and you want the reading at full charge to be no more than 3.0 V, leaving headroom for resistor tolerance.

Given Battery, full charge3 × 4.2 V = 12.6 V Maximum ADC voltage at full charge3.0 V R2 (bottom)10 kΩ

(a) What divider ratio \(R_2/(R_1 + R_2)\) do you need?

That's the inverse: the factor the firmware multiplies by. The ratio is less than 1.

\(3.0 / 12.6 = 0.238\).

(b) With R2 = 10 kΩ, what's the smallest R1 that achieves it?

That's the total, R1 + R2. Take away R2.

\(R_1 + R_2 = R_2 / 0.238\).

\(R_1 + R_2 = 10\,\text{k} / 0.238 = 42\ \text{k}\Omega\), so \(R_1 = 32\ \text{k}\Omega\). It isn't a standard value, so round up to 33 kΩ (E12), which gives 2.93 V at full charge. Rounding down would overshoot 3.0 V.

(c) With 33 kΩ and 10 kΩ, how much current does the divider draw from a full pack?

\(12.6\ \text{V} / 43\ \text{k}\Omega = 293\ \mu\text{A}\). Over a month in storage that's about 210 mAh.

Discussion: what if the drain is too much?

Scaling both resistors up by ten cuts the drain to 29 µA, but the Thévenin resistance becomes 77 kΩ, beyond what many ADCs accept as a source. A 100 nF capacitor at the ADC pin solves that for a slowly changing voltage like a battery. The other approach is to switch the divider with a MOSFET and only power it while measuring (Module 6). Either way, also check that the ADC pin is safe if the MCU is unpowered but the battery is connected: the divider can then push current into the pin's protection diodes.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●●The pressure reading that's 7 % low

A tank pressure transducer outputs 0.5 V at 0 psi and 4.5 V at 1000 psi. The test-stand data logger's channels accept 0 to 3.3 V, so a divider (R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 20 kΩ) scales the transducer down by 2/3. The logger software multiplies by 1.5 to undo it. On the first pressurisation the logged pressure is consistently low.

Given Transducer0.5–4.5 V for 0–1000 psi, output resistance negligible DividerR1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 20 kΩ Logger input resistance100 kΩ (from its datasheet)

(a) What's the Thévenin resistance the logger sees, looking back into the divider?

That's R1 + R2 in series. With the transducer replaced by a wire, they're in parallel as seen from the output.

\(R_\text{th} = 10\,\text{k} \parallel 20\,\text{k} = 6.67\ \text{k}\Omega\). The logger is only 15× that, so expect several percent of error.

(b) At 1000 psi the transducer outputs 4.5 V. What voltage does the logger actually measure?

That's the unloaded divider output. The logger's 100 kΩ loads it.

\(V_\text{th} = 3.0\ \text{V}\). Then it's a divider of Rth and the logger.

\(V = 3.0 \times 100/(100 + 6.67) = 2.81\ \text{V}\).

(c) What pressure does the logger report at a true 1000 psi?

Close, but the transducer's 0 psi point is at 0.5 V, not 0 V. Subtract the offset before scaling.

The software computes \(2.8125 \times 1.5 = 4.22\ \text{V}\), and converts: \((4.22 - 0.5)/4.0 \times 1000 = 930\ \text{psi}\). It reads 70 psi low at full scale, about 7 %, and the error grows with pressure.

Discussion: three ways to fix it

(1) Account for the loading in software: use the loaded ratio, \(R_2 \parallel R_L / (R_1 + R_2 \parallel R_L)\), instead of 2/3. Cheap, but the logger's input resistance varies from unit to unit. (2) Scale the divider down: 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ give Rth = 667 Ω, 150× smaller than the logger, so the error drops below 1 %. The transducer then has to drive 1.5 mA; check that it can. (3) Put a unity-gain op-amp buffer between the divider and the logger (Module 10). Whatever you choose, calibrate the whole chain end to end with a reference gauge before trusting it.

Exercise 3Analysis · ●●○The pressure spike that wasn't

On the test stand, a pressure transducer and a solenoid valve sit next to each other, 5 m from the DAQ box. To save wire, they share one ground return back to the DAQ. Every time the valve opens, the logged pressure jumps, then drops back when the valve closes.

Given Shared ground return5 m of 18 AWG, 21 mΩ/m Valve current3.0 A Transducer0.5–4.5 V for 0–1000 psi (4 mV/psi) DAQ inputmeasures the signal wire against the DAQ's own ground

(a) How far does the ground at the transducer rise above the DAQ's ground when the valve is open?

That's the wire's resistance in ohms. Multiply by the current flowing through it.

\(R = 5 \times 0.021 = 0.105\ \Omega\), so \(V = 3.0 \times 0.105 = 0.315\ \text{V}\).

(b) How big is the false pressure jump?

The transducer's output rides on top of its local ground, and the DAQ measures from its own ground. KVL around that path.

The DAQ sees the transducer's output plus 0.315 V. At 4 mV/psi that's \(0.315 / 0.004 \approx 79\ \text{psi}\) of pressure that doesn't exist, appearing exactly when the valve opens. If the sequence has a pressure-based abort, it could abort on this.

Discussion: what would you change?

Give the valve its own return wire, so no valve current flows in the sensor's ground. Better still, measure the transducer differentially: run both its signal and its ground back to a differential DAQ input, which measures the voltage between the two wires and ignores the common shift (Modules 10 and 13). And a habit worth building: when a sensor reading changes the moment an actuator switches, suspect the wiring before the sensor.

What you should see: drops that add up to the supply within your meter's accuracy, branch currents that add up at each node, a 1 MΩ divider that reads about 5 % low on a 10 MΩ meter while the 10 kΩ version reads correctly, and a 9 V battery with one or two ohms inside it.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. KCL: the currents into a node add up to the currents out. The ground return carries the sum of every load current.
  2. KVL: the voltages around any loop add up to zero. Every volt of the supply is dropped somewhere, including in wires, connectors and ground returns.
  3. Series resistances add; parallel conductances add. Series is bigger than the biggest, parallel smaller than the smallest.
  4. A divider scales a voltage by \(R_2/(R_1 + R_2)\). Its resistors trade battery drain against sensitivity to loading.
  5. Any linear circuit looks like \(V_\text{th}\) in series with \(R_\text{th}\) from two terminals. Keep the load at least 100× \(R_\text{th}\), or account for it.
  6. Superposition: with several sources, add the effect of each one alone. Linear circuits only, and never for power.
  7. On schematics, same-named labels are connected and dotless crossings are not. In datasheets, design to min and max, not typical.
