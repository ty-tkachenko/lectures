Communication buses

Chips talk to each other over a handful of standard buses: UART, SPI and I²C between chips on a board, CAN and RS-485 between boards over cables. Each is a set of agreements about which wire carries what and when. This module explains how each works at the level of voltages and timing, why some buses use two wires per signal, what happens to a fast edge on a long cable, how to choose a bus, and how to find out what's actually on the wires when something doesn't work.

Builds on: EE modules 1–8, especially open-drain outputs and pull-ups (Module 8). Bench: a microcontroller board, a sensor with I²C or SPI, a logic analyzer, an oscilloscope, and a length of twisted-pair cable.

What a bus agrees on

Sending a byte from one chip to another needs agreement on four things: which voltages mean 1 and 0 (Module 8), when each bit is valid (timing: either a shared clock wire, or a pre-agreed rate), who may talk when (one transmitter per wire, or rules for sharing), and how each bit is carried electrically (one wire against ground, or a pair of wires against each other). The buses in this module are different answers to those questions, each suited to a different distance, speed and number of devices.

UART

A UART (universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter) sends bytes over a single wire in each direction (TX from one device to RX on the other), with no clock wire. The line idles high. Each byte is a frame: a start bit (low), the data bits least-significant first, an optional parity bit, and a stop bit (high). The common "8N1" format is 8 data bits, no parity, one stop bit: 10 bit times per byte.

DiagramA UART frame: 0x4B in 8N1

Without a clock, the receiver relies on timing alone. It waits for the start bit's falling edge, then samples each bit at the middle of its expected bit time, using its own clock. Both ends must therefore agree on the baud rate (bits per second), and their clocks must be close: an error between them accumulates over the frame, and by the stop bit the sampling point has drifted by ten times the per-bit error. The frame survives as long as the last sample stays inside its bit, roughly a ±4–5 % total mismatch in practice, shared between the two ends.

Application

A microcontroller running from its internal RC oscillator, which may be accurate only to ±1–3 % over temperature, can talk to a GPS module or a radio over UART at room temperature and fail when cold. Running the microcontroller from a crystal (±0.005 %) removes the problem, and is good practice for any UART link. A related trap: the baud rate is generated by dividing the processor clock, so some rates can't be produced exactly; the microcontroller's reference manual tabulates the resulting error.

Design exerciseTX to TX

A new board's UART link to a sensor produces nothing at all. The schematic connects the microcontroller's TX pin to the sensor's pin labelled TX, and RX to RX.

What's wrong?

  1. The baud rates differ.
    A baud mismatch produces garbled data, not silence on both ends.
  2. TX and RX are named from each device's own point of view: one device's TX must connect to the other's RX. Two outputs are connected together, and two inputs listen to nothing.
    The classic UART wiring mistake; many boards add series resistors or solder jumpers so it can be fixed.
  3. UART needs a clock wire.
    UART is asynchronous: no clock wire.
  4. The sensor needs pull-up resistors on TX and RX.
    UART lines are push-pull and idle high by themselves.

Each device labels its pins from its own perspective, so TX connects to RX in each direction. When pin names are ambiguous (some modules label them from the host's perspective), check the datasheet's direction, and consider footprints for 0 Ω links that let the two be swapped on the board.

SPI

SPI (serial peripheral interface) adds the clock that UART lacks. A controller (the microcontroller) drives a clock line, SCLK, and data travels on each edge of it: controller to peripheral on MOSI (also called COPI or SDO), and peripheral to controller on MISO (CIPO or SDI), simultaneously. Each peripheral has its own chip-select line (CS), which the controller pulls low to talk to it; only the selected device drives MISO.

DiagramAn SPI bus with two peripherals, and its timing

Because every signal is driven push-pull and the clock comes from the controller, SPI is simple and fast: tens of megahertz on a board, with no addresses and no protocol overhead. The cost is wires, one chip select per device, and the need to agree on the mode: which clock polarity idles, and which edge samples the data. Mismatched modes are the most common SPI bug, and usually show up as data shifted by one bit.

I²C

I²C uses only two wires, SDA (data) and SCL (clock), shared by every device. Both are open-drain with a pull-up each (Module 8), so any device can pull a line low and none can fight another. Each peripheral has a 7-bit address: the controller starts a transaction with a start condition (SDA falling while SCL is high), sends the address and a read/write bit, and the addressed device answers by pulling SDA low for one clock, an acknowledge (ACK). Data bytes follow, each acknowledged, and a stop condition (SDA rising while SCL is high) ends the transaction.

DiagramAn I²C bus and the start of a transaction

Open-drain lines are what make I²C's sharing safe, and also what limit its speed: every rising edge is the pull-up charging the bus capacitance, so the rise time is set by R × C, exactly as in Module 8. The specification limits the rise time (1000 ns in standard mode, 100 kHz; 300 ns in fast mode, 400 kHz), and limits how much current a device must sink (3 mA), which sets a minimum pull-up. The right pull-up sits between the two limits:

$$R_\text{min} = \frac{V_{DD} - V_{OL}}{I_{OL}} \qquad R_\text{max} = \frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_\text{bus}}$$
0.8473 RC is the time to rise from 30 % to 70 % of VDD, the span the specification measures

Design exerciseTwo sensors with the same address

A board needs two identical pressure sensors on one I²C bus, but each has a fixed address, 0x76.

Which solution works?

  1. Connect them both; the controller will see two acknowledges.
    Both would respond to every transaction and corrupt each other's data on reads.
  2. Use the sensor's address-select pin if it has one (many have a pin that changes the address to 0x77), or put them on separate buses, or use an I²C multiplexer that connects one at a time.
    Each device on a bus needs a unique address; hardware provides several ways to get one.
  3. Use different pull-up values for each sensor.
    Pull-ups belong to the bus, not to a device, and don't change addresses.
  4. Read them at different clock speeds.
    Both would still respond to the same address.

Most sensors offer an address-select pin with two or four options. Without one, a second I²C peripheral on the microcontroller, or an I²C multiplexer, separates them. Checking every device's address for conflicts is part of the schematic review.

Single-ended and differential signalling

UART, SPI and I²C are single-ended: each signal is one wire, measured against ground. That works on a board, where ground is a solid plane and the wires are short. Over a cable it has two weaknesses. Noise induced on the wire, from a motor, a radio or a nearby power cable, adds directly to the signal. And the two ends' grounds aren't at exactly the same voltage, because return current through the ground wire's resistance shifts one end relative to the other (Module 1); the receiver sees that shift as part of the signal.

Differential signalling sends each signal on a pair of wires driven in opposite directions, and the receiver looks only at the difference between them. Noise induced on a closely twisted pair appears almost equally on both wires (common-mode) and cancels in the subtraction; so does any ground offset between the ends, within the receiver's common-mode range. Twisting the pair makes the induced noise more equal on both wires, and also reduces the pair's own radiation.

CAN and RS-485 for long runs

Both are differential buses designed for cables between boards, tens to hundreds of metres long, in electrically noisy places. Both run on a twisted pair with a 120 Ω termination resistor at each end of the bus, and allow many devices along it. Each microcontroller connects through a small transceiver chip that converts between its logic-level UART or CAN pins and the differential pair.

CAN's arbitration is worth understanding from the electrical side. The bus has two states: dominant, when a transceiver drives the pair apart, and recessive, when nobody drives and the termination pulls the pair together. Like an open-drain line, a dominant bit from any node overrides recessive bits from all others. Each node reads the bus while it transmits; a node that sends recessive but reads dominant knows it has lost arbitration and stops, leaving the higher-priority message intact.

Design exerciseA CAN bus with the wrong termination

A CAN bus with four nodes works on the bench with short wires, but in the vehicle, with 15 m of cable, it reports frequent errors. Measuring between CAN-H and CAN-L with power off reads 30 Ω.

What's wrong?

  1. The cable is too long for CAN.
    15 m is well within CAN's range at typical speeds.
  2. A correctly terminated bus measures 60 Ω (two 120 Ω resistors in parallel). 30 Ω means four terminators: every node has its own 120 Ω fitted. The extra terminations load the drivers and add reflections at the stubs; only the two nodes at the physical ends should be terminated.
    The resistance measurement is the quickest check of a CAN bus's termination.
  3. The nodes need pull-up resistors.
    CAN transceivers bias the bus themselves; pull-ups aren't used.
  4. Twisted pair should be replaced by two separate wires.
    Twisted pair is what makes the bus immune to noise.

Termination resistors belong at the two ends of the bus, matching the cable's characteristic impedance (next section). Modules that ship with a terminator fitted usually have a jumper or solder bridge to remove it. With power off, the bus should read about 60 Ω; 120 Ω means one is missing, 40 Ω or 30 Ω means extras.

Ringing and termination on long wires

A signal doesn't arrive at the far end of a wire instantly: it travels at roughly two-thirds of the speed of light, about 5 ns per metre in a cable. When the time for an edge to travel the wire is comparable to the edge's rise time, the wire has to be treated as a transmission line: it has a characteristic impedance Z₀, typically around 100–120 Ω for a twisted pair, and an edge arriving at an end that doesn't match Z₀ partly reflects (the RF notes, Module 1, cover this in depth).

A logic input is almost an open circuit, so it reflects nearly all of an arriving edge back towards the driver. The driver's low output impedance reflects it again, and the edge bounces back and forth, appearing at the receiver as overshoot and ringing. If a ring crosses the input's threshold, the receiver sees extra transitions, which on a clock line means extra clock edges and corrupted data. The fix is to make one end of the line match Z₀ so the reflection is absorbed:

  • End (parallel) termination: a resistor equal to Z₀ across the line at the receiver. The edge is absorbed when it arrives. Used on CAN and RS-485, at both ends since either end may transmit.
  • Series (source) termination: a resistor at the driver that, added to the driver's own output impedance, equals Z₀. The edge launches at half amplitude, doubles at the open receiver end, and its reflection is absorbed when it returns to the source. It uses no steady current, which suits point-to-point logic signals such as SPI clocks.

A useful rule: termination is needed when the wire's one-way delay exceeds about one-sixth of the signal's rise time. Note that it depends on the edge speed, not the data rate: a slow UART from a fast microcontroller pin can still ring.

Two rules of thumb settle most choices. Single-ended buses (UART, SPI, I²C) belong on a board, or at most on a short cable inside an enclosure; anything that leaves a box on a long cable, or runs past motors, valves and radios, should be differential. And when several boards must share information robustly, with error detection built in, CAN's arbitration and checksums save writing them in firmware.

Design exerciseA sensor 3 m away

A pressure sensor with an I²C interface must be mounted 3 m from the flight computer, along a harness that also carries valve wiring.

What's the best approach?

  1. Run I²C directly over the harness with strong pull-ups.
    3 m of cable adds hundreds of picofarads and couples valve switching noise onto single-ended, open-drain lines with slow edges.
  2. Put a small microcontroller (or an I²C-to-differential extender) next to the sensor, and carry the data to the flight computer over a differential bus such as CAN or RS-485.
    Keep I²C short, on the board; use a differential bus for the long, noisy run.
  3. Use a shielded cable and keep I²C at 400 kHz.
    Shielding helps noise but not capacitance; 400 kHz makes the rise-time problem worse.
  4. Use an analog sensor instead and run the analog signal 3 m.
    An analog signal over the same harness picks up the same noise, with no error detection at all.

I²C is designed for a bus capacitance of a few hundred picofarads and a single shared ground, both of which a 3 m harness next to switching valves violates. Converting to a differential bus near the sensor gives noise immunity, tolerance of ground offsets and, with CAN, built-in error detection. Dedicated I²C bus extenders exist for when firmware changes aren't possible.

Debugging with a logic analyzer

A logic analyzer records many digital lines at once, as ones and zeros against time, and decodes the protocols on them: it can show a UART's bytes as text, an I²C transaction's address, acknowledges and data, an SPI exchange's bytes on each line. Inexpensive USB analyzers do this well up to a few megahertz, which covers most microcontroller buses, and are the most useful tool for "the sensor doesn't respond".

DiagramA logic analyzer capture of an I²C read, decoded

A few practices make captures reliable:

  • Sample fast enough: at least four times, better ten times, the bit rate, so short pulses and edges are captured accurately.
  • Connect ground close to the signals being measured, as with the oscilloscope (Module 3).
  • Trigger on the event of interest, such as a chip select going low or an I²C start, so the capture begins where it matters.
  • Use the oscilloscope when the logic analyzer's ones and zeros look plausible but the system still misbehaves. A logic analyzer shows what a threshold decided; it can't show a slow edge, a ring crossing the threshold or a level sitting near the threshold, which is often the real problem.
Application

A typical first capture when a sensor doesn't respond: the controller sends the start, the address and the read/write bit, and the ninth clock shows SDA high, a NACK. That narrows the cause to the sensor: wrong address (check the address-select pin), no power, held in reset, or the SDA and SCL lines swapped. If instead the capture shows nothing at all on the lines, the problem is the microcontroller's pin configuration or firmware, and the wiring to the sensor isn't the suspect.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●○○UART throughput

A GPS module sends data at 115 200 baud in 8N1 format.

(a) How long does one byte take?

That's 8 bits. A frame also has a start and a stop bit: 10 bits per byte.

10 bits/115 200 = 86.8 µs.

(b) What's the maximum throughput?

115 200/10 = 11 520 bytes per second. A GPS module sending 600 bytes of messages ten times a second needs 6000 bytes/s: fine at 115 200, too much at 9600.

Exercise 2Design · ●●○I²C pull-up limits

A 3.3 V I²C bus runs at 400 kHz (rise time ≤ 300 ns) with 100 pF of bus capacitance. Devices must sink at most 3 mA at VOL = 0.4 V.

(a) Minimum pull-up?

(3.3 − 0.4)/0.003 = 967 Ω.

(b) Maximum pull-up?

tr = 0.8473 RC.

R = 300 ns/(0.8473 × 100 pF) = 3.54 kΩ. A 2.2 kΩ pull-up sits comfortably between the limits.

Exercise 3Analysis · ●●○When does a wire need termination?

An SPI clock with 3 ns edges runs over a 1.5 m ribbon cable (about 5 ns/m).

(a) What's the one-way delay?

1.5 × 5 = 7.5 ns.

(b) Using the one-sixth rule, what's the longest wire that doesn't need termination for this edge?

Delay ≤ tr/6.

3 ns/6 = 0.5 ns of delay, or about 0.1 m. At 1.5 m the line needs series termination at the driver, slower edges, or both.

Exercise 4Analysis · ●○○Checking a CAN bus

A CAN bus is correctly terminated with 120 Ω at each end.

What resistance should a meter read between CAN-H and CAN-L with power off?

Two 120 Ω resistors in parallel: 60 Ω.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. UART: no clock; both ends must agree on baud rate, and clock errors accumulate across the frame. TX connects to RX.
  2. SPI: clocked, push-pull, fast, one chip select per device; agree on the mode.
  3. I²C: two shared open-drain lines with addresses; pull-ups trade sink current against rise time.
  4. Differential signalling rejects common-mode noise and ground offsets; use it for anything on a long cable.
  5. CAN and RS-485 run on twisted pair with 120 Ω at each end; CAN adds arbitration and error checking.
  6. A wire longer than about rise time/6 of delay is a transmission line: terminate it, or slow the edges.
  7. A logic analyzer shows decoded traffic; an oscilloscope shows why a threshold decided what it did.
