Transistors as switches

A microcontroller pin can deliver a few milliamps at 3.3 V. A solenoid valve wants an amp or two at 12 V or more, and an igniter several amps for a fraction of a second. A transistor bridges that gap: a small control signal switches a large current. This module explains how MOSFETs and bipolar transistors behave as switches, how to drive them fully on, where the heat comes from, how to handle inductive loads, and how these pieces combine into a channel that fires an igniter only when it's meant to.

Builds on: EE modules 1–5, especially inductive kickback (Module 3) and diodes (Module 5). Bench: bench supply, oscilloscope, a logic-level MOSFET, an NPN transistor, a solenoid valve or relay, diodes and a Zener or TVS.

Why a transistor

A microcontroller output pin is a small switch to its own supply, able to source or sink perhaps 10–20 mA at 3.3 V. Connecting a 12 V, 1 A solenoid valve to it directly would ask for fifty times more current at four times the voltage, and the pin, or the whole controller, would be destroyed. What's needed is a component that a small signal can turn fully on and fully off, and that can carry the load's current and block its voltage. That's a transistor used as a switch.

DiagramA small signal controlling a large load

An ideal switch has zero resistance when on, infinite resistance when off, and changes state instantly. A transistor approaches all three well enough for most purposes, and the ways it falls short (a little on-resistance, a finite switching time) are exactly what decide how hot it gets. The two common types are the MOSFET and the bipolar junction transistor (BJT).

MOSFETs as switches

A MOSFET has three terminals: the gate, the drain and the source. The gate sits on a very thin insulating layer above the silicon between drain and source, so it draws no steady current at all; electrically it's the plate of a capacitor. In an N-channel MOSFET, a positive gate-to-source voltage attracts electrons to the surface under the gate. Once VGS exceeds the threshold voltage Vth, those electrons form a conducting channel from drain to source, and the more the gate voltage exceeds the threshold, the more conductive the channel becomes.

Fully on, the MOSFET behaves like a small resistance, RDS(on): from a few milliohms in a power MOSFET to an ohm or so in a small-signal part. Datasheets specify RDS(on) at particular gate voltages, typically 10 V and, for logic-level MOSFETs, also 4.5 V or 2.5 V. Two warnings follow:

  • The threshold voltage is where the MOSFET starts to conduct, not where it's fully on. At Vth it passes perhaps 250 µA. A MOSFET with a 2 V threshold driven with 3.3 V may be only partly on.
  • A partly-on MOSFET gets hot. If the gate voltage isn't high enough for the channel to carry the load current, the MOSFET limits the current and drops a large voltage across itself, dissipating VDS × ID.

Every power MOSFET also contains a body diode from source to drain, a side-effect of its construction. It means a MOSFET blocks current in one direction only, which matters for reverse-polarity protection (Module 5) and for high-side switching.

Design exerciseA MOSFET with a 2 V threshold on a 3.3 V pin

A MOSFET's datasheet lists VGS(th) = 1.0–2.5 V and RDS(on) = 10 mΩ at VGS = 10 V. A designer drives it from a 3.3 V microcontroller pin to switch 5 A.

Is that safe?

  1. Yes: 3.3 V is above the maximum threshold of 2.5 V.
    The threshold is where conduction begins, at a fraction of a milliamp.
  2. Not from the information given: RDS(on) is only specified at 10 V. A part at the high end of the threshold range may be far from fully on at 3.3 V, limiting the current and dissipating watts. Choose a MOSFET with RDS(on) specified at 2.5 V or 3.3 V, or add a gate driver.
    Only an RDS(on) specified at your gate voltage guarantees the MOSFET is fully on.
  3. Yes, because MOSFET gates draw no current.
    True, but irrelevant: the question is how well the channel conducts at 3.3 V.
  4. No: MOSFETs can't be driven by microcontrollers.
    Logic-level MOSFETs are designed for exactly this.

Check the "typical output characteristics" graph and, more importantly, whether RDS(on) is guaranteed at your gate voltage. A part rated only at 10 V may have a channel that can carry 5 A at 3.3 V on a good day and a fraction of that with a high-threshold part or when cold. Logic-level parts list RDS(on) at 4.5 V and often 2.5 V; those are the ones to drive directly from logic.

BJTs as switches

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has a base, collector and emitter. Unlike a MOSFET's gate, its base draws current: a small base current IB allows a collector current up to β times larger, where the current gain β (or hFE) is typically 50–300 and varies widely between parts and with temperature. The base-emitter junction is a diode, so the base sits about 0.7 V above the emitter when the transistor conducts.

As a switch, a BJT is driven into saturation: given more base current than the load needs, so that the collector-emitter voltage collapses to VCE(sat), about 0.1–0.3 V. The base resistor is chosen from first principles:

$$I_B = \frac{I_C}{\beta_\text{min}} \times \text{overdrive} \qquad R_B = \frac{V_\text{drive} - 0.7\ \text{V}}{I_B}$$
an overdrive of 2–5 ensures saturation even with the lowest-gain part
DiagramA BJT switching a relay, and how it compares with a MOSFET

At high currents the MOSFET wins: a BJT's fixed 0.2 V at 5 A is 1 W, while a 10 mΩ MOSFET dissipates 0.25 W, and the BJT would need 100 mA or more of base current. For small loads such as relays, LEDs and buzzers, a small BJT is cheap, rugged and easy to drive from any logic level. BJTs also appear as level shifters, for example to pull a P-channel MOSFET's gate low from a 3.3 V signal, as in the pyro channel later in this module.

Low-side and high-side switching

A switch can go between the load and ground (low-side) or between the supply and the load (high-side). Electrically both interrupt the current, but they differ in how easy they are to drive and in what the load is connected to when it's off.

DiagramLow-side and high-side switches
  • Low-side with an N-channel MOSFET is the simplest: its source is at ground, so a logic output drives its gate directly. The cost is that the load stays connected to the supply when off, so a short from the load's wiring to ground (to the vehicle's structure, for example) turns the load on with no switch at all.
  • High-side switching keeps the load at ground potential when off, which is safer for wiring that runs through a vehicle. A P-channel MOSFET does this simply: its gate is pulled below the supply to turn it on, usually through a small transistor acting as a level shifter. An N-channel MOSFET on the high side has lower resistance but needs its gate driven above the supply, which takes a dedicated high-side driver or a load switch chip.
Application

One common example of an inductive load switched on the low side is a solenoid valve: an N-channel MOSFET per valve, driven directly from the controller, with a clamp across the coil. A load where an accidental short must never turn it on, such as an igniter, is switched on both sides, as the pyro-channel section shows, so that no single fault in the wiring or the electronics can complete the circuit.

Design exerciseA chafed wire

A valve on a vehicle is switched low-side. Its wire from the switch to the valve runs past a sharp edge of the metal frame, which is connected to the electronics' ground. The insulation wears through.

What happens?

  1. Nothing: the switch is off, so no current flows.
    The switch is on the low side; the short bypasses it.
  2. The valve's low side is now connected to ground by the frame, bypassing the switch, so the valve opens and stays open regardless of the controller.
    With a low-side switch, the wire between the load and the switch is at the supply voltage through the load when off; grounding it turns the load on.
  3. The supply short-circuits and the fuse blows.
    The current still flows through the valve's coil, which limits it to its normal value.
  4. The valve turns off permanently.
    The short completes the circuit rather than breaking it.

With low-side switching, the wire between load and switch carries the switching function: grounding it anywhere along its length turns the load on. With high-side switching, the same chafe would short a wire that's already at ground when off, causing nothing, and when on it would short the supply through the switch, blowing a fuse rather than operating the valve. For loads where unintended activation is dangerous, this is a strong argument for high-side switching, or for switching both sides.

Gate drive and switching losses

The gate draws no steady current, but it's a capacitor, so turning a MOSFET on means charging it, and the current available to charge it sets how fast the switch changes state. Datasheets describe this with the total gate charge Qg, typically 5–100 nC: the switching time is roughly Qg divided by the gate current. A microcontroller pin through a 100 Ω resistor supplies about 30 mA, so a 30 nC MOSFET takes about a microsecond to switch.

Switching isn't instantaneous, and during the transition the MOSFET briefly carries current while voltage is still across it. Following the charge into the gate step by step explains where the energy goes:

  1. The gate charges from 0 to Vth: nothing happens at the drain yet.
  2. From Vth to the plateau voltage, the drain current rises to the load current. The drain voltage hasn't moved, because the load (an inductor, or a clamped circuit) holds the current, so the MOSFET dissipates V × I.
  3. On the Miller plateau, the gate voltage stops rising while the drain voltage falls: the gate current is busy charging the gate-drain capacitance, whose far side is moving. The MOSFET is still dissipating, now with the full current and a falling voltage.
  4. Once the drain has fallen, the gate charges the rest of the way and RDS(on) falls to its final value.

Each turn-on and turn-off therefore costs a burst of energy, roughly E ≈ ½ V I tswitch. Switching at a frequency f costs that energy f times a second, on top of the steady conduction loss I²RDS(on):

$$P \approx \underbrace{I^2 R_\text{DS(on)}\,D}_{\text{conduction}} + \underbrace{V I\,t_\text{switch}\,f}_{\text{switching (on + off)}}$$
D: the fraction of time the switch is on · the temperature rise is P × θJA (RF Module 4)

A gate resistor is still worth having even though it slows switching: it damps ringing between the gate capacitance and the wiring's inductance, and limits the current the driving pin has to supply. Values of 10–100 Ω are typical for a MOSFET driven from logic.

Keeping the switch off when nobody's driving it

A microcontroller's pins are inputs, effectively disconnected, while it's held in reset, during power-up, and while it's being programmed. A MOSFET gate left floating can pick up enough charge from leakage or a nearby signal to turn partly on. A pull-down resistor from gate to source (10–100 kΩ) holds the gate at 0 V whenever nothing drives it, so the load is off by default. This one resistor is the start of fail-safe design.

Design exerciseA valve that clicks at power-up

A valve driver's MOSFET gate connects directly to a microcontroller pin, with no other parts. Every time the system is powered on, the valve briefly opens.

What's the cause, and the fix?

  1. The firmware sets the pin high at start-up.
    Possible, but the firmware hasn't run yet during the reset period, which is when it happens.
  2. During power-up and reset the pin floats, and the gate picks up enough charge to turn the MOSFET partly on. A pull-down resistor from gate to source keeps it off until the firmware drives the pin.
    A floating gate is a capacitor with nothing defining its voltage.
  3. The MOSFET's body diode conducts at power-up.
    The body diode only conducts if the drain goes below the source.
  4. The flyback diode turns the valve on.
    The flyback diode only conducts when the coil's current is interrupted.

Until the microcontroller has started and configured the pin as an output, the pin is high-impedance and the gate's voltage is undefined: charge from the drain through the gate-drain capacitance, as the supply ramps up, can be enough to turn it on. A 100 kΩ resistor from gate to source fixes the gate at 0 V. Configuring the pin low as early as possible in the firmware is good practice too, but the resistor covers the time before the firmware runs.

Clamping inductive loads

Module 3 showed why a switched coil needs a flyback diode: an inductor's current can't stop instantly, and without a path the voltage at the switch rises until something breaks down. The diode solves that, but it also slows the coil down. Once the switch opens, the coil's current circulates through the diode, and what removes the coil's stored energy is only the small voltage across the diode and the coil's own resistance. From v = L di/dt, the current decays faster if the voltage opposing it is larger:

$$L\frac{di}{dt} = -(V_\text{clamp} + iR) \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_\text{decay} = \frac{L}{R}\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{I_0 R}{V_\text{clamp}}\right)$$
a higher clamp voltage empties the coil faster; the switch must withstand Vsupply + Vclamp

That's the trade-off: a plain diode is gentle on the switch but slow; a diode in series with a Zener, or a TVS across the switch, lets the voltage rise to a defined level and removes the energy several times faster. Mechanically, a solenoid valve closes when its coil's current falls below the level needed to hold the plunger, so the clamp choice directly sets how quickly the valve shuts.

Design exerciseA valve that closes too slowly

A propellant valve must close within 10 ms of the command, but tests show it takes 25 ms. Its coil has a plain flyback diode.

Which change attacks the cause?

  1. Use a faster MOSFET.
    The MOSFET already switches in microseconds; the delay is the coil's current decaying.
  2. Add a Zener in series with the flyback diode (or clamp with a TVS), so the coil's current decays against a much higher voltage, and check the MOSFET's voltage rating covers the supply plus the clamp voltage.
    The decay rate is set by the voltage opposing the current; a plain diode offers less than a volt.
  3. Remove the diode entirely.
    That gives the fastest decay but an uncontrolled voltage spike that will destroy the switch.
  4. Increase the supply voltage.
    A higher supply raises the holding current, which makes it slower to fall.

The coil's energy has to be absorbed somewhere, and the rate is set by the opposing voltage. A diode-plus-Zener clamp lets the switch voltage rise to the supply plus the Zener voltage while the current decays, cutting the release time several-fold. The MOSFET must be rated above that total, with margin, and the Zener must absorb the coil's energy, ½LI², every time the valve closes.

Pyro channels: arming, continuity and fail-safe states

An igniter (often called an e-match) is a small resistive bridge wire, usually 0.5–3 Ω, coated with a pyrotechnic composition. A current above its all-fire rating, typically around an amp for a short time, heats the wire enough to ignite it; a current below its no-fire rating, typically a few hundred milliamps, can be applied indefinitely without igniting it. A channel that fires one is a high-current switch, but with requirements that go beyond any other load: it must fire reliably when commanded, and it must not fire under any single fault, at power-up, during programming, or if a wire is damaged. Everything in this module so far comes together here.

DiagramA pyro channel with independent arming, firing and continuity sensing

The design rests on a few principles, each traceable to earlier sections:

  • Independent series switches. The current passes through a mechanical arm switch (often a key or a removable plug, sometimes with a "remove before flight" tag), a high-side MOSFET controlled by an ARM signal, and a low-side MOSFET controlled by a FIRE signal. Firing needs all three; a single stuck switch, firmware bug or chafed wire (the low-side exercise above) can't do it alone.
  • Off by default. Each gate has a resistor holding it off whenever nothing drives it, so the channel is safe during power-up, reset, brown-out and programming.
  • Firing current with margin. The switches, wiring and battery must deliver well above the all-fire current into the igniter, even with the battery sagging (Module 1) and through every connector's resistance. MOSFETs are chosen for a low RDS(on) at the available gate voltage, and rated for the pulse current.
  • Continuity sensing. Before arming, the controller checks that an igniter is connected and intact, using a current far below the no-fire level, measured with an ADC.
  • A defined behaviour after firing. A fired igniter may become open or short. If it shorts, the channel would carry the battery's full current until switched off, so firmware turns the fire switch off after a fixed time, and a fuse or current limit (Module 7) protects the battery.

Design exerciseA continuity check that's too thorough

To get a cleaner continuity reading, someone proposes reducing the sense resistor from 10 kΩ to 10 Ω, so a few hundred milliamps flow through the igniter during the check.

Why is that a bad idea?

  1. The ADC can't read such a large voltage.
    The voltage would still be within 0–3.3 V; the problem is the current through the igniter.
  2. A continuity current of a few hundred milliamps approaches the igniter's no-fire rating: the check itself could heat the bridge wire and fire it, or degrade it. Continuity currents are kept to milliamps or less, far below no-fire.
    The whole point of continuity sensing is to verify the igniter without any risk of firing it.
  3. It would drain the battery.
    It would, briefly, but that's the lesser problem.
  4. The MOSFETs would have to be on.
    The continuity path bypasses them.

The continuity current must stay far below the no-fire current under every fault, including with the sense supply at its maximum and the igniter at its lowest resistance. With 10 kΩ from 3.3 V, the worst case is 0.33 mA, a factor of a thousand below a typical no-fire rating. The reading doesn't need to be precise: it only has to separate "open", "short" and "a few ohms".

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Design · ●○○A base resistor for a relay

An NPN transistor switches a 100 mA relay coil from a 3.3 V logic output. The transistor's minimum β is 50, and the design uses an overdrive of 2.

(a) What base current is needed?

IB = 100 mA / 50 × 2 = 4 mA.

(b) What base resistor gives that current?

That ignores the base-emitter drop: only 3.3 − 0.7 V appears across the resistor.

RB = (3.3 − 0.7)/0.004 = 650 Ω. Choose 620 Ω (slightly more current), not 680 Ω.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Heat in a valve driver

A MOSFET with RDS(on) = 25 mΩ (at the gate voltage used) holds a valve open with 3 A, continuously. It's in an SO-8 package with θJA = 60 K/W, at 40 °C ambient.

(a) Conduction loss?

P = I²R = 9 × 0.025 = 0.225 W.

(b) Junction temperature?

Tj = 40 + 0.225 × 60 = 53.5 °C: comfortable.

Discussion: now PWM it

If the same valve is driven with PWM at 20 kHz to reduce its holding current, each switching event costs roughly ½ × 12 V × 3 A × 100 ns = 1.8 µJ, twice per cycle, or 72 mW at 20 kHz. That's modest; at 200 kHz, or with a slow gate drive, switching loss would dominate.

Exercise 3Design · ●●○A faster valve clamp

A 12 V valve coil has L = 50 mH and R = 12 Ω (1 A).

(a) How much energy does the clamp absorb each time the valve is switched off?

E = ½LI² = ½ × 0.05 × 1² = 25 mJ (less the part dissipated in the coil's own resistance during the decay).

(b) With a 24 V Zener plus a diode (24.8 V total clamp), how long does the current take to fall to zero?

t = (L/R) ln(1 + I₀R/Vclamp).

L/R = 4.17 ms; ln(1 + 12/24.8) = ln(1.484) = 0.395; t ≈ 1.6 ms. With a plain diode (0.8 V) it would be 4.17 × ln(16) ≈ 11.6 ms.

Exercise 4Analysis · ●○○Continuity reading

The continuity circuit above uses a 10 kΩ sense resistor from 3.3 V, a 2 Ω igniter, and a 1 kΩ resistor from the igniter's bottom terminal to ground.

(a) What voltage does the ADC read?

V = 3.3 × (2 + 1000)/(10 000 + 2 + 1000) = 3.3 × 1002/11 002 = 0.30 V.

(b) What's the continuity current?

I = 3.3/11 002 Ω = 0.30 mA, about a thousand times below a typical no-fire current.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A transistor lets a small signal switch a large current. MOSFETs suit high currents; BJTs suit small loads and level shifting.
  2. A MOSFET is fully on only when VGS is well above Vth; check that RDS(on) is specified at your gate voltage. A partly-on MOSFET gets hot.
  3. Drive a BJT into saturation with IB = overdrive × IC/βmin.
  4. Low-side switching is simplest; high-side keeps the load at ground when off, so a grounded wire can't turn it on.
  5. Gate charge sets switching time; losses are conduction (I²R) plus switching (∝ V I t f). Pull-down resistors keep gates off when nothing drives them.
  6. A plain flyback diode protects the switch but slows the coil's release; a Zener or TVS clamp speeds it, at a higher switch voltage.
  7. A pyro channel uses independent series switches, off-by-default gates, low-current continuity sensing and a defined behaviour after firing, so that no single fault fires it.
