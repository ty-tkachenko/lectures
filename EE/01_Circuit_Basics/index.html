Voltage, current, resistance & power

Every circuit, from a microcontroller board to a motor driver, runs on four quantities and one simple law. This module builds an intuitive understanding of each, so that you can predict what a circuit does before measuring it.

Current

Metal wires are full of electrons that are free to move. Connect a battery across a wire and they drift along it. Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point, measured in amperes (A). One amp is one coulomb of charge per second.

$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$
current (A) = charge (C) ÷ time (s)

Two properties of current are worth keeping in mind:

  • Current flows in loops. Charge leaves one terminal of the battery, goes through the circuit and returns to the other terminal. If the loop is broken anywhere (a loose crimp, an open switch, a blown fuse), current stops everywhere in that loop at once.
  • Current isn't used up. Whatever goes into a component comes out the other side. Components use energy, not charge.
i
Which way does it flow?Electrons actually drift from − to +. Long before anyone knew that, engineers agreed to draw current from + to −, which is called conventional current. Every schematic, datasheet and textbook uses conventional current, and so will we. The direction you pick doesn't change any answer, as long as you're consistent.
!
Common misconception: "the resistor uses up some of the current"Put a resistor and an LED in series across a battery, and it's tempting to think the LED gets less current because the resistor "used some". It doesn't. The resistor turns some energy into heat, but every electron that goes in comes out the other side. There's only one path, so the same current flows through everything in a single loop, whatever order the parts are in. That's the first of Kirchhoff's laws, which Module 2 builds on.

Voltage and ground

Voltage is the energy each unit of charge gains or loses between two points, measured in volts (V). One volt is one joule per coulomb. A 7.4 V battery gives every coulomb that passes through it 7.4 joules, and the circuit takes that energy back out as heat, light, motion or radio waves.

The key word is between. "This pin is at 3.3 V" is shorthand for "this pin is 3.3 V above the point we agreed to call zero." That agreed point is called ground (GND). It's usually the battery's negative terminal, and it's drawn with this symbol:

DiagramVoltages are measured from a reference

A useful picture is a water system: voltage is like a pressure difference, current is the flow rate, and a resistor is a narrow pipe. Just as you can't talk about the pressure "at" a point without saying what it's relative to, you can't talk about voltage at a node without a reference. The analogy has limits: water can spill out of a pipe, but current can't leave a circuit; it always returns to its source.

!
"Ground" is a reference, not the EarthIn a real device, ground is a copper net that every circuit agrees to measure from. It has resistance like any other wire. When large currents flow through it (a motor starting or a solenoid valve opening, for example), different points on "ground" can sit at different voltages. This causes a whole class of hard-to-find faults, covered in Modules 12 and 13.
Worked example

In the diagram, put the meter's red probe on node A and the black probe on node B. Both nodes are measured from the same ground, so the meter reads the difference:

\(V_{AB} = V_A - V_B = 5.0 - 3.3 = 1.7\ \text{V}\)

Swap the probes and it reads −1.7 V. The ground reference cancels out of the subtraction, so the voltage between two points doesn't depend on where you decided "zero" is.

Resistance and Ohm's law

A resistor opposes current. Push harder (more voltage) and more current flows. Make the path narrower (more resistance) and less current flows. For most conductors at a steady temperature, the relationship is a straight line:

$$V = I\,R \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad I = \frac{V}{R} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad R = \frac{V}{I}$$
Ohm's law: volts = amps × ohms

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω). Values span a wide range: milliohms for wires and current-sense shunts, ohms to tens of ohms for heaters and solenoid coils, kilohms for pull-up resistors and voltage dividers, and megohms for a multimeter's input.

The simulator below is a battery and one resistor. Change either one and watch the current respond: with the voltage fixed, doubling the resistance halves the current. The dots stand for packets of charge. They always move at the same speed, so more current shows up as more dots passing each point every second, not as faster ones.

Worked example

A 3.3 V microcontroller pin drives a 1 kΩ resistor to ground. The pin supplies \(I = V/R = 3.3\ \text{V} / 1000\ \Omega = 3.3\ \text{mA}\).

A useful shortcut: volts ÷ kilohms = milliamps. Most signal-level circuits work in kΩ and mA.

Design exerciseA power indicator LED

A board needs an LED that lights when it's powered. It runs from 5 V. A red LED drops about 2 V across itself whenever it's on (Module 5 explains why), and you want 10 mA through it.

What series resistor sets 10 mA?

That would be right if the LED took no voltage. The resistor only sees what's left after the LED's 2 V.

The resistor and LED share the 5 V. How much is left across the resistor?

The resistor sees 5 − 2 = 3 V, so \(R = 3\ \text{V} / 10\ \text{mA} = 300\ \Omega\). The nearest standard value is 300 Ω (E24) or 330 Ω (E12), which gives about 9 mA, bright enough for an indicator.

Power and power ratings

Every resistor turns electrical energy into heat. The rate at which it does so is the power, measured in watts (W), or joules per second. Multiply voltage (energy per charge) by current (charge per second) and you get energy per second:

$$P = V\,I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$
all three forms are the same equation, with Ohm's law substituted in

Use whichever form matches what you know. If you know the current through a wire, use \(I^2R\). If you know the voltage across a resistor, use \(V^2/R\).

Every resistor has a power rating: the most heat it can get rid of without damage. A common 0603 surface-mount resistor is rated ⅒ W, and a through-hole one is often ¼ W. Run a part at its full rating and it gets hot (often over 100 °C). The rating also assumes good airflow and room temperature, and a sealed enclosure sitting in the sun has neither.

✓
Rule of thumb: derate to 50 %Pick a part whose rating is at least twice the power you calculate. The same idea applies to voltage ratings on capacitors and current ratings on connectors. Derating is the cheapest reliability you can buy.
Worked example

A 100 Ω resistor sits directly across a 5 V rail. It dissipates \(P = V^2/R = 25/100 = 0.25\ \text{W}\).

A ¼ W resistor would technically be "within rating", but it would run at its maximum temperature all the time, and the rating assumes cooler conditions than a sealed enclosure in the sun. Applying the 50 % rule, you'd pick a ½ W part. It's also worth asking whether the circuit needs to dissipate a quarter of a watt at all. A smaller package doesn't help: smaller parts have less area to shed heat, so their ratings are lower.

Design exerciseDoes the LED resistor fit in an 0402?

Back to the 300 Ω resistor carrying 10 mA in the power indicator. The board is tight on space, and an 0402 resistor is rated 1/16 W (62.5 mW).

How much power does the resistor dissipate?

Correct value, but in watts. The answer is in milliwatts.

That's 5 V × 10 mA, the power of the whole circuit. The resistor only sees 3 V of it.

\(P = I^2R = (0.01)^2 \times 300 = 30\ \text{mW}\), or \(V I = 3\ \text{V} \times 10\ \text{mA}\). That's 48 % of the 0402's rating, just inside the 50 % rule, so it fits.

Wire resistance

We usually draw wires as perfect conductors, and for a few centimetres of PCB trace carrying milliamps that's fine. But wire resistance grows with length and shrinks with cross-sectional area:

$$R_\text{wire} = \frac{\rho\,\ell}{A}$$
ρ is copper's resistivity (1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m), ℓ the length and A the cross-sectional area

Wire size is often given in AWG (American Wire Gauge). The numbering runs backwards: bigger numbers mean thinner wire. Every 3 gauges halves the area, so it doubles the resistance per metre.

i
Why long cables matterPicture a 12 V supply driving a solenoid (an electromagnet that pulls a plunger, as in a valve or a door latch) at the end of a long cable. The solenoid needs a minimum current to pull in reliably. Remember that current makes a round trip, out along one conductor and back along the other, so the cable's resistance counts twice. Too thin or too long a cable, and the solenoid doesn't pull in.

This is the first example of a pattern that comes up again and again: the "ideal" part of the schematic is often not where the voltage goes. A common fix is to put the switch, often a relay, next to the load. The long cable then carries only a small control signal, and the large load current travels a few metres of thick wire.

One common example in rocketry is firing an igniter. For safety, the igniter is fired from tens of metres away, and it needs a minimum current, its all-fire current, to light reliably. A launch cable that's too long or too thin leaves the igniter short of that current, exactly as in the figure above, which is why many launch systems put the firing relay at the pad.

Example

A solenoid works every time on the bench with a 1 m lead, then fails to pull in once it's installed at the end of a 30 m cable. Same supply, same solenoid. The difference is the cable. A 1 m lead adds almost no resistance, but tens of metres of thin wire can add several ohms, more than the solenoid itself. The current falls below what it needs.

Note what didn't happen: the voltage didn't "run out" along the cable. Voltage gets divided between every resistance in the loop, and a long cable simply takes a big share.

Design exerciseHow long can the cable be?

A 12 V supply drives a 1.6 Ω solenoid that needs at least 1 A, and you want a 2× margin (2 A). The cable is 22 AWG twin, 52.9 mΩ per metre per conductor. Ignore the supply's and switch's own resistance.

What's the longest one-way cable length that still delivers 2 A?

That's the total length of copper, out and back. The one-way length is half.

At 2 A the whole loop can be at most 12 / 2 = 6 Ω. Subtract the solenoid, and what's left is the cable's budget.

Loop budget: 6 Ω. Cable budget: 6 − 1.6 = 4.4 Ω for both conductors, so 2.2 Ω each. Length: \(2.2 / 0.0529 \approx 41.6\ \text{m}\) one way. If the load needs to be further away, go to thicker wire or move the switch next to the load. You can check this in the simulator above.

Batteries

A battery isn't a perfect voltage source. A good model is an ideal voltage source in series with a small internal resistance. The more current you draw, the more voltage is lost inside the battery, and the lower the terminal voltage sags:

$$V_\text{terminal} = V_\text{open circuit} - I\,R_\text{internal}$$

Battery capacity is usually given in milliamp-hours (mAh): a 2200 mAh pack can supply 2.2 A for one hour, or 220 mA for ten hours. Energy is capacity × voltage, so a 2S (two-cell, 7.4 V) 2200 mAh pack holds about 7.4 × 2.2 ≈ 16 Wh.

$$t_\text{runtime} \approx \frac{\text{capacity (Ah)}}{I_\text{load}\ (\text{A})}$$
a first estimate; real packs deliver less at high current and in the cold

i
A common failureA motor, heater or solenoid draws a large current spike, the shared battery sags, and the control electronics' supply dips below its brown-out threshold. The processor resets, data logging stops, and whatever it was doing restarts from the top. The fixes are separate batteries (or at least separate wiring) for high-current loads, a hold-up capacitor, and brown-out detection in firmware. Module 7 covers these in detail. One common example is a flight computer that shares a battery with a solenoid valve: it can reset the moment the valve opens.
Worked example · runtime

A 2200 mAh pack powers electronics drawing a steady 300 mA: \(t = 2200 / 300 \approx 7.3\ \text{h}\).

Plan on 70–80 % of that in practice, because of cold, ageing and the cutoff voltage.

Worked example · sag

A 2S pack with 40 mΩ per cell has \(2 \times 0.040 = 0.080\ \Omega\) inside it. A 5 A load sags it by \(5 \times 0.080 = 0.4\ \text{V}\).

A cold or old pack with three times the resistance sags 1.2 V.

Design exerciseSpec a pack that won't brown out

A motor and its control electronics share a 2S pack. Near the end of the pack's charge the cells are at 3.5 V each (7.0 V open circuit). Starting the motor draws 8 A, and the electronics' regulator needs at least 6.0 V at its input.

What's the highest internal resistance per cell the pack can have?

That's the limit for the whole pack. It has two cells in series, so each one gets half.

You can afford to lose 7.0 − 6.0 = 1.0 V at 8 A. What total resistance is that?

Allowed sag 1.0 V at 8 A gives \(R \le 1.0 / 8 = 125\ \text{m}\Omega\) total, so 62.5 mΩ per cell. A fresh pack at room temperature may be fine, but internal resistance rises sharply in the cold and with age. That's a strong argument for giving the motor its own battery.

✓
Measuring a module's current drawTo find how much a module on a board draws (a radio, say), break its supply line and put the meter (in amps mode) into the gap. Touching the probes across its power pins would short the rail. Good boards make this easy with a jumper or a 0 Ω resistor in each supply path, which you can lift to insert the meter. Don't try to work it out from voltage and a "resistance": a radio module isn't a resistor, and its current changes with what it's doing (transmitting vs idle).

Bench safety

  • Current is what hurts you, and voltage is what pushes it through you. Dry skin is a few tens of kilohms or more, so the 7.4 V of a 2S LiPo can't push a dangerous current through your body. Mains voltage and high-voltage supplies can, and need proper precautions.
  • Lithium batteries are the real hazard on a low-voltage bench. As the battery simulator showed, a short can draw hundreds of amps. That melts wires, welds tools and can start a fire. Insulate exposed leads, charge in a LiPo bag, and never charge unattended.
  • Use a current-limited bench supply for first power-up. Set the current limit just above what you expect. If something's shorted, the supply limits the current instead of the board burning. (More on this in Module 14.)
  • ESD: static from your body can kill sensitive chips without you feeling anything. Use the wrist strap at the bench.

Exercise 1Sizing · ●●○Sizing a battery

A remote sensor station runs a microcontroller board, a radio and a GPS receiver from one 2S LiPo. It has to run for 5 hours 10 minutes between charges.

Given Microcontroller board180 mA Radio (average)120 mA GPS receiver30 mA Usable fraction of capacity80 % (cold, cutoff voltage, ageing)

(a) Total current draw?

180 + 120 + 30 = 330 mA. All three loads hang off the same pack, so their currents add.

(b) Minimum rated capacity, accounting for the 80 % usable fraction?

That's the capacity actually used. The pack's rating has to be bigger, because only 80 % of it is usable.

Total time = 5 h 10 min ≈ 5.17 h. Capacity used = current × time. Then divide by 0.8.

Used: 0.33 A × 5.17 h ≈ 1.71 Ah. Rated: 1.71 / 0.8 ≈ 2.13 Ah, so you'd pick a 2200 mAh pack, or a bigger one if the weight budget allows.

Discussion: what this leaves out

Current isn't constant. The radio transmits in bursts at maybe 5× its average current, and those peaks cause sag (Section 6). Heaters, motors and solenoids add large short-term loads too. Low temperature cuts usable capacity further. A real power budget lists every load with its average and peak current; Module 7 builds one.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Does the solenoid get enough current?

A solenoid is 30 m from its supply: a 12 V lead-acid battery and a relay, connected to the solenoid by twin 22 AWG cable.

Given Battery12.0 V, internal resistance 0.05 Ω Relay contacts0.05 Ω Cable22 AWG, 52.9 mΩ per metre per conductor, 30 m one way Solenoid1.6 Ω, needs at least 1.0 A to pull in

(a) Total cable resistance (both conductors)?

That's one conductor. Current goes out and back.

60 m × 0.0529 Ω/m = 3.17 Ω. That's already twice the solenoid's resistance.

(b) Current through the solenoid?

Everything is in one loop, so add all the resistances: battery + relay + cable + solenoid.

\(R_\text{total} = 0.05 + 0.05 + 3.17 + 1.6 = 4.87\ \Omega\), so \(I = 12 / 4.87 = 2.46\ \text{A}\), about 2.5× the minimum it needs.

(c) Voltage actually across the solenoid?

\(V = I R = 2.46 \times 1.6 = 3.94\ \text{V}\). Only a third of the battery voltage reaches the solenoid. Most of the rest is lost in the cable.

Discussion: what would you change?

Swap in 16 AWG (13.2 mΩ/m) and the cable drops to 0.79 Ω, so the current rises to about 4.8 A. Or move the relay next to the solenoid and run only a low-current control line back to the supply. Also check the opposite limit: at 4.8 A the 1.6 Ω coil dissipates about 37 W. Solenoids are rated for a particular voltage or current, and running one far above it overheats the coil, so more current isn't automatically better.

Exercise 3Design · ●●●A shunt resistor to measure solenoid current

You want to log how much current a solenoid valve draws, so you can confirm it actually opened. The plan is to put a small "shunt" resistor in series with the valve and measure the voltage across it. To keep from stealing voltage from the valve, the shunt should drop no more than 100 mV at the valve's maximum current.

Given Maximum valve current2.0 A Maximum shunt voltage100 mV Derating50 % (rule of thumb)

(a) Largest shunt resistance you can use?

Right value, but in ohms. The box wants milliohms.

\(R = V/I = 0.1 / 2 = 0.05\ \Omega = 50\ \text{m}\Omega\).

(b) Power dissipated in the shunt at 2 A?

\(P = I^2R = 4 \times 0.05 = 0.2\ \text{W}\) (or \(V I = 0.1 \times 2\)). With 50 % derating, choose a part rated ≥ 0.4 W, for example a ½ W 1206 current-sense resistor.

Discussion: why not make the shunt bigger, to get a bigger signal?

A bigger shunt gives a bigger signal that's easier to measure, but it wastes more power, steals more voltage from the valve, and gets hotter. 100 mV is too small to feed straight into an ADC, so in Module 10 it's amplified with a current-sense amplifier. That's the standard trade-off: a small shunt plus gain.

MultimeterCurrent-limited bench supplyResistors: 47 Ω ¼ W, 100 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩAA cell + 10 Ω load5 m of thin hookup wireBreadboard
  1. Read and measure. Read each resistor's colour code, then measure it with power off. Is it within the tolerance band (often ±1 % or ±5 %)?
  2. Verify Ohm's law. Put the 1 kΩ across the supply. Step the voltage from 0 to 10 V in 2 V steps, measuring the current in series each time. Plot I against V. The slope should be 1/R.
  3. Feel the derating rule. Set the supply to 3.4 V across the 47 Ω ¼ W resistor (≈ 0.25 W). After a minute, check its temperature with an IR thermometer, or carefully with a fingertip. It will be hot.
  4. Measure internal resistance. Measure the AA cell's open-circuit voltage. Connect the 10 Ω load and measure again. Calculate \(R_\text{int} = (V_\text{oc} - V_\text{load}) / I\).
  5. The long-wire problem. Pass 1 A through 5 m of thin wire (current-limit the supply). Measure the voltage drop end to end and calculate the wire's resistance per metre. Compare it with the AWG table.
What you should see: a straight V–I line whose slope is 1/R, an AA cell with a few tenths of an ohm inside it, and a thin wire whose Ω/m matches the gauge table to within a few percent.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Current flows in loops and is the same everywhere in a single loop. Components use energy, not charge.
  2. Voltage is always between two points. "Ground" is just the agreed reference, and it's made of real copper with real resistance.
  3. Ohm's law \(V = IR\) and power \(P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R\) cover most everyday calculations. Derate parts to 50 % of their power rating.
  4. Wires and batteries have resistance. Long cables starve loads of current, and battery sag under heavy loads can reset your electronics.
  5. Voltmeters go across, ammeters go in series, ohmmeters need the power off. Never put an ammeter across a source.
