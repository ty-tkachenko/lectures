Voltage, current, resistance & power

Everything on the rocket, from the flight computer to the igniter, runs on four quantities and one simple law. These notes aim to make them feel physical, so you can look at a circuit and predict what it does before you measure it.

Current: charge on the move

Metal wires are full of electrons that are free to move. Connect a battery across a wire and they drift along it. Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point, measured in amperes (A). One amp is one coulomb of charge per second.

$$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$
current (A) = charge (C) ÷ time (s)

Two habits will save you a lot of confusion:

  • Current flows in loops. Charge leaves one terminal of the battery, goes through the circuit and returns to the other terminal. If the loop is broken anywhere (a loose crimp, an open switch, a blown fuse), current stops everywhere in that loop at once.
  • Current isn't used up. Whatever goes into a component comes out the other side. Components use energy, not charge.
i
Which way does it flow?Electrons actually drift from − to +. Long before anyone knew that, engineers agreed to draw current from + to −, which is called conventional current. Every schematic, datasheet and textbook uses conventional current, and so will we. The direction you pick doesn't change any answer, as long as you're consistent.
!
Common misconception: "the resistor uses up some of the current"Put a resistor and an LED in series across a battery, and it's tempting to think the LED gets less current because the resistor "used some". It doesn't. The resistor turns some energy into heat, but every electron that goes in comes out the other side. There's only one path, so the same current flows through everything in a single loop, whatever order the parts are in. That's the first of Kirchhoff's laws, which Module 2 builds on.

Voltage only exists between two points

Voltage is the energy each unit of charge gains or loses between two points, measured in volts (V). One volt is one joule per coulomb. A 7.4 V battery gives every coulomb that passes through it 7.4 joules, and the circuit takes that energy back out as heat, light, motion or radio waves.

The key word is between. "This pin is at 3.3 V" is shorthand for "this pin is 3.3 V above the point we agreed to call zero." That agreed point is called ground (GND). It's usually the battery's negative terminal, and it's drawn with this symbol:

DiagramVoltages are measured from a reference

A useful picture is a water system: voltage is like a pressure difference, current is the flow rate, and a resistor is a narrow pipe. Just as you can't talk about the pressure "at" a point without saying what it's relative to, you can't talk about voltage at a node without a reference. Don't push the analogy too far, though. Water can spill out of a pipe, but current can't leave a circuit, because it always returns home.

!
"Ground" is not the Earth, and it's not magicOn the rocket, ground is just a copper net that every circuit agrees to measure from. It has resistance like any other wire. When large currents flow through it (a solenoid valve opening, for example), different points on "ground" can sit at different voltages. That's behind a whole family of avionics bugs, and we'll come back to it in Modules 12 and 13.
Worked example

In the diagram, put the meter's red probe on node A and the black probe on node B. Both nodes are measured from the same ground, so the meter reads the difference:

\(V_{AB} = V_A - V_B = 5.0 - 3.3 = 1.7\ \text{V}\)

Swap the probes and it reads −1.7 V. The ground reference cancels out of the subtraction, so the voltage between two points doesn't depend on where you decided "zero" is.

Resistance and Ohm's law

A resistor opposes current. Push harder (more voltage) and more current flows. Make the path narrower (more resistance) and less current flows. For most conductors at a steady temperature, the relationship is a straight line:

$$V = I\,R \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad I = \frac{V}{R} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad R = \frac{V}{I}$$
Ohm's law: volts = amps × ohms

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω). You'll see a huge range: milliohms for wires and current-sense shunts, tens of ohms for igniters and solenoid coils, kilohms for pull-up resistors and voltage dividers, and megohms for a multimeter's input.

Worked example

A 3.3 V microcontroller pin drives a 1 kΩ resistor to ground. The pin supplies \(I = V/R = 3.3\ \text{V} / 1000\ \Omega = 3.3\ \text{mA}\).

A shortcut worth memorising: volts ÷ kilohms = milliamps. Most signal-level circuits live in kΩ and mA, so you'll use it constantly.

Design exerciseAn "armed" indicator LEDoptional

The pad box needs an LED that lights when the pyro channel is armed. It runs from 5 V. A red LED drops about 2 V across itself whenever it's on (you'll see why in Module 5), and you want 10 mA through it.

What series resistor sets 10 mA?

That would be right if the LED took no voltage. The resistor only sees what's left after the LED's 2 V.

The resistor and LED share the 5 V. How much is left across the resistor?

The resistor sees 5 − 2 = 3 V, so \(R = 3\ \text{V} / 10\ \text{mA} = 300\ \Omega\). The nearest standard value is 300 Ω (E24) or 330 Ω (E12), which gives about 9 mA. Plenty bright.

Power: where the energy goes

Every resistor turns electrical energy into heat. The rate at which it does so is the power, measured in watts (W), or joules per second. Multiply voltage (energy per charge) by current (charge per second) and you get energy per second:

$$P = V\,I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$
all three forms are the same equation, with Ohm's law substituted in

Use whichever form matches what you know. If you know the current through a wire, use \(I^2R\). If you know the voltage across a resistor, use \(V^2/R\).

Every resistor has a power rating: the most heat it can get rid of without damage. A common 0603 surface-mount resistor is rated ⅒ W, and a through-hole one is often ¼ W. Run a part at its full rating and it gets hot (often over 100 °C). The rating also assumes good airflow and room temperature, and an avionics bay sealed inside a sun-baked airframe has neither.

✓
Team rule of thumb: derate to 50 %Pick a part whose rating is at least twice the power you calculate. The same idea applies to voltage ratings on capacitors and current ratings on connectors. Derating is the cheapest reliability you can buy.
Worked example

A 100 Ω resistor sits directly across a 5 V rail. It dissipates \(P = V^2/R = 25/100 = 0.25\ \text{W}\).

A ¼ W resistor would technically be "within rating", but it would run at its maximum temperature all the time, and the rating assumes cooler conditions than an avionics bay. Applying the 50 % rule, you'd pick a ½ W part. Better still, ask whether the circuit really needs to burn a quarter of a watt at all. (And don't reach for a smaller package: smaller parts have less area to shed heat, so their ratings are lower.)

Design exerciseDoes the LED resistor fit in an 0402?optional

Back to the 300 Ω resistor carrying 10 mA in the arming indicator. The board is tight on space, and an 0402 resistor is rated 1/16 W (62.5 mW).

How much power does the resistor dissipate?

Right number, but in watts. The box wants milliwatts.

That's 5 V × 10 mA, the power of the whole circuit. The resistor only sees 3 V of it.

\(P = I^2R = (0.01)^2 \times 300 = 30\ \text{mW}\), or \(V I = 3\ \text{V} \times 10\ \text{mA}\). That's 48 % of the 0402's rating, just inside the 50 % rule, so it fits.

Wires aren't perfect: the igniter-cable problem

We usually draw wires as perfect conductors, and for a few centimetres of PCB trace carrying milliamps that's fine. But wire resistance grows with length and shrinks with cross-sectional area:

$$R_\text{wire} = \frac{\rho\,\ell}{A}$$
ρ is copper's resistivity (1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m), ℓ the length and A the cross-sectional area

Wire size is often given in AWG (American Wire Gauge). The numbering runs backwards: bigger numbers mean thinner wire. Every 3 gauges halves the area, so it doubles the resistance per metre.

🚀
Why this matters on the padFor safety, the igniter is fired from far away, so there can be tens of metres of cable between the firing box and the pad. The igniter needs a minimum all-fire current to light reliably. Remember that current makes a round trip, out along one conductor and back along the other, so the cable's resistance counts twice. Too thin or too long a cable, and the igniter just sits there while you hold.

!
Safety firstThe numbers here are examples. Always use the all-fire and no-fire currents from your igniter's datasheet, and follow your club's pyro and arming procedures. Continuity testing and arming are covered in Module 6.
Field story

An igniter fires every time on the bench with a 1 m lead, then sits there doing nothing on the pad. Same firing box, same batch of igniters. The difference is the cable. A 1 m lead adds almost no resistance, but tens of metres of thin wire can add several ohms, more than the igniter itself. The current falls below the all-fire level.

Note what didn't happen: the voltage didn't "run out" along the cable. Voltage gets divided between every resistance in the loop, and a long cable simply takes a big share.

Design exerciseHow long can the launch cable be?optional

The firing box supplies 12 V. The igniter is 1.6 Ω with an all-fire current of 1 A, and you want a 2× margin (2 A). The cable is 22 AWG twin, 52.9 mΩ per metre per conductor. Ignore the battery's and relay's own resistance.

What's the longest one-way cable length that still delivers 2 A?

That's the total length of copper, out and back. The one-way length is half.

At 2 A the whole loop can be at most 12 / 2 = 6 Ω. Subtract the igniter, and what's left is the cable's budget.

Loop budget: 6 Ω. Cable budget: 6 − 1.6 = 4.4 Ω for both conductors, so 2.2 Ω each. Length: \(2.2 / 0.0529 \approx 41.6\ \text{m}\) one way. If the pad needs to be further away, go to thicker wire or move the relay closer. You can check this in the simulator above.

Batteries: capacity, energy and internal resistance

A battery isn't a perfect voltage source. A good model is an ideal voltage source in series with a small internal resistance. The more current you draw, the more voltage is lost inside the battery, and the lower the terminal voltage sags:

$$V_\text{terminal} = V_\text{open circuit} - I\,R_\text{internal}$$

Battery capacity is usually given in milliamp-hours (mAh): a 2200 mAh pack can supply 2.2 A for one hour, or 220 mA for ten hours. Energy is capacity × voltage, so a 2S (two-cell, 7.4 V) 2200 mAh pack holds about 7.4 × 2.2 ≈ 16 Wh.

$$t_\text{runtime} \approx \frac{\text{capacity (Ah)}}{I_\text{load}\ (\text{A})}$$
a first estimate; real packs deliver less at high current and in the cold

🚀
A classic avionics failureA valve or igniter draws a big current spike, the shared battery sags, and the flight computer's supply dips below its brown-out threshold. The computer resets, the data logging stops, and the valve sequence restarts from the top. The fixes are separate batteries (or at least separate wiring) for high-current loads, a hold-up capacitor, and brown-out detection in firmware. You'll design these properly in Module 7.
Worked example · runtime

A 2200 mAh pack powers avionics drawing a steady 300 mA: \(t = 2200 / 300 \approx 7.3\ \text{h}\).

Plan on 70–80 % of that in practice, because of cold, ageing and the cutoff voltage.

Worked example · sag

A 2S pack with 40 mΩ per cell has \(2 \times 0.040 = 0.080\ \Omega\) inside it. A 5 A valve sags it by \(5 \times 0.080 = 0.4\ \text{V}\).

A cold or old pack with three times the resistance sags 1.2 V.

Design exerciseSpec a pack that won't brown outoptional

The main valve and the flight computer share a 2S pack. Near the end of a long pad hold the cells are at 3.5 V each (7.0 V open circuit). Opening the valve draws 8 A, and the computer's regulator needs at least 6.0 V at its input.

What's the highest internal resistance per cell the pack can have?

That's the limit for the whole pack. It has two cells in series, so each one gets half.

You can afford to lose 7.0 − 6.0 = 1.0 V at 8 A. What total resistance is that?

Allowed sag 1.0 V at 8 A gives \(R \le 1.0 / 8 = 125\ \text{m}\Omega\) total, so 62.5 mΩ per cell. A fresh pack at room temperature may be fine, but internal resistance rises sharply in the cold and with age. That's a strong argument for giving the valve its own battery.

Measuring a module's current drawTo find how much the GPS draws, break its supply line and put the meter (in amps mode) into the gap. Touching the probes across its power pins would short the rail. Good boards make this easy with a jumper or a 0 Ω resistor in each supply path, which you can lift to insert the meter. Don't try to work it out from voltage and a "resistance": a GPS module isn't a resistor, and its current changes with what it's doing (acquiring vs tracking).

Staying safe at the bench

  • Current is what hurts you, and voltage is what pushes it through you. Dry skin is a few tens of kilohms or more, so the 7.4 V on the rocket can't push a dangerous current through your body. Mains voltage and high-voltage supplies can, so treat them with respect.
  • LiPos are the real hazard on our bench. As the battery simulator showed, a short can draw hundreds of amps. That melts wires, welds tools and can start a fire. Insulate exposed leads, charge in a LiPo bag, and never charge unattended.
  • Pyro channels are always treated as live. Follow the club's arming procedure, use shunts on igniters, and never be in line with a motor or igniter while connecting.
  • Use a current-limited bench supply for first power-up. Set the current limit just above what you expect. If something's shorted, the supply limits the current instead of the board burning. (More on this in Module 14.)
  • ESD: static from your body can kill sensitive chips without you feeling anything. Use the wrist strap at the bench.

Exercise 1Sizing · ●●○How big a battery for the pad hold?

The flight computer, radio and GPS run from one 2S LiPo. The launch window allows up to a 3-hour hold on the pad. After a 10-minute flight, recovery can take up to 2 hours, and you want the radio beacon running the whole time.

Given Flight computer180 mA Telemetry radio (average)120 mA GPS30 mA Usable fraction of capacity80 % (cold, cutoff voltage, ageing)

(a) Total current draw?

180 + 120 + 30 = 330 mA. All three loads hang off the same pack, so their currents add.

(b) Minimum rated capacity, accounting for the 80 % usable fraction?

That's the capacity you'll actually use. The pack's rating has to be bigger, because only 80 % of it is usable.

Total time = 3 h + 10 min + 2 h ≈ 5.17 h. Capacity used = current × time. Then divide by 0.8.

Used: 0.33 A × 5.17 h ≈ 1.71 Ah. Rated: 1.71 / 0.8 ≈ 2.13 Ah, so you'd pick a 2200 mAh pack, or a bigger one if the weight budget allows.

Discussion: what did we leave out?

Current isn't constant. The radio transmits in bursts at maybe 5× its average current, and those peaks cause sag (Section 6). Heaters, servos and valves add large short-term loads too. Low temperature at altitude cuts usable capacity further. A real power budget lists every load with its average and peak current, and that's exactly what you'll build in Module 7.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Will the igniter fire through the launch cable?

The firing box is 30 m from the pad. It uses a 12 V lead-acid battery and a relay, connected to the igniter by twin 22 AWG cable.

Given Battery12.0 V, internal resistance 0.05 Ω Relay contacts0.05 Ω Cable22 AWG, 52.9 mΩ per metre per conductor, 30 m one way Igniter1.6 Ω, all-fire current 1.0 A (example)

(a) Total cable resistance (both conductors)?

That's one conductor. Current goes out and back.

60 m × 0.0529 Ω/m = 3.17 Ω. That's already twice the igniter's resistance.

(b) Firing current?

Everything is in one loop, so add all the resistances: battery + relay + cable + igniter.

\(R_\text{total} = 0.05 + 0.05 + 3.17 + 1.6 = 4.87\ \Omega\), so \(I = 12 / 4.87 = 2.46\ \text{A}\), about 2.5× the all-fire current.

(c) Voltage actually across the igniter?

\(V = I R = 2.46 \times 1.6 = 3.94\ \text{V}\). Only a third of the battery voltage reaches the igniter. Most of the rest is lost in the cable.

Discussion: what would you change?

Swap in 16 AWG (13.2 mΩ/m) and the cable drops to 0.79 Ω, so the current rises to about 4.1 A. Or move the relay box to the pad and run only a low-current control line back to the firing box. Also check the opposite limit: the no-fire current. Your continuity-test current has to stay well below it, or the continuity check itself becomes a hazard.

Exercise 3Design · ●●●A shunt resistor to measure valve current

You want the flight computer to log how much current the main oxidizer valve draws, so you can confirm it actually opened. The plan is to put a small "shunt" resistor in series with the valve and measure the voltage across it. To keep from stealing voltage from the valve, the shunt should drop no more than 100 mV at the valve's maximum current.

Given Maximum valve current2.0 A Maximum shunt voltage100 mV Derating50 % (team rule)

(a) Largest shunt resistance you can use?

Right value, but in ohms. The box wants milliohms.

\(R = V/I = 0.1 / 2 = 0.05\ \Omega = 50\ \text{m}\Omega\).

(b) Power dissipated in the shunt at 2 A?

\(P = I^2R = 4 \times 0.05 = 0.2\ \text{W}\) (or \(V I = 0.1 \times 2\)). With 50 % derating, choose a part rated ≥ 0.4 W, for example a ½ W 1206 current-sense resistor.

Discussion: why not make the shunt bigger, to get a bigger signal?

A bigger shunt gives a bigger signal that's easier to measure, but it wastes more power, steals more voltage from the valve, and gets hotter. 100 mV is too small to feed straight into an ADC, so in Module 10 you'll amplify it with a current-sense amplifier. That's the standard trade-off: a small shunt plus gain.

MultimeterCurrent-limited bench supplyResistors: 47 Ω ¼ W, 100 Ω, 1 kΩ, 10 kΩAA cell + 10 Ω load5 m of thin hookup wireBreadboard
  1. Read and measure. Read each resistor's colour code, then measure it with power off. Is it within the tolerance band (often ±1 % or ±5 %)?
  2. Verify Ohm's law. Put the 1 kΩ across the supply. Step the voltage from 0 to 10 V in 2 V steps, measuring the current in series each time. Plot I against V. The slope should be 1/R.
  3. Feel the derating rule. Set the supply to 3.4 V across the 47 Ω ¼ W resistor (≈ 0.25 W). After a minute, check its temperature with an IR thermometer, or carefully with a fingertip. Hot.
  4. Measure internal resistance. Measure the AA cell's open-circuit voltage. Connect the 10 Ω load and measure again. Calculate \(R_\text{int} = (V_\text{oc} - V_\text{load}) / I\).
  5. The long-wire problem. Pass 1 A through 5 m of thin wire (current-limit the supply!). Measure the voltage drop end to end and calculate the wire's resistance per metre. Compare it with the AWG table.
What you should see: a straight V–I line whose slope is 1/R, an AA cell with a few tenths of an ohm inside it, and a thin wire whose Ω/m matches the gauge table to within a few percent.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. Current flows in loops and is the same everywhere in a single loop. Components use energy, not charge.
  2. Voltage is always between two points. "Ground" is just the agreed reference, and it's made of real copper with real resistance.
  3. Ohm's law \(V = IR\) and power \(P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R\) cover most everyday calculations. Derate parts to 50 % of their power rating.
  4. Wires and batteries have resistance. Long cables starve igniters, and battery sag under heavy loads can reset your electronics.
  5. Voltmeters go across, ammeters go in series, ohmmeters need the power off. Never put an ammeter across a source.
