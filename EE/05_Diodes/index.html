Diodes

A diode lets current flow one way and blocks it the other. That single property protects circuits from batteries connected backwards, turns AC into DC, clamps the voltage spike from a switched coil, lights indicators, absorbs electrostatic discharge, and lets two batteries share a load safely. This module explains how diodes behave, including the voltage they drop and the heat that costs, and how to choose one for each of these jobs.

The diode as a one-way valve

A diode has two terminals: the anode and the cathode. Conventional current flows easily from anode to cathode, in the direction the arrow in its symbol points, and is blocked in the other direction. A diode is said to be forward biased when its anode is more positive than its cathode, and reverse biased the other way round. On the part itself, a painted band marks the cathode.

DiagramThe diode: symbol, packages, and what's inside

Inside, a diode is a junction between two kinds of silicon. P-type silicon has impurities added that leave it with mobile positive charge carriers (missing electrons, called holes); N-type has impurities that provide extra mobile electrons. Where they meet, electrons and holes near the boundary combine and leave a thin depletion region with no mobile carriers, which acts as an insulator. Its charged edges set up a built-in voltage of about 0.6–0.7 V in silicon. Applying a forward voltage larger than that pushes carriers across, and current flows; applying a reverse voltage widens the depletion region, and almost nothing flows.

You've already met one essential use in Module 3: the flyback diode across a switched coil, which gives the coil's current a path when the switch opens instead of letting it generate a destructive voltage spike. Much of this module covers other ways of using that one-way behaviour.

The forward drop and the I–V curve

A diode isn't a perfect switch. In the forward direction, its current rises exponentially with voltage, following the Shockley equation:

$$I = I_S\left(e^{V/(nV_T)} - 1\right) \qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 26\ \text{mV at room temperature}$$
IS: a tiny saturation current, set by the device · n: between 1 and 2

Two practical consequences follow. First, because the curve is so steep, a forward-biased diode's voltage stays nearly constant over a wide range of current: each tenfold increase in current adds only about 60–120 mV. That's why a silicon diode is often described as "dropping about 0.7 V" when it conducts. Second, because the rise is exponential, a diode must never be connected directly across a voltage source: a little more voltage means vastly more current. Something else in the circuit, usually a resistor, has to set the current.

Different diode types have different drops at the same current. Schottky diodes, made from a metal–semiconductor junction, drop 0.2–0.45 V, which makes them the choice wherever the drop costs power, at the price of higher reverse leakage. LEDs drop 1.8–3.3 V depending on their colour. And the forward drop of every type falls by about 2 mV for each degree of temperature rise, which means a hot diode conducts more easily.

Application

Most multimeters have a diode-test range, which pushes a small current (about 1 mA) through the part and displays the voltage. A good silicon diode reads 0.5–0.7 V one way and "OL" (open) the other; a Schottky about 0.2–0.3 V; a shorted diode near 0 V both ways; an open one "OL" both ways. The same range lights most LEDs faintly, which is the quickest way to find an LED's polarity, and it's also a quick check of a transistor's internal junctions (Module 6).

Design exerciseTwo diodes in parallel for more current

A diode rated for 1 A must carry 1.6 A, so a designer connects two identical diodes in parallel, expecting each to carry 0.8 A.

What actually happens?

  1. Each carries exactly 0.8 A, since they're identical.
    "Identical" parts differ slightly, and the exponential curve magnifies small differences.
  2. One diode, with a slightly lower forward drop, takes most of the current, heats up, and its drop falls further, so it takes even more. The current doesn't share evenly, and the hotter diode can be overloaded.
    A small voltage difference is a large current ratio on an exponential curve, and the negative temperature coefficient makes it worse.
  3. The current alternates between the two diodes.
    Nothing makes them take turns.
  4. The two diodes cancel each other out.
    They're in parallel and in the same direction, so both conduct.

A difference of 20 mV in forward drop is a current ratio of about 2:1 on a typical diode curve, and the diode carrying more current gets hotter, which lowers its drop (−2 mV/°C) and makes it take more still. Paralleling diodes needs a small resistor in series with each to force sharing, or better, a single diode rated for the full current.

Rectifiers

A rectifier turns AC into DC. The simplest, a half-wave rectifier, is one diode in series with the load: it passes the positive half-cycles and blocks the negative ones. A bridge rectifier uses four diodes arranged so that both half-cycles drive current through the load in the same direction, at the cost of two diode drops instead of one.

DiagramHalf-wave and bridge rectifiers, and the current path in each half-cycle

The rectified output is a series of humps, not a steady voltage. A smoothing capacitor across the load charges to the peak of each hump and supplies the load in between, discharging slowly. The output then sits near the peak with a small sawtooth ripple. The ripple follows directly from Module 3's ΔV = IΔt/C: the capacitor supplies the load current I for roughly one period between peaks,

$$\Delta V_\text{ripple} \approx \frac{I}{f_\text{ripple}\,C}$$
fripple = f for half-wave, 2f for a bridge

The capacitor recharges only during the short time near each peak when the input exceeds its voltage, so the diodes carry the whole period's charge in brief, high-current pulses, often five to ten times the average load current. Diodes for rectifiers are rated for this repetitive peak current as well as the average.

Application

A transformer, bridge and capacitor were for decades the inside of every mains adapter; switching supplies have mostly replaced them, but the same rectifier still sits at their input. Rectification also appears without any transformer: a small brushed motor or alternator driven mechanically produces AC that a bridge turns into DC, and a bridge on a power input makes a circuit work whichever way round its supply is connected, at the cost of two diode drops.

LEDs and current limiting

A light-emitting diode is a diode made from a semiconductor whose electrons give up their energy as light when they cross the junction. The colour depends on the material, and so does the forward drop: the energy of each photon comes from the voltage across the junction, so blue light (more energy per photon) needs a higher voltage than red.

ColourTypical forward dropNotes
Infrared1.2–1.5 VRemote controls, optical sensors, optocouplers
Red, orange, yellow1.8–2.2 VEasiest to drive from 3.3 V
Green (modern), blue, white2.8–3.3 VMarginal or impossible from a 3.3 V supply through a resistor

Brightness follows current, not voltage, and because of the exponential I–V curve, an LED's current can't be set with a voltage directly. A series resistor converts the remaining voltage into a defined current:

$$R = \frac{V_\text{supply} - V_F}{I_\text{LED}}$$
indicator LEDs are clearly visible at 1–5 mA; their maximum is usually 20–30 mA
DiagramDriving an indicator LED from a supply or a logic output

Microcontroller pins can supply only limited current, typically a few milliamps to about 20 mA per pin and less in total across a port, so indicator LEDs are run at a few milliamps. The sinking arrangement on the right is useful when the LED's supply is higher than the logic's, or when the pin is open-drain (Module 8).

Design exerciseA blue LED on 3.3 V

A blue status LED with a 3.0 V typical forward drop is driven from a 3.3 V logic output through a 100 Ω resistor. Some boards' LEDs are bright, some are dim, and some are almost off, and they get dimmer as the battery runs down.

Why?

  1. The LEDs are faulty.
    The circuit itself is the problem.
  2. Only about 0.3 V is left across the resistor, so the current depends on tiny differences in forward drop and output voltage. A 0.1 V variation changes the current by a third.
    With so little headroom, the resistor can't define the current.
  3. The resistor is too large.
    A smaller resistor would make the variation in current even larger in absolute terms.
  4. Blue LEDs need AC.
    LEDs are DC devices.

The resistor sets the current only if the voltage across it is a substantial, well-defined part of the supply. Here (3.3 − 3.0)/100 = 3 mA nominally, but the forward drop varies by ±0.1–0.2 V between parts and with temperature, and the pin's high level sags under load, so the current ranges from near zero to over 5 mA. Use a red or yellow LED (1 V or more across the resistor), drive the blue LED from a higher supply through a transistor, or use a constant-current driver.

Zener and TVS diodes

Push enough reverse voltage across any diode and it breaks down: the field across the depletion region becomes strong enough to pull carriers through, and the reverse current rises steeply. In an ordinary diode this is a failure mode. A Zener diode is built to break down at a precise, specified voltage, from about 2.4 V to over 100 V, and to survive doing so continuously. Reverse-biased beyond its Zener voltage VZ, it holds its voltage close to VZ over a wide range of current, just as a forward-biased diode holds about 0.7 V. You can see this in the I–V figure above by reversing the Zener.

The Zener shunt regulator

A resistor from a higher supply and a Zener to ground make the simplest voltage regulator. The resistor sets a current; the Zener takes whatever part of it the load doesn't, holding the output near VZ. It's inefficient, because the resistor and Zener burn power continuously, and it only works while the resistor can supply the full load current with some left over for the Zener. It's used as a reference voltage, a low-current supply, or a clamp, rarely as a main supply (Module 7 covers proper regulators).

TVS diodes: absorbing transients

A TVS (transient voltage suppressor) diode is a breakdown diode built for a different job: absorbing large, brief pulses of energy, such as electrostatic discharge from a finger, a voltage spike on a long cable, or an inductive load switching off elsewhere on the same supply. It does nothing in normal operation, because its standoff voltage is above the circuit's working voltage. When a transient pushes the voltage beyond its breakdown, it conducts heavily and holds the voltage at its clamping voltage, diverting the surge current to ground. TVS diodes come in unidirectional and bidirectional forms and are rated by the peak pulse power they can absorb.

DiagramA TVS diode clamping a transient at a connector

Placement decides whether a TVS works. It must sit right at the connector, with a short, direct path to ground, so the surge current is diverted before it travels along the board; a few centimetres of trace have enough inductance (Module 3) to let a fast spike pass the TVS before it can react. A small resistor after the TVS, before a sensitive input, limits whatever current still gets through.

Application

Any signal that leaves the board on a cable, such as a sensor input wired through a harness, is exposed to ESD when the connector is handled and to spikes coupled from other wiring in the harness. A common protection for an analog input to an ADC is a TVS (or a low-capacitance ESD diode) at the connector, a series resistor of a few hundred ohms to a few kilohms, and the ADC's own internal clamp diodes as the last line. The series resistor matters because it limits the current into the ADC's clamp diodes to a level they can survive.

Design exerciseA TVS with too low a standoff voltage

A 12 V supply input is protected with a TVS whose standoff voltage is 12 V and whose breakdown voltage is 13.3 V. The supply is a 3-cell lithium battery that reads 12.6 V when fully charged.

What's the problem?

  1. None: 12.6 V is below the breakdown voltage.
    It's close: the breakdown voltage has a tolerance, falls at low temperature for many parts, and leakage rises steeply as it's approached.
  2. The battery's full-charge voltage exceeds the TVS's standoff voltage, so the TVS leaks significant current, may run warm and drain the battery, and any tolerance or charger overshoot can push it into conduction.
    The standoff voltage must be above the highest normal operating voltage, with margin.
  3. The TVS will make the voltage too low for the circuit.
    A TVS doesn't drop voltage in normal operation; the issue is leakage near breakdown.
  4. TVS diodes can't be used on power inputs.
    They're used on power inputs all the time, chosen for the right voltage and power.

The standoff voltage is the highest voltage at which the TVS stays essentially non-conducting; it must exceed the maximum normal voltage, here 12.6 V plus any charger overshoot. A part with a 15 V standoff is the usual choice for a 12 V nominal input. The trade-off is that its clamping voltage is higher too, so the downstream circuit must tolerate that clamping voltage for the duration of a transient.

Reverse-polarity protection

A battery connected backwards drives current the wrong way through every part on the board, and many parts, especially regulators and microcontrollers with internal protection diodes, are destroyed within milliseconds. Connectors are keyed to prevent it, but keys break, test leads get swapped, and batteries are hand-wired. A diode in the supply path makes the circuit immune, and there are three common ways to arrange it:

DiagramThree ways to protect against a reversed supply
MethodLoss at 2 ANotes
Series silicon diode0.8 V × 2 A = 1.6 WSimple; the drop also reduces the voltage available to the circuit
Series Schottky diode0.45 V × 2 A = 0.9 WThe usual choice for small currents; watch its reverse leakage when hot
P-channel MOSFET2² × 0.02 Ω = 0.08 WAlmost lossless; the MOSFET's body diode gets it started (Module 6)
Fuse and shunt diodeThe fuse's resistance onlyNo loss in normal use, but a reversal destroys the fuse, which must then be replaced

The loss scales with current, so the right answer depends on the circuit: a Schottky diode for a sensor drawing 50 mA, a MOSFET for a board drawing amps from a battery whose every volt and watt matters.

Design exerciseProtection that causes a brown-out

A board runs from a single lithium cell (3.0–4.2 V) through a 3.3 V regulator with 0.2 V of dropout. A series silicon diode is added for reverse-polarity protection.

What's the consequence?

  1. None: the diode only matters if the battery is reversed.
    The diode is in the current path all the time, dropping voltage whenever the board runs.
  2. The diode drops about 0.7 V, so the regulator needs 3.3 + 0.2 + 0.7 = 4.2 V at its input side of the diode; the 3.3 V rail falls out of regulation almost as soon as the battery starts to discharge.
    On a low-voltage supply, a diode's drop is a large fraction of the headroom.
  3. The board draws more current.
    The current is set by the load; the diode wastes voltage, not current.
  4. The regulator will oscillate.
    The diode doesn't affect the regulator's stability.

On a 3.0–4.2 V cell, there's almost no headroom: even a Schottky's 0.3–0.4 V costs a large part of the battery's usable capacity. This is the classic case for a P-channel MOSFET, whose drop is a few millivolts at these currents.

Diode-OR of redundant supplies

When two supplies, for example two batteries or an external supply and an internal battery, must both be able to power a load, connecting them directly together is dangerous: the higher one drives current into the lower, which can be large enough to damage a battery. Feeding the load through a diode from each makes a diode-OR. Each diode conducts only when its source is the higher one, so the load takes power from whichever source is higher, and no current can flow from one source back into the other.

The figure shows a subtlety: with real diodes, the load doesn't switch abruptly from one source to the other. When the two voltages are within a few tens of millivolts, both diodes conduct and the current divides according to the exponential curve; as one battery discharges below the other, the load hands over smoothly to the higher one. Each battery discharges in turn rather than together.

Application

A common use of a diode-OR is switching between an external supply and an internal battery. A vehicle on the launch pad is often powered through an umbilical cable, with its internal battery taking over when the umbilical disconnects. If the umbilical supply is set slightly higher than the battery, a diode-OR draws all the power from the umbilical while it's connected, keeps the battery from being back-fed, and hands over to the battery without interruption at disconnection. At several amps, the diode losses justify ideal-diode controllers, and some designs add a dedicated power-path controller with charging (Module 7).

Design exerciseConnecting two batteries directly

To double the capacity, two 2-cell lithium batteries are connected directly in parallel. One is at 8.2 V, fully charged; the other at 7.4 V, half discharged. Each has about 50 mΩ of internal resistance.

What happens at the moment they're connected?

  1. They settle at the average voltage with only a small current.
    The voltage difference is driven through very little resistance.
  2. A current of about (8.2 − 7.4)/0.1 = 8 A flows from the full battery into the half-empty one until they equalise, enough to spark at the connector and stress both packs.
    Module 1's internal resistance is all that limits the current.
  3. Nothing flows until a load is connected.
    The two batteries form a closed loop with each other.
  4. The lower battery's voltage rises instantly to 8.2 V.
    The current flows until the voltages converge, which takes time.

With only the two internal resistances (0.1 Ω together) in the loop, 0.8 V of difference drives about 8 A. That's why batteries are only paralleled when matched in charge, through a resistor while they equalise, or through a diode-OR or ideal-diode controller that prevents current flowing from one into the other.

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Design · ●○○An indicator LED

A red status LED (VF = 2.0 V) is driven from a 3.3 V logic output at 5 mA.

(a) What resistor value is needed?

That ignores the LED's forward drop. Only the remaining voltage, 3.3 − 2.0, appears across the resistor.

R = (3.3 − 2.0)/0.005 = 260 Ω. The nearest standard E12 value is 270 Ω, giving 4.8 mA.

(b) How much power does the resistor dissipate?

P = I²R = 0.005² × 260 = 6.5 mW: tiny, so any small resistor will do.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Rectifier ripple

A bridge rectifier runs from 50 Hz mains through a transformer and feeds a 0.5 A load. The smoothing capacitor is 2200 µF.

(a) Estimate the peak-to-peak ripple.

That uses 50 Hz. A bridge produces two humps per cycle, so the ripple frequency is 100 Hz.

ΔV ≈ I/(frippleC), with fripple = 2 × 50 Hz.

ΔV = 0.5/(100 × 0.0022) = 2.27 V.

(b) What capacitance would halve the ripple?

Ripple is inversely proportional to C, so twice the capacitance: about 4500 µF (4700 µF in practice).

Discussion: the hidden cost

A larger capacitor lowers the ripple but shortens the time each cycle during which the diodes conduct, so the same charge arrives in shorter, higher pulses. The diodes' repetitive peak current rating, the transformer's heating and the capacitor's ripple-current rating all need checking.

Exercise 3Design · ●●○Reverse-polarity protection on a 2 A board

A board draws 2 A from a battery. Two protection options are being compared: a Schottky diode with a 0.45 V drop at 2 A, and a P-channel MOSFET with 15 mΩ of on-resistance.

(a) Power lost in the Schottky diode?

0.45 × 2 = 0.9 W, enough to need a power package and some copper area to stay cool.

(b) Power lost in the MOSFET?

P = I²R.

2² × 0.015 = 0.06 W, fifteen times less, with a drop of only 30 mV.

Exercise 4Design · ●●●A Zener regulator

A 5.1 V Zener regulator runs from an input that varies from 10 V to 14 V and supplies a load of up to 20 mA. The Zener needs at least 5 mA to stay in regulation.

(a) What's the largest series resistor that still regulates at the worst case?

The worst case is the lowest input with the highest load: the resistor must pass 20 + 5 mA with 10 − 5.1 V across it.

R = (10 − 5.1)/0.025 = 196 Ω. Choose the next lower standard value, 180 Ω.

(b) With 180 Ω, how much power does the Zener dissipate at 14 V with no load?

Current = (14 − 5.1)/180 = 49 mA, all through the Zener: 5.1 × 0.049 = 0.25 W. A 500 mW Zener is fine; a 200 mW part would not be.

Discussion: why this is rarely the main supply

At 14 V and full load, the resistor dissipates (8.9)²/180 = 0.44 W and the circuit delivers 0.1 W to the load: under 15 % efficient. A linear regulator does better and a switching regulator much better (Module 7). The Zener regulator earns its place as a simple reference or a clamp, where the currents are small.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A diode conducts from anode to cathode (towards the band) and blocks the other way. Its current rises exponentially with forward voltage, so something else must set the current.
  2. Forward drop: about 0.7 V for silicon, 0.2–0.45 V for Schottky, 1.8–3.3 V for LEDs, falling by about 2 mV/°C. The drop times the current is heat.
  3. Rectifiers turn AC into DC; a smoothing capacitor reduces the ripple to about I/(fC), with the diodes conducting in short, high-current pulses.
  4. An LED needs a series resistor (or a current source); make sure enough voltage is left across it.
  5. A Zener holds its breakdown voltage; a TVS absorbs transients above its standoff voltage. Place a TVS at the connector with a short path to ground.
  6. Protect against reversed batteries with a series Schottky at low current, or a P-MOSFET when the drop or the heat matters.
  7. A diode-OR lets two supplies share a load without back-feeding each other; the higher one supplies it.
