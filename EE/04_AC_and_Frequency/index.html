AC signals & frequency response

Most signals change with time: a sensor output with vibration on it, a supply rail with switching noise, a radio carrier. This module introduces the tools for describing them: sine waves, phase, RMS, and impedance, which extends Ohm's law to capacitors and inductors. With those, you can predict what a circuit does to each frequency in a signal, design a filter, and recognise the resonance that makes some circuits ring.

Builds on: EE modules 1–3. Bench: function generator, oscilloscope, resistors, capacitors and an inductor.

Sine waves: amplitude, frequency and phase

Modules 1 to 3 dealt mostly with steady (DC) voltages and single switching events. An AC (alternating) signal changes continuously, and the reference shape is the sine wave:

$$v(t) = A\sin(2\pi f t + \varphi)$$
A: amplitude (peak value) · f: frequency in hertz (cycles per second) · φ: phase

The period T = 1/f is the time for one cycle: 20 ms for 50 Hz mains, 1 ms for a 1 kHz tone, about 1 ns for a 915 MHz radio carrier. The quantity 2πf appears so often that it gets its own name, the angular frequency ω = 2πf, in radians per second.

DiagramThe parts of a sine wave

Phase describes where in its cycle a wave is at a given moment, as an angle: a full cycle is 360°. Phase only becomes meaningful when two waves of the same frequency are compared. If one is delayed by a time Δt relative to the other, it lags by a phase of

$$\Delta\varphi = 360^\circ \times f\,\Delta t$$

The same 1 ms delay is a small 18° at 50 Hz, a full cycle (360°) at 1 kHz, and many cycles at higher frequencies. Phase is therefore delay measured in fractions of a cycle, which is why it matters more and more as frequency rises.

Every signal is a sum of sines

The reason sine waves get so much attention is a result due to Fourier: any repeating signal, however sharp-edged, can be built by adding sine waves at the fundamental frequency and its integer multiples, the harmonics, each with the right amplitude and phase. A square wave, for example, is

$$v(t) = \frac{4A}{\pi}\left(\sin\omega t + \tfrac13\sin 3\omega t + \tfrac15\sin 5\omega t + \cdots\right)$$
odd harmonics only, each weaker in proportion to its harmonic number

This is what makes the rest of the module work. If a circuit's effect on a single sine wave is known at every frequency, its effect on any signal follows: split the signal into sines, let the circuit treat each one, and add them back up. The circuit's response to each frequency is its frequency response.

The figure shows why sharp edges and high frequencies go together: the steep edges are made by the high harmonics. Remove them, as any circuit with limited bandwidth does, and the edges slow down. Conversely, a signal with fast edges, such as a microcontroller's clock or a switching regulator's switch node, contains energy at frequencies many times its repetition rate, which is how a 1 MHz switching regulator can interfere with a 100 MHz radio.

RMS

An AC voltage's average is zero, so averaging isn't a useful way to say how "big" it is. The question that matters is how much power it delivers. A resistor dissipates v²/R at each instant, so the average power depends on the average of v². The RMS (root mean square) value is the steady DC voltage that would deliver the same average power:

$$V_\text{RMS} = \sqrt{\overline{v(t)^2}} \qquad P = \frac{V_\text{RMS}^2}{R}$$
for a sine wave, VRMS = A/√2 ≈ 0.707 A

Mains voltages are quoted as RMS: 230 V mains has a peak of 325 V, and 120 V mains a peak of 170 V. Components connected to them have to withstand the peak, not the RMS value.

The √2 only applies to sine waves. Many inexpensive multimeters measure the average of the rectified waveform and multiply by 1.11, the ratio that's correct for a sine; on any other waveform they read wrongly. A true-RMS meter computes the actual RMS value and reads correctly for any shape within its bandwidth.

Application

Heaters, motors and solenoid valves are often driven with PWM (pulse-width modulation): switched fully on and off at a fixed frequency, with the fraction of time on setting the average. For a resistive heater, the power follows the RMS value: at 25 % duty from a 12 V supply, VRMS = 12√0.25 = 6 V, so the heater receives a quarter of its full power. A multimeter on its DC range reads the average, 3 V, which would wrongly suggest a sixteenth of full power if used in V²/R.

Design exerciseRating a capacitor for mains

A capacitor is to be connected directly across 230 V RMS mains.

What voltage must it withstand, at minimum, in normal operation?

  1. 230 V, the mains voltage.
    230 V is the RMS value; the voltage swings higher than that every cycle.
  2. About 325 V, the peak of the sine wave, plus margin for mains transients.
    The peak is √2 × 230 V. Capacitors for mains use are also specifically rated (X and Y classes) to survive surges.
  3. 460 V, twice the RMS value.
    The peak is √2 times the RMS value, not twice.
  4. 163 V, the RMS value divided by √2.
    That divides where it should multiply.

RMS describes heating power; the insulation sees the instantaneous voltage, which peaks at √2 × 230 ≈ 325 V every cycle. Mains also carries transients of a kilovolt or more, which is why capacitors connected to mains must be safety-rated types, not ordinary capacitors rated at 400 V.

Impedance of R, L and C

Module 3 described capacitors and inductors with rules about change: i = C dv/dt and v = L di/dt. With a sine-wave voltage, those rules give a neat result, and it's the key to analysing any AC circuit.

Apply v = A sin ωt to a capacitor. Its current is C dv/dt = ωCA cos ωt. Two things have happened. The current's amplitude is ωC times the voltage's, so the capacitor behaves like a resistance of 1/(ωC), which falls as frequency rises. And the current is a cosine where the voltage is a sine: it reaches its peak a quarter-cycle earlier. The current leads the voltage by 90°. For an inductor the same steps give an effective resistance of ωL, rising with frequency, with the current lagging by 90°.

Impedance, Z, combines the size and the phase shift into a single quantity, using complex numbers: the "j" (engineers use j, since i is current) marks a 90° phase shift.

$$Z_R = R \qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} \qquad Z_L = j\omega L$$
|Z| is the ratio of voltage amplitude to current amplitude, in ohms; the angle of Z is the phase of the voltage relative to the current

The power of this is that Ohm's law and everything in Module 2, including Kirchhoff's laws, series and parallel combinations and the voltage divider, work unchanged with impedances in place of resistances. The arithmetic is complex, but the circuit analysis is the same.

Design exerciseA capacitor that passes the noise but blocks the DC

A sensor's output is a 2.5 V DC level with a small 10 kHz ripple on top. A 1 µF capacitor is connected in series with the signal, before an input whose resistance to ground is 10 kΩ.

What reaches the input?

  1. Both the DC level and the ripple, unchanged.
    A capacitor has infinite impedance at DC.
  2. The ripple, almost unchanged, but not the 2.5 V DC level: at 10 kHz the capacitor's impedance is about 16 Ω, tiny next to 10 kΩ, while at DC it's an open circuit.
    1/(2π × 10 kHz × 1 µF) ≈ 16 Ω. This is AC coupling, a high-pass filter.
  3. The DC level only; the capacitor shorts the ripple.
    A capacitor in series passes high frequencies; one to ground would short them.
  4. Nothing: capacitors block all current.
    No charge crosses the dielectric, but alternating current flows in the circuit, as Module 3 described.

The capacitor and the 10 kΩ form a divider whose ratio depends on frequency. At 10 kHz, ZC ≈ 16 Ω, so virtually all of the ripple appears across the 10 kΩ. At DC, ZC is infinite, so none of the 2.5 V gets through. This is how an oscilloscope's "AC coupling" setting works, and it's the high-pass filter of the next section.

RC low-pass and high-pass filters

A resistor and a capacitor make a voltage divider (Module 2) whose ratio depends on frequency. With the capacitor to ground, it's a low-pass filter: at low frequencies the capacitor's impedance is large and the output follows the input; at high frequencies the capacitor's impedance is small and shorts the output to ground. Swap the two parts and it becomes a high-pass filter.

DiagramRC low-pass and high-pass filters, and what they look like at the extremes

Writing the low-pass filter as a divider with impedances gives its response directly:

$$H = \frac{v_\text{out}}{v_\text{in}} = \frac{Z_C}{R + Z_C} = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC} \qquad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}} \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$$

At the cut-off frequency fc, the capacitor's impedance equals R, the output is 1/√2 (70.7 %) of the input, so half the power gets through, and the output lags the input by 45°. Well below fc the signal passes; well above it, the output falls in proportion to frequency: ten times the frequency, a tenth of the amplitude. The high-pass filter is the mirror image.

Application

One common use of an RC low-pass filter is in front of an ADC (Module 11), where it removes high-frequency noise picked up by the sensor wiring before the signal is sampled. For a pressure transducer whose readings change over tens of milliseconds, a filter with fc around 100 Hz keeps the measurement responsive while strongly reducing noise at kilohertz and above, for example from a pump motor or a switching regulator. Choosing fc is always a trade between removing noise and slowing the response: a filter with time constant τ = RC takes about 5τ to settle after a step (Module 3).

Design exerciseA filter that changes when it's connected

An RC low-pass filter with R = 10 kΩ and C = 100 nF (fc ≈ 160 Hz) works as designed on the bench into an oscilloscope. Connected to the next circuit, whose input resistance is 10 kΩ, its output is half the expected size at low frequencies, and its cut-off has moved.

Why?

  1. The capacitor is faulty.
    It worked on the bench; what changed is the load.
  2. The 10 kΩ load is in parallel with the capacitor. At low frequencies it forms a 10 kΩ/10 kΩ divider with R, halving the output, and the resistance the capacitor sees drops to 5 kΩ, doubling fc.
    This is Module 2's loading problem, with a capacitor added.
  3. The oscilloscope was adding gain.
    The scope's 1 MΩ or 10 MΩ input barely loads the filter.
  4. Filters only work with a sine-wave input.
    Filters work on any signal, frequency by frequency.

The formula fc = 1/(2πRC) assumes nothing loads the output. The 10 kΩ load turns the circuit into a divider at DC (output halved) and, by Thévenin's theorem, the capacitor now sees R ∥ Rload = 5 kΩ, so fc doubles to about 320 Hz. Either make the filter's resistance much smaller than the load's (ten times or more), or buffer the filter's output with an op-amp (Module 10).

Bode plots and decibels

Frequency responses span huge ranges, from hertz to megahertz in frequency and from 1 to 0.0001 in gain, so they're plotted on logarithmic scales. A Bode plot shows gain in decibels and phase in degrees against frequency on a logarithmic axis. Gain in decibels, for voltages, is

$$G_\text{dB} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_\text{out}}{V_\text{in}}$$
×2 in voltage ≈ +6 dB · ×10 = +20 dB · ×0.707 = −3 dB · ×0.1 = −20 dB

(The factor is 20 for voltage ratios and 10 for power ratios, because power goes as voltage squared; the RF notes use decibels throughout.) Decibels have two advantages beyond compressing the scale. Gains of stages in a chain multiply, so in decibels they add. And on a log-log plot, the RC filter's response becomes two straight lines, which makes it quick to sketch:

DiagramSketching a low-pass filter's Bode plot from straight lines

Each RC stage adds a further −20 dB per decade above its cut-off, so two stages in a row (buffered from each other, or with the second stage's impedance much higher than the first's) roll off at −40 dB per decade. That's the main reason to use more than one stage: for the same cut-off, interference ten times above it is reduced by 40 dB instead of 20 dB.

Design exerciseOne stage or two?

A signal at 10 Hz must pass with little loss, and interference at 1 kHz must be reduced by at least 30 dB.

Can a single RC low-pass stage do it?

  1. Yes: put fc at 100 Hz, and the 1 kHz interference is reduced by 30 dB.
    One decade above fc, a single stage gives only about 20 dB.
  2. Only by putting fc near 30 Hz, close enough to the 10 Hz signal to start reducing it and delaying it noticeably; two stages at 100 Hz give about 40 dB at 1 kHz while leaving 10 Hz almost untouched.
    −20 dB per decade per stage: 30 dB needs 1.5 decades from one stage, or less than one decade from two.
  3. No filter can separate signals a factor of 100 apart.
    Two decades is plenty for a simple filter.
  4. Use a high-pass filter instead.
    A high-pass filter would remove the wanted 10 Hz signal.

A single stage falls at 20 dB per decade, so 30 dB at 1 kHz needs fc about 1.5 decades lower, near 32 Hz. The 10 Hz signal would then be reduced by about 0.4 dB and phase-shifted by 17°, which may be acceptable. Two stages at 100 Hz give about 40 dB at 1 kHz with only 0.1 dB loss at 10 Hz. More stages buy a steeper roll-off; Module 3 of the RF notes takes this further with higher-order filters.

Resonance

An inductor's impedance rises with frequency and a capacitor's falls, and they have opposite phases (+90° and −90°). Put them in series, and at one frequency their impedances are equal in size and cancel exactly, leaving only the circuit's resistance. That's the resonant frequency:

$$\omega_0 L = \frac{1}{\omega_0 C} \quad\Rightarrow\quad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

Physically, energy sloshes back and forth between the capacitor's electric field and the inductor's magnetic field, like a pendulum swinging between height and speed. The resistance in the circuit is friction: it removes a little energy every cycle. The quality factor Q measures how little: for a series circuit, Q = (1/R)√(L/C). A high-Q circuit responds strongly over a narrow band of frequencies around f0 (its bandwidth is f0/Q), and when kicked by a sudden change, it rings for many cycles before settling.

Resonance is useful when it's intended: it selects one frequency in a radio's tuned circuit and a crystal oscillator's crystal, and it's the basis of many filters. It's a problem when it's accidental. Every capacitor has some series inductance and every inductor some capacitance, so self-resonance appears in every real part (Module 3). And any circuit with an inductor and a capacitor, such as a supply filter, rings when a sudden change excites it.

Application

Power inputs often carry an LC filter, a series inductor and a capacitor to ground, to keep switching noise from travelling along the supply wiring. When a battery is connected, the step excites the LC circuit, and with very little resistance in the path the capacitor's voltage overshoots to almost twice the battery voltage before settling. A 12 V input can briefly see more than 20 V, enough to damage a regulator rated for 16 V. The fix is damping: some deliberate resistance, such as an electrolytic capacitor with a moderate ESR placed in parallel with the ceramic, or a small resistor in series with an extra capacitor, so that Q is below about 1.

Design exerciseA better capacitor that made things worse

A board's power input has a 10 µH inductor followed by a 100 µF electrolytic capacitor. To save space, the electrolytic is replaced by an equal-value ceramic capacitor with a much lower ESR. Now the board occasionally resets or fails when the battery is plugged in.

What changed?

  1. The ceramic capacitor's value fell under DC bias, so the filter no longer works.
    DC-bias derating (Module 3) does reduce a ceramic's value, but that doesn't explain damage at plug-in.
  2. The electrolytic's ESR was damping the LC circuit. With the low-ESR ceramic, Q is much higher, so connecting the battery makes the input ring and overshoot towards twice the battery voltage.
    Lower resistance raises Q, and a high-Q LC circuit overshoots a step by almost 100 %.
  3. Ceramic capacitors can't be used on power inputs.
    They're used everywhere on power inputs; they just need damping here.
  4. The inductor saturates with the ceramic capacitor.
    Saturation depends on current, not on the capacitor's type.

For 10 µH and 100 µF, √(L/C) ≈ 0.32 Ω. An electrolytic with 0.3 Ω of ESR gives Q ≈ 1: barely any overshoot. A ceramic with a few milliohms gives Q of 50 or more, and the step response rings up to nearly twice the input. The solution is to keep some damping, usually by adding a small electrolytic or polymer capacitor alongside the ceramic, chosen so that its ESR is close to √(L/C).

The bridge to the RF notes

Everything on this page assumes that the circuit is small compared with the wavelength of the signals in it: a wire is a wire, and a voltage at one end appears instantly at the other. That's an excellent assumption at audio frequencies and in most of a microcontroller board. At higher frequencies it stops being true. A 1 GHz signal has a wavelength of 30 cm in free space and about 15 cm on a circuit board, so a 2 cm trace is already a significant fraction of a wavelength, and the voltage along it varies.

That's where the RF notes start. Their first modules build directly on the ideas here: impedance, phase, decibels and frequency response carry over unchanged, and resonance and self-resonance become everyday design tools. If you're heading there, the most useful things to take from this module are a feel for impedance as a function of frequency and fluency with decibels.

Exercise 1Analysis · ●○○RMS and power

A function generator drives a 10 V peak sine wave into a 50 Ω load.

(a) What's the RMS voltage?

10/√2 = 7.07 V.

(b) What average power does the load dissipate?

That uses the peak voltage. Power uses the RMS value: 7.07²/50.

P = 7.07²/50 = 1.0 W. Using the peak voltage would give 2 W, twice the real figure.

Exercise 2Design · ●●○Filtering a sensor signal

A sensor signal needs a low-pass filter with a cut-off of 100 Hz. R is chosen as 10 kΩ.

(a) What capacitance is needed?

C = 1/(2πRfc).

C = 1/(2π × 10 000 × 100) = 159 nF. The nearest standard value is 150 nF, giving fc = 106 Hz.

(b) With fc = 100 Hz, by how much is 5 kHz interference reduced?

|H| = 1/√(1 + (f/fc)²), then 20 log₁₀.

f/fc = 50, |H| = 1/√2501 ≈ 0.020, which is −34 dB.

Discussion: the cost in response time

τ = RC = 1.6 ms, so the filter takes about 8 ms to settle after a step. For a pressure measurement that's usually fine. For a fast event, such as detecting an ignition transient, it might hide exactly what's being looked for, which is why the filter is chosen with the signal's needs in mind, not just the noise.

Exercise 3Analysis · ●●○An input filter's resonance

A power input filter uses a 10 µH inductor and a 22 µF capacitor.

(a) What's its resonant frequency?

f0 = 1/(2π√(10×10⁻⁶ × 22×10⁻⁶)) = 1/(2π × 14.8×10⁻⁶) ≈ 10.7 kHz.

(b) What series resistance would give Q = 1?

Q = (1/R)√(L/C).

√(L/C) = √(10×10⁻⁶ / 22×10⁻⁶) = 0.67 Ω, so R = 0.67 Ω gives Q = 1.

Discussion: why not add the resistor in series

A 0.67 Ω resistor in series with the supply current would waste power and drop voltage. Instead, the damping resistance is placed in series with an extra capacitor, in parallel with the main one: it damps the resonance but carries no DC current. An electrolytic capacitor's own ESR often serves as this resistor.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A sine wave has amplitude, frequency and phase. Phase is delay measured in cycles: Δφ = 360° × f × Δt.
  2. Any repeating signal is a sum of sines at its fundamental and harmonics; sharp edges need high harmonics.
  3. RMS is the equivalent DC value for power. For a sine, VRMS = A/√2; for other shapes, only a true-RMS meter reads correctly.
  4. Impedance extends Ohm's law: ZC = 1/(jωC) falls with frequency, ZL = jωL rises, each with a 90° phase shift. All of Module 2's techniques work with impedances.
  5. An RC filter's cut-off is fc = 1/(2πRC), where the gain is −3 dB and the phase 45°; beyond it, the response falls at 20 dB per decade per stage. Loading changes both.
  6. An LC circuit resonates at f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)); Q sets how sharp the peak is and how long it rings. Undamped input filters can overshoot to nearly twice the supply.
