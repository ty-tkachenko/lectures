Reading schematics and datasheets

!
Draft, moved from Module 2The rest of this module is not written yet. This section was moved here so Module 2 could stay on circuit laws. It will be integrated when Module 12 is written in full.

A schematic shows how parts are connected, not where they sit on the board. Here is Module 2's battery monitor as it would appear in a real schematic, with the conventions that most often cause mistakes marked:

DiagramThe battery monitor as drawn in a real schematic
  1. Power labels. The VBAT flag connects this wire to every other VBAT flag in the whole schematic, possibly on another page. No wire is drawn between them.
  2. Reference designators and values. R7 is "the seventh resistor". The letter tells you the kind of part: R resistor, C capacitor, L inductor, D diode, Q transistor, U integrated circuit, J connector, F fuse. Values are written without the unit and with the multiplier in place of the decimal point: 4k7 is 4.7 kΩ, 100n is 100 nF.
  3. Junction dots. A dot means the wires are connected. Here three wires meet at the top of R8, and the capacitor joins at a second dot.
  4. Crossings without a dot are not connected. The SPI clock passes straight over the sense net. Good schematics avoid four-way junctions entirely, so a crossing is never ambiguous.
  5. Ground symbols are all one node, like power labels. Boards often have several named grounds (GND, AGND, PGND, chassis), and which ones are joined, and where, is a deliberate design decision, covered with grounding later in this module.
  6. Net labels. VBAT_SENSE names the node, so you can find it in the layout and refer to it by name. Two labels with the same name are connected, even if they're drawn pages apart.

Datasheets

Every part has a datasheet, and the numbers in it come in different kinds that mean very different things:

SectionWhat it meansHow to use it
Absolute maximum ratingsExceed these and the part may be damaged, even brieflyNever a place to operate. Stay well inside, with margin for transients
Recommended operating conditionsThe range over which the part is designed to workDesign to these: supply voltage, temperature, input range
Electrical characteristicsGuaranteed min and max values, plus a "typical", under the stated test conditionsDesign to the min or max. Check the conditions column: they may not match your circuit
Typical performance graphsHow a typical part behavesGood for intuition, not a guarantee
Application circuitThe manufacturer's suggested schematicThe best starting point. Deviate on purpose, not by accident
!
"Typical" is not a specificationA typical value is what the median part does at room temperature. Your part could be anywhere between the min and max, and at −20 °C it might sit at the edge. If a design only works with the typical value, it doesn't work.
✓
Where loading hides in a datasheetADC datasheets list a maximum source impedance (sometimes called external input impedance), often a few tens of kilohms for slow sampling and much less for fast. That's the 100× rule from the ADC's side: the Thévenin resistance of your divider has to stay below it, or the readings come out low. The 100 nF capacitor on the battery monitor avoids the problem by holding the voltage steady while the ADC samples, so slow signals like battery voltage can use larger resistors.
