Capacitors & inductors

Resistors respond instantly: change the voltage and the current follows at once. Capacitors and inductors store energy, so they respond over time. That's what makes them useful for steadying supplies and filtering signals, and it's also behind sparking connectors, transistors destroyed by switched coils, and ringing on oscilloscope traces.

Capacitors

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Push current into one side and charge builds up there, with an equal and opposite charge on the other side. No current crosses the insulator, but as one plate fills, the other empties, so from outside it looks as if current flows through. The charge stored is proportional to the voltage across it:

$$Q = C\,V$$
capacitance C in farads (F): coulombs per volt

A farad is a very large capacitance. Real parts run from picofarads (pF, 10⁻¹² F) in radio circuits, through nanofarads (nF) and microfarads (µF) on most boards, up to millifarads in power supplies. Differentiate the equation and you get the rule that explains almost everything a capacitor does:

$$i = C\,\frac{dv}{dt}$$
current flows only while the voltage is changing

Two consequences follow. With a steady voltage, no current flows, so in a DC circuit that has settled, a capacitor behaves like an open circuit. And a capacitor's voltage can't change instantly, because that would take infinite current. That makes capacitors good at holding a voltage steady: they supply current when the voltage tries to drop and absorb it when the voltage tries to rise.

A charged capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,V^2$$
DiagramA capacitor resists changes in voltage; an inductor resists changes in current
Worked example

A 100 µF capacitor sits across a 5 V supply that suddenly stops delivering current, while the circuit keeps drawing 20 mA. Rearranging \(i = C\,dv/dt\), the voltage falls at

\(\dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{i}{C} = \dfrac{0.02}{100 \times 10^{-6}} = 200\ \text{V/s}\), or 0.2 V every millisecond.

That's the idea behind a hold-up capacitor: it keeps a circuit running through a brief interruption, such as a loose connector or the supply sagging while a motor starts.

Design exerciseHow long can a capacitor hold up a rail?

A microcontroller runs from a 3.3 V rail and draws 50 mA. It keeps working down to 3.0 V. A 1000 µF capacitor sits across the rail.

If the supply cuts out, how long does the capacitor keep the rail above 3.0 V?

That's how long it would take to discharge all the way from 3.3 V to 0 V. The microcontroller stops at 3.0 V.

Rearrange \(i = C\,dv/dt\) to \(t = C\,\Delta V / I\). How much can the voltage fall?

\(t = C\,\Delta V / I = 1000\,\mu\text{F} \times 0.3\ \text{V} / 50\ \text{mA} = 6\ \text{ms}\). That's not long, because only 0.3 V of the 3.3 V is usable. Exercise 1 at the end shows why hold-up capacitors usually go before the regulator, where far more of the voltage can be used.

Inductors

An inductor is a coil of wire, often wound on a magnetic core. Current through the coil builds a magnetic field, and changing that field induces a voltage that opposes the change. Its rule is the mirror image of the capacitor's:

$$v = L\,\frac{di}{dt}$$
inductance L in henries (H)

With a steady current there's no voltage across an (ideal) inductor, so in a settled DC circuit it behaves like a wire. And an inductor's current can't change instantly, because that would take infinite voltage. Inductors keep current flowing: they produce whatever voltage it takes to do so.

The energy is stored in the magnetic field:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}\,L\,I^2$$

Inductance appears in two forms. Deliberate inductors appear in switching power supplies (Module 7) and filters. Accidental inductance is everywhere: every wire and trace has some, roughly 1 nH per millimetre, and every motor, relay and solenoid coil is a large inductor.

The RC time constant

Connect a capacitor to a supply through a resistor. At the first instant the capacitor is empty, so it has no voltage across it, and the full supply voltage sits across the resistor: the current is \(V/R\). As charge builds up, the capacitor's voltage rises, the resistor's share shrinks, and the current falls. The closer the capacitor gets to full, the slower it fills. The result is an exponential curve:

$$v_C(t) = V\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau} \qquad \tau = R\,C$$
τ (tau), the time constant, in seconds when R is in ohms and C in farads

Discharging through a resistor gives the same curve upside down: \(v_C = V e^{-t/\tau}\). The time constant is all you need to know about the speed. After one τ the capacitor is 63 % of the way there; after five it's within 1 %, which engineers treat as "done".

Design exerciseA power-on delay

A chip's enable pin is fed from its 3.3 V supply through 10 kΩ, with 1 µF from the pin to ground. The chip turns on when the pin reaches 2.0 V.

How long after power-up does the chip turn on?

That's one time constant, when the pin reaches 63 % of 3.3 V, about 2.1 V. The threshold is a little lower.

Solve \(2.0 = 3.3\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) for t, with τ = RC.

τ = 10 kΩ × 1 µF = 10 ms. \(e^{-t/\tau} = 1 - 2.0/3.3 = 0.394\), so \(t = -\tau \ln 0.394 = 0.93\,\tau = 9.32\ \text{ms}\). Capacitor tolerance (Section 6) makes the real delay vary by 10 % or more from part to part, so don't rely on it for precise timing.

Inrush current

Boards have capacitors across their power input, often hundreds of microfarads in total. When you first connect a battery, those capacitors are empty, and an empty capacitor looks like a short circuit. The only things limiting the current are the small resistances in the way: the battery's internal resistance, the wiring, the connector and the capacitor's own internal resistance (its ESR, Section 6). Together that can be a tenth of an ohm or less.

$$I_\text{peak} \approx \frac{V}{R_\text{path}} \qquad \tau = R_\text{path}\,C$$

The surge is short, because \(R_\text{path}\) is small and τ is tiny, but it can be tens or hundreds of amps. That's the spark when plugging in a large battery. It pits connector contacts, it can trip a supply's protection, and the rapid change of current through the wiring's inductance causes voltage spikes of its own.

✓
How designs limit inrushA precharge resistor that's connected first and bypassed a moment later, "anti-spark" connectors with a built-in resistor on one pin, or an electronic load switch that turns on slowly (Module 7). All of them do the same thing: they make the capacitor charge through more resistance, or more slowly, than the bare wiring would.
Design exercisePeak inrush current

A fully charged 2S LiPo (8.4 V) is plugged into a board with 470 µF across its input. The battery's internal resistance is 80 mΩ, the wiring and connector add 20 mΩ, and the capacitor's ESR is 50 mΩ.

What's the peak inrush current?

You left out the capacitor's ESR. Every resistance in the loop counts.

At the first instant the capacitor has no voltage across it. All 8.4 V is across the resistances in the loop.

\(R_\text{path} = 0.08 + 0.02 + 0.05 = 0.15\ \Omega\), so \(I_\text{peak} = 8.4 / 0.15 = 56\ \text{A}\). It only lasts a few time constants, \(\tau = 0.15 \times 470\,\mu\text{F} \approx 70\ \mu\text{s}\), so the battery is unaffected, but the connector contacts take the full surge.

RL circuits and inductive kickback

An inductor in series with a resistor has a time constant too. Switch on a supply and the current can't jump, so it rises exponentially towards \(V/R\):

$$i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$

Switching off is the problem. Picture an inductor carrying a steady current when the switch in series with it opens and tries to stop that current in a microsecond. The inductor resists: it generates \(v = L\,di/dt\) to keep the current going, and with a fast switch that's hundreds or thousands of volts. This is inductive kickback. The voltage appears across the opening switch. A mechanical switch arcs, which erodes its contacts. A transistor breaks down, absorbs the inductor's stored energy, and may fail on the spot or after a few hundred cycles.

Any inductive load, meaning anything built around a coil of wire, kicks back like this. One common example is a solenoid valve: current through a coil pulls a plunger that opens the valve, and when you switch the current off, the coil kicks back. Relay coils, motors and loudspeakers do the same.

The fix is a flyback diode across the inductor, pointing so it doesn't conduct in normal operation. When the switch opens, the inductor's current switches over to the diode and circulates around the inductor-and-diode loop, dying away with time constant L/R. The switch only ever sees the supply voltage plus the diode's 0.7 V drop. Module 5 covers diodes, and Module 6 the transistor that does the switching.

The flyback diode's drawbackBecause the diode keeps the current circulating, the coil takes longer to release: a solenoid valve closes more slowly and a relay drops out later. When release speed matters, a Zener or TVS diode in series with the flyback diode lets the voltage rise to a controlled level, so the energy is absorbed faster while the switch stays within its rating.
Design exerciseSize of the kickback

A relay coil has 20 mH of inductance and carries 0.5 A. The transistor driving it turns off, and the current falls to zero in 10 µs.

What voltage does the coil generate while the current falls?

Check the units: 10 µs is \(10 \times 10^{-6}\) s, and 20 mH is 0.02 H.

\(v = L\,\Delta i / \Delta t\).

\(v = 0.02 \times 0.5 / 10^{-5} = 1000\ \text{V}\). In practice the transistor breaks down long before that and clamps the voltage at its breakdown rating, absorbing the coil's energy as heat. A diode costing a few cents prevents it.

Real capacitors

A capacitor's datasheet has more numbers on it than its capacitance, and several of them change how it behaves in your circuit:

  • Voltage rating. The most voltage it can safely have across it. Exceed it and the insulator breaks down. Derate as in Module 1: electrolytics and tantalums especially should run well below their rating.
  • Tolerance. Ceramic capacitors are commonly ±10 % or ±20 %, electrolytics often −20 % to +80 %. Don't design anything that depends on the exact value.
  • ESR (equivalent series resistance). Every capacitor behaves as if it had a small resistor in series with it. ESR limits how fast it can deliver current and heats the part when ripple current flows through it.
  • ESL (equivalent series inductance). A small series inductance from the part's leads and body, which matters at high frequencies and for decoupling (Section 7).
  • Polarity. Electrolytic and tantalum capacitors only work one way round. Reversed, they fail, sometimes violently; tantalums can catch fire.

DC-bias derating

The ceramic capacitors on almost every board (dielectric types X5R and X7R) have a property that is easy to miss: their capacitance falls as you put DC voltage across them. A 10 µF part in a small package, run near its rated voltage, can have less than a third of its marked value. The marking is measured at almost no voltage. Larger packages and higher voltage ratings lose less. Type C0G (also called NP0) ceramics don't have the effect, but they're only made in small values.

Design exerciseEnough capacitance on a 5 V rail

A regulator's datasheet asks for at least 4 µF of effective output capacitance on its 5 V output. You have 10 µF 0603 capacitors, and the manufacturer's curve shows they keep 35 % of their capacitance at 5 V.

How many do you need?

One 10 µF part only has 3.5 µF left at 5 V, short of the 4 µF required.

Each part gives \(0.35 \times 10 = 3.5\ \mu\text{F}\) at 5 V, so you need two (7 µF). Or one larger part with a higher voltage rating. Regulators can oscillate when their output capacitance falls short, and a design that looked fine on the schematic fails on the bench because of exactly this effect.

Decoupling capacitors

Digital chips don't draw a steady current. Every time their internal logic switches, they take a burst of current lasting a few nanoseconds. The supply that feeds them is centimetres away, and the trace in between has inductance. From \(v = L\,di/dt\), a fast change in current through that inductance means a voltage drop: the chip's supply dips at exactly the moment it needs current. Large enough dips cause resets, corrupted data and noise on every signal the chip produces.

A decoupling capacitor placed right next to the chip's power pin supplies those bursts locally, over a path with very little inductance, and recharges from the supply between bursts. The usual starting point is a 100 nF ceramic at every power pin, as close as possible, plus a few microfarads of bulk capacitance nearby. Module 12 covers placement in detail.

Design exerciseHow much does the decoupling capacitor droop?

A chip takes a 100 mA burst lasting 20 ns, and in that time the trace inductance lets almost nothing through, so its 100 nF decoupling capacitor supplies all of it.

How far does the capacitor's voltage drop?

Correct value, but in volts. The answer is in millivolts.

\(\Delta V = I\,\Delta t / C\).

\(\Delta V = 0.1 \times 20 \times 10^{-9} / 100 \times 10^{-9} = 0.02\ \text{V} = 20\ \text{mV}\), well under 1 % of a 3.3 V rail. In practice the capacitor's own inductance and its connection to the chip matter as much as its value, which is why placement is so important.

The oscilloscope

A multimeter gives one number. An oscilloscope draws voltage against time, which is needed for everything on this page: charging curves, kickback spikes, supply dips. The main controls:

  • Vertical scale (volts per division) and timebase (time per division) set how much of the signal fits on the screen. The screen is usually 10 divisions across and 8 high.
  • Trigger. A repeating signal only looks stable if every sweep starts at the same point in it. The trigger sets that point, typically "when the signal crosses this level, rising". If the trace is unstable or smeared, check the trigger first. Single-shot mode captures a one-off event, like an inrush spike.
  • Coupling. DC coupling shows the whole signal. AC coupling removes the average so you can zoom in on small ripple riding on a supply.

Probes

The standard probe is a 10× probe: it divides the signal by ten, which lets it present a high resistance (10 MΩ) and low capacitance (around 10 pF) to the circuit, so it disturbs it less. The scope has to know the probe is 10× or every reading is off by a factor of ten. Most scopes have a per-channel probe setting; check it every time.

!
Probe ground, part 1: the clip is earthedOn a mains-powered bench scope, the probe's ground clip is connected to mains earth through the scope's power cord. Clip it to anything that isn't already at earth potential and you've made a short circuit through the scope. With a battery-powered board that's floating, it's harmless. With anything connected to mains, or to a computer over USB, it can blow traces, destroy the device and damage the scope. Clip ground to ground, and if you need the voltage between two points that aren't ground, use two channels and subtract, or a differential probe.

The second issue with the probe ground is less obvious. The ground clip's 15 cm lead is an inductor, and together with the probe tip's capacitance it forms a resonant circuit. Every fast edge makes it ring, and the ringing is added by the probe, not present in the circuit. For fast signals, take the long lead off and use the short spring that comes with the probe, touching ground right next to the point you're measuring.

Design exercises

Problems drawn from real hardware. Each takes a few steps. Enter numbers to check your reasoning as you go, or open the worked solutions and discussion. The figures above are useful for sanity-checking.

Exercise 1Design · ●●○Sizing a hold-up capacitor

A board runs from a 2S battery through a linear regulator. When a motor on the same battery starts, the battery's voltage can drop out for up to 5 ms, and a diode stops the board's input capacitor from discharging back into the motor. You want the board to keep running through the dropout. A linear regulator draws the same current at its input as it delivers at its output.

Given Input voltage before the dropout7.4 V Regulator's minimum input voltage3.6 V Board current80 mA Dropout length5 ms

(a) How far can the input capacitor's voltage fall before the regulator stops working?

7.4 − 3.6 = 3.8 V.

(b) What's the minimum capacitance at the regulator's input?

That's what you'd need on the 3.3 V output, where only 0.3 V of droop is allowed. The capacitor is on the input.

\(C = I\,t / \Delta V\).

\(C = 0.08 \times 0.005 / 3.8 = 105\ \mu\text{F}\). A 220 µF electrolytic gives margin for tolerance, ageing and cold.

Discussion: why put it before the regulator?

On the 3.3 V output, the capacitor can only fall 0.3 V before the microcontroller stops, so the same job takes about 1300 µF. Before the regulator it can fall 3.8 V, so it needs twelve times less. The energy view says the same thing: the capacitor delivers \(\tfrac12 C (V_1^2 - V_2^2)\), and a large voltage swing makes most of its stored energy available. The diode matters too: without it, the capacitor would discharge into the starting motor instead of into the board.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Inrush when connecting a 4S pack

A motor controller has 1000 µF across its input. It's connected to a fully charged 4S LiPo with an ordinary connector. Everything in the path, from the battery's internal resistance to the capacitors' ESR, adds up to 0.1 Ω.

Given Battery, full charge4 × 4.2 V = 16.8 V Input capacitance1000 µF Total path resistance0.1 Ω

(a) Peak inrush current?

\(16.8 / 0.1 = 168\ \text{A}\), through a connector rated for perhaps 30 A continuous.

(b) Time constant of the surge?

\(\tau = 0.1 \times 1000\,\mu\text{F} = 100\ \mu\text{s}\). The surge is mostly over within half a millisecond.

(c) Energy stored in the capacitors once they're charged?

\(\tfrac12 \times 10^{-3} \times 16.8^2 = 0.141\ \text{J}\).

Discussion: where else does energy go?

A useful result: charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through any resistance wastes exactly as much energy as ends up stored, here another 0.141 J. With a small resistance it's dumped in a fraction of a millisecond, much of it at the connector contacts as they touch, which is what pits and welds them. A precharge resistor doesn't reduce that energy, but it spreads it over a resistor designed to take it and over a much longer time. Anti-spark connectors build that resistor into the plug.

Exercise 3Design · ●●●Protecting a solenoid's switch

A transistor switches a 12 V solenoid valve. The transistor is rated for 30 V and turns off in about 1 µs.

Given Supply12 V Coil24 Ω, 30 mH Transistor rating30 V Flyback diode forward drop0.7 V

(a) Steady coil current?

\(12 / 24 = 0.5\ \text{A}\). In steady state the inductor is just its 24 Ω of wire.

(b) Without protection, what voltage would the coil generate to stop 0.5 A in 1 µs?

\(v = L\,\Delta i/\Delta t\).

\(0.03 \times 0.5 / 10^{-6} = 15\,000\ \text{V}\). It never reaches that: the 30 V transistor breaks down first and absorbs the coil's energy.

(c) With a flyback diode across the coil, what's the highest voltage the transistor sees?

That's the voltage across the diode (and the coil). The transistor sits between the bottom of the coil and ground, so it also has the 12 V supply above it.

The coil's bottom end rises until the diode conducts, 0.7 V above the 12 V supply: 12.7 V, comfortably inside 30 V.

(d) With the diode, roughly how long is the time constant of the current dying away?

\(\tau = L/R = 0.03 / 24 = 1.25\ \text{ms}\), so the current takes around 5 ms to die away and the valve closes correspondingly later.

Discussion: when a slow release matters

If the valve must close quickly, put a Zener or TVS diode in series with the flyback diode, for example a 15 V Zener. The coil voltage can then rise to about 15.7 V, so the energy is absorbed several times faster, and the transistor sees about 12 + 15.7 ≈ 28 V. That's under 30 V but with little margin, so a 60 V transistor would be the better choice. Module 5 covers Zeners and TVS diodes.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A capacitor's voltage can't change instantly (\(i = C\,dv/dt\)); an inductor's current can't (\(v = L\,di/dt\)). In settled DC, a capacitor is an open circuit and an inductor is a wire.
  2. RC and RL circuits change exponentially with time constants τ = RC and τ = L/R: 63 % after one τ, within 1 % after five.
  3. Empty capacitors look like a short circuit at switch-on. Inrush current is limited only by the resistance in the path.
  4. Interrupting an inductor's current produces a large voltage spike. Put a flyback diode across every relay, solenoid and motor coil you switch.
  5. Real capacitors have tolerance, ESR, a voltage rating and, for X5R and X7R ceramics, a capacitance that shrinks with DC voltage.
  6. A 100 nF decoupling capacitor at every power pin supplies chips' fast current bursts locally.
  7. Set the scope for the probe you're using, clip ground only to ground, and use the ground spring for fast edges.
