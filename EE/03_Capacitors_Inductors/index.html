Capacitors & inductors

Resistors respond instantly: change the voltage and the current follows at once. Capacitors and inductors store energy, so they respond over time. That's what makes them useful for steadying supplies and filtering signals, and it's also behind sparking connectors, transistors destroyed by switched coils, and supply dips when a chip draws a sudden burst of current.

Capacitors

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Push current into one side and charge builds up there, with an equal and opposite charge on the other side. No current crosses the insulator, but as one plate fills, the other empties, so from outside it looks as if current flows through. The charge stored is proportional to the voltage across it:

$$Q = C\,V$$
capacitance C in farads (F): coulombs per volt

A farad is a very large capacitance. Real parts run from picofarads (pF, 10⁻¹² F) in radio circuits, through nanofarads (nF) and microfarads (µF) on most boards, up to millifarads in power supplies. Differentiate the equation and you get the rule that explains almost everything a capacitor does:

$$i = C\,\frac{dv}{dt}$$
current flows only while the voltage is changing

Two consequences follow. With a steady voltage, no current flows, so in a DC circuit that has settled, a capacitor behaves like an open circuit. And a capacitor's voltage can't change instantly, because that would take infinite current. That makes capacitors good at holding a voltage steady: they supply current when the voltage tries to drop and absorb it when the voltage tries to rise.

A charged capacitor stores energy in the electric field between its plates:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,V^2$$
DiagramA capacitor resists changes in voltage; an inductor resists changes in current
Worked example

A 100 µF capacitor sits across a 5 V supply that suddenly stops delivering current, while the circuit keeps drawing 20 mA. Rearranging \(i = C\,dv/dt\), the voltage falls at

\(\dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{i}{C} = \dfrac{0.02}{100 \times 10^{-6}} = 200\ \text{V/s}\), or 0.2 V every millisecond.

That's the idea behind a hold-up capacitor: it keeps a circuit running through a brief interruption, such as a loose connector or the supply sagging while a motor starts. The voltage to start from is the lowest the capacitor will actually be sitting at, not the battery's nominal voltage. Exercise 1 is the worked case.

Check your understandingBigger capacitor, or more allowed droop?

A hold-up capacitor keeps a microcontroller running for a few milliseconds after its supply cuts out. You can make one change: double the capacitor, or use a chip that keeps working down to a lower voltage, so the rail can droop twice as far.

Which gives the longer hold-up time?

  1. Doubling the capacitor.
    Doubling C does double the hold-up time, and so does doubling the allowed droop. The charge the capacitor can give up is \(Q = C\,\Delta V\), so the two changes do the same thing.
  2. Doubling the allowed droop.
    Doubling ΔV does double the hold-up time, and so does doubling C. Neither one is ahead of the other; \(t = C\,\Delta V/I\) is proportional to both.
  3. They give the same time.
    The time is proportional to both C and the allowed droop.
  4. Neither helps: the current sets the time.
    The current matters, but so do both of these.

The capacitor supplies a charge \(Q = C\,\Delta V\) before the rail falls too far, and the chip uses it at a rate I. Doubling C or doubling ΔV doubles the charge available, so both double the time. This is why hold-up capacitors go before the regulator when possible: there the allowed droop is several volts instead of a few hundred millivolts, worth as much as a far bigger capacitor.

Check your understanding"105 µF is enough, it's a 7.4 V pack"

A board holds itself up through a 5 ms dropout with a capacitor ahead of a regulator that needs at least 3.6 V, while the board draws 80 mA. Someone computes \(C = 0.080 \times 0.005 / (7.4 - 3.6) = 105\ \mu\text{F}\) from the pack's nominal 7.4 V, and waves away the series diode as "only a diode".

What does that calculation miss?

  1. Nothing. Nominal voltage is the voltage a 2S pack sits at, and a diode drop is too small to bother with.
    7.4 V is the label, not the voltage you can count on while the pack is sagging. The diode is in series with the capacitor, so its drop is subtracted before the capacitor ever charges.
  2. The current is wrong: a regulator draws less at its input than at its output.
    A linear regulator passes the load current through from input to output. The current in the calculation is the right one; the voltage it starts from is not.
  3. The capacitor starts from a sagged pack minus the diode drop, about 6.6 − 0.7 = 5.9 V, so it only has 2.3 V of room. 105 µF runs out before the 5 ms is over.
    Design to the lowest voltage the capacitor will actually be sitting at. The nominal figure is the same kind of number as a datasheet "typical".
  4. Hold-up capacitors have to go on the regulator's output, so the input calculation is irrelevant.
    The input side is the better place, because the allowed swing is volts rather than a few hundred millivolts. It only works if that swing is the real one.

\(C = It/\Delta V\). Using 3.8 V of room asks for 105 µF; using 2.3 V asks for about 175 µF. The difference is the sag and the diode, both of which are in the circuit the capacitor has to survive. A Schottky at 0.3 V would be a milder case (about 150 µF). The ordinary diode is the one to design to unless that Schottky is actually fitted. Inductors

An inductor is a coil of wire, often wound on a magnetic core. Current through the coil builds a magnetic field, and changing that field induces a voltage that opposes the change. Its rule is the mirror image of the capacitor's:

$$v = L\,\frac{di}{dt}$$
inductance L in henries (H)

With a steady current there's no voltage across an (ideal) inductor, so in a settled DC circuit it behaves like a wire. And an inductor's current can't change instantly, because that would take infinite voltage. Inductors keep current flowing: they produce whatever voltage it takes to do so.

The energy is stored in the magnetic field:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}\,L\,I^2$$

Inductance appears in two forms. Deliberate inductors appear in switching power supplies (Module 7) and filters. Accidental inductance is everywhere: every wire and trace has some, roughly 1 nH per millimetre, and every motor, relay and solenoid coil is a large inductor.

The RC time constant

Connect a capacitor to a supply through a resistor. At the first instant the capacitor is empty, so the whole supply voltage sits across the resistor and the current is \(V/R\). Call the gap the voltage still left to climb, \(V - v_C\). That whole gap is across the resistor, so the charging current is proportional to the gap that remains:

$$i = \frac{V - v_C}{R}$$
a bigger remaining gap means a bigger charging current

In a short interval \(\Delta t\) the capacitor's voltage rises by \(\Delta v = i\,\Delta t/C\). Substitute the current and the rise is a fixed fraction of the gap:

$$\Delta v = (V - v_C)\,\frac{\Delta t}{\tau} \qquad \tau = R\,C$$
τ (tau), the time constant, in seconds when R is in ohms and C in farads

The fraction \(\Delta t/\tau\) does not depend on how large the gap is. Every interval therefore closes the same share of whatever gap is left, and the gap that remains is what the next interval works on.

Chop one time constant into \(n\) equal slices. After one slice the fraction of the gap still open is \(1 - 1/n\). After \(n\) slices, one whole \(\tau\), it is \((1 - 1/n)^n\). Four slices leave \((3/4)^4 \approx 0.32\) of the gap; ten slices leave about 0.35. Finer slicing settles at \(1/e \approx 0.37\): about 37 % of the gap remains, so the capacitor is 63 % of the way to the supply. The same fraction applies over the next \(\tau\), so after two time constants about \(0.37^2 \approx 14\) % of the gap is left, and after five, \((0.37)^5\) is under 1 %. Five time constants is what engineers treat as settled.

A curve that always closes the same fraction of the remaining gap is an exponential. Charging, written in that compact form, is

$$v_C(t) = V\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\,e^{-t/\tau}$$
the same fraction rule, with \(e^{-1} \approx 0.37\) left of the gap after each τ

No calculus went into the fraction. \(\tau = RC\) is the one number that sets the speed, and the percentages above are what equal fractions produce. Discharging is the same rule with the gap measured down from the starting voltage toward zero: \(v_C = V e^{-t/\tau}\).

Check your understandingA delay on a slowly rising supply

A chip's enable pin is fed from its 3.3 V supply through 10 kΩ, with 1 µF to ground, so it turns on about 10 ms after the supply appears. On a new board the supply doesn't jump to 3.3 V; it ramps up slowly over 50 ms.

Does the RC still hold the chip off until the supply has settled?

  1. Yes: the delay always starts once the supply is fully up.
    The RC can't tell when the supply is "fully up". It just follows whatever voltage it's given.
  2. No: the capacitor follows the slow ramp, lagging it by roughly τ, so the pin can cross its threshold while the supply is still rising.
    A slow ramp is something an RC tracks, with a lag of about one time constant.
  3. The chip never turns on.
    The capacitor still charges; it just follows the ramp.
  4. The delay becomes 60 ms: 50 ms of ramp plus 10 ms of RC.
    The RC delay doesn't wait for the ramp to finish before it starts.

An RC delay works because the capacitor can't follow a sudden step. A slow ramp isn't sudden: the capacitor voltage follows it, about one time constant behind. Here the pin crosses 2.0 V roughly 10 ms after the supply does, at which point the supply is only about 2.6 V on its way to 3.3 V. If the chip must wait for a settled supply, use a reset or supervisor chip that watches the supply voltage itself rather than relying on a fixed delay.

Check your understandingWhy the curve bends over

A capacitor charging through a resistor heads for the supply voltage on a curve, fast at first and slower later, rather than in a straight line.

Why does it close the same fraction of the remaining gap every time constant?

  1. Because the formula contains an exponential, and exponentials bend.
    The formula is the compact way to write the fraction rule. It is not the reason the voltage bends.
  2. Because the resistor heats up and its resistance rises as the capacitor charges.
    The curve has this shape with a constant resistance. Heating is a side effect, not the mechanism.
  3. The charging current is the remaining gap divided by R, so it shrinks in proportion to the gap. A current proportional to the gap that's left closes a fixed fraction of it in each interval.
    Straight-line charging would need a constant current. Here the current falls as soon as the capacitor voltage rises.
  4. Because a capacitor is nonlinear: it stores less charge per volt as it fills.
    An ideal capacitor is linear, Q = CV. The bending comes from the shrinking current, not from C changing.

The gap is \(V - v_C\), and all of it sits across the resistor, so \(i = (V - v_C)/R\). In a short interval the capacitor climbs by a fraction \(\Delta t/(RC)\) of that gap, whatever the gap currently is. Repeating a fixed fraction is the exponential: about 37 % of the gap left after one τ, and under 1 % after five. The same argument with the gap measured in current gives the RL curve, with \(\tau = L/R\).

Inrush current

Boards have capacitors across their power input, often hundreds of microfarads in total. When you first connect a battery, those capacitors are empty, and an empty capacitor looks like a short circuit. The only things limiting the current are the small resistances in the way: the battery's internal resistance, the wiring, the connector and the capacitor's own internal resistance (its ESR, Section 6). Together that can be a tenth of an ohm or less.

$$I_\text{peak} \approx \frac{V}{R_\text{path}} \qquad \tau = R_\text{path}\,C$$

The surge is short, because \(R_\text{path}\) is small and τ is tiny, but it can be tens or hundreds of amps. That's the spark when plugging in a large battery. It pits connector contacts, it can trip a supply's protection, and the rapid change of current through the wiring's inductance causes voltage spikes of its own.

✓
How designs limit inrushA precharge resistor that's connected first and bypassed a moment later, "anti-spark" connectors with a built-in resistor on one pin, or an electronic load switch that turns on slowly (Module 7). All of them do the same thing: they make the capacitor charge through more resistance, or more slowly, than the bare wiring would.
Check your understandingWhat sets the size of the inrush spike?

Plugging a battery into a board with a large input capacitor causes a big current spike.

Which change halves the peak current?

  1. Halving the capacitance.
    At the first instant the capacitor voltage is still zero, so it looks like a short circuit. Capacitance sets how long the surge lasts, through τ = RC, not the peak current.
  2. Doubling the resistance in the path.
    At the first instant the empty capacitor is a short, so only the path resistance limits the current.
  3. Either one.
    Only one of them affects the peak. The other changes how long it lasts.
  4. Neither: the battery sets the peak.
    The battery's internal resistance is part of the path resistance, but you can add more.

At the instant of connection the capacitor is empty, has no voltage across it, and looks like a short circuit. The peak current is just the battery voltage divided by everything resistive in the path, whatever the capacitance. The capacitance only sets how long the surge lasts, through τ = RC. That's why inrush limiting always works by adding resistance, or by turning on slowly.

RL circuits and inductive kickback

An inductor in series with a resistor follows the same fraction rule, with the gap measured in current instead of voltage. Switch on a supply and the current cannot jump, so it starts at zero. The gap is the current still short of the final value \(V/R\). The resistor drops \(iR\), which leaves \(V - iR\) across the inductor, and \(v = L\,di/dt\) turns that into a rise in current:

$$\Delta i = \left(\frac{V}{R} - i\right)\frac{\Delta t}{\tau} \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$
the current-gap closes by the same fraction every interval, just as the voltage-gap did for the capacitor

So the current climbs the same exponential the capacitor's voltage did, 63 % of the way after one \(\tau\) and within 1 % after five:

$$i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$

Switching off is the problem. Picture an inductor carrying a steady current when the switch in series with it opens and tries to stop that current in a microsecond. The inductor resists: it generates \(v = L\,di/dt\) to keep the current going, and with a fast switch that's hundreds or thousands of volts. This is inductive kickback. Note the direction: while the switch is on, the supply end of the coil is positive with respect to the other end, and to keep the current flowing the coil's voltage reverses, a large negative spike. The coil's lower end flies far above the supply, and that end is connected to the opening switch. A mechanical switch arcs, which erodes its contacts. A transistor breaks down, absorbs the inductor's stored energy, and may fail on the spot or after a few hundred cycles.

Any inductive load, meaning anything built around a coil of wire, kicks back like this. One common example is a solenoid valve: current through a coil pulls a plunger that opens the valve, and when you switch the current off, the coil kicks back. Relay coils, motors and loudspeakers do the same.

The fix is a flyback diode across the inductor, pointing so it doesn't conduct in normal operation. When the switch opens, the inductor's current switches over to the diode and circulates around the inductor-and-diode loop, dying away with time constant L/R. The switch only ever sees the supply voltage plus the diode's 0.7 V drop. Module 5 covers diodes, and Module 6 the transistor that does the switching.

Check your understandingA faster switch, a bigger kick

Transistors that switch off faster waste less power while switching, so faster is usually better. This one drives a relay coil with no flyback diode.

What does a faster switch-off do to the voltage spike?

  1. It makes the spike smaller, because the event is over sooner.
    The coil's voltage is L times how fast the current falls. A shorter turn-off is a faster fall, so the voltage is larger, not smaller.
  2. It makes the spike larger.
    The coil generates \(L\,di/dt\), and a faster switch-off means a larger di/dt.
  3. It doesn't change it: the coil's stored energy is the same.
    The energy is the same, but it's released over a shorter time, so the voltage is higher.
  4. It depends only on the supply voltage.
    The supply voltage sets the current before switch-off, not how fast it's forced to stop.

The coil holds the same energy, \(\tfrac12 LI^2\), however you switch it off. Forcing the current to zero faster means a larger di/dt, so the coil generates a larger voltage to oppose it. Unprotected, a fast switch makes the kick worse, which is one more reason the flyback diode isn't optional. With the diode in place the switch can be as fast as you like, because the current has somewhere to go.

Real capacitors

A capacitor's datasheet has more numbers on it than its capacitance, and several of them change how it behaves in your circuit:

  • Voltage rating. The most voltage it can safely have across it. Exceed it and the insulator breaks down. Derate as in Module 1: electrolytics and tantalums especially should run well below their rating.
  • Tolerance. Ceramic capacitors are commonly ±10 % or ±20 %, electrolytics often −20 % to +80 %. Don't design anything that depends on the exact value.
  • ESR (equivalent series resistance). Every capacitor behaves as if it had a small resistor in series with it. ESR limits how fast it can deliver current and heats the part when ripple current flows through it.
  • ESL (equivalent series inductance). A small series inductance from the part's leads and body. A fast current through it drops voltage, \(v = L\,di/dt\), which is why a decoupling capacitor has to sit close to the pin it serves (Section 8). How that inductance makes the part stop behaving as a capacitor above some frequency is Module 4.
  • Polarity. Electrolytic and tantalum capacitors only work one way round. Reversed, they fail, sometimes violently; tantalums can catch fire.

DC-bias derating

The ceramic capacitors on almost every board (dielectric types X5R and X7R) have a property that is easy to miss: their capacitance falls as you put DC voltage across them. A 10 µF part in a small package, run near its rated voltage, can have less than a third of its marked value. The marking is measured at almost no voltage. Larger packages and higher voltage ratings lose less. Type C0G (also called NP0) ceramics don't have the effect, but they're only made in small values.

Real inductors

Real inductors depart from the ideal \(v = L\,di/dt\) in ways that matter just as much as a capacitor's ESR and DC bias. An inductor's datasheet lists:

  • DC resistance (DCR). The resistance of the winding. It wastes power as \(I^2R\) and sets the steady current in a coil driven from a fixed voltage: the 24 Ω of the solenoid in Section 5 is its DCR.
  • Saturation current (Isat). A magnetic core can only carry so much magnetic flux. As the current approaches Isat, the core saturates and the inductance falls, often defined on the datasheet as the current at which L has dropped by 20 to 30 %. Past that point the current rises much faster than \(V/L\) predicts.
  • Rated (thermal) current (Irms). The current that heats the part by a stated amount, typically 40 °C, through its DCR. It's a separate limit from Isat, and either one can be the lower of the two.
  • Winding capacitance. The turns have capacitance between them. Together with the inductance that sets a frequency where the two cancel; Module 4 uses it as the example of resonance. Below that frequency the part is still the inductor described here.
  • Tolerance and shielding. ±20 % is common. Unshielded inductors spread their magnetic field into nearby circuits; shielded ones keep most of it inside the part.

The core material decides how saturation happens. Air-core inductors never saturate but only reach small values. Ferrite cores give high inductance in a small part but saturate abruptly. Powdered-iron and metal-composite cores saturate gradually, losing inductance steadily as the current rises.

One common example where saturation matters is the inductor in a switching regulator (Module 7), which carries a current that ramps up and down every few microseconds. If the peak of that ramp passes Isat, the current runs away and can destroy the regulator's switch.

Check your understandingMore voltage across the same inductor

In a switching regulator, the inductor has a voltage across it for a fixed on-time each cycle, and its current ramps up during that time. The input voltage rises, so the voltage across the inductor during the on-time rises too, with the same on-time.

What happens to the inductor?

  1. Nothing: the inductance sets the current, not the voltage.
    Inductance sets how fast the current changes for a given voltage.
  2. The current ramps up more steeply and peaks higher, bringing it closer to saturation.
    The ramp's slope is V/L, so a bigger voltage over the same time means a bigger rise.
  3. The current ramps up more slowly.
    More voltage across an inductor makes the current change faster, not slower.
  4. The inductance increases to compensate.
    If anything, the inductance falls as the current approaches saturation.

For an inductor, voltage sets the rate of change of current: \(di/dt = V/L\). More voltage for the same time gives a larger rise, so the peak current goes up. If that peak gets near Isat, the inductance starts falling, the ramp gets steeper still, and the current can run away. That's why a regulator's inductor is chosen for the worst case: the highest input voltage and the heaviest load.

Decoupling capacitors

Digital chips don't draw a steady current. Every time their internal logic switches, they take a burst of current lasting a few nanoseconds. The supply that feeds them is centimetres away, and the trace in between has inductance. From \(v = L\,di/dt\), a fast change in current through that inductance means a voltage drop: the chip's supply dips at exactly the moment it needs current. Large enough dips cause resets, corrupted data and noise on every signal the chip produces.

A decoupling capacitor placed right next to the chip's power pin supplies those bursts locally, over a path with very little inductance, and recharges from the supply between bursts. The usual starting point is a 100 nF ceramic at every power pin, as close as possible, plus a few microfarads of bulk capacitance nearby. Module 12 covers placement in detail.

Things to try
  1. Select None. The burst has to come through the supply trace. How far does the pin voltage dip?
  2. Add the 100 nF capacitor at the pin. The dip shrinks because that capacitor, not the distant supply, provides the charge.
  3. Move that capacitor 1 cm away, then try the 100 µF electrolytic at the pin. In both cases more inductance sits in the path the burst has to take.

With no local capacitor, the burst current has to change in the supply trace. From \(v = L\,di/dt\), that inductance drops the voltage at the pin for the length of the edge. The ringing afterwards is the trace inductance and the chip's own small capacitance trading the energy back and forth.

A capacitor next to the pin already holds charge at the supply voltage. The burst takes that charge over a few millimetres, so the long trace is hardly in the path. Between bursts the supply puts the charge back slowly, which the trace can do. Move the same capacitor a centimetre away and that centimetre's inductance sits between the stored charge and the pin: the capacitor still has the charge, and the pin still sees \(L\,di/dt\).

A physically larger part has more inductance in its own body and leads, so the fast edge drops voltage there too, even though the part stores more charge for a slower demand. That is why the usual starting point is a small capacitor right at the pin, with a larger one nearby for the slower current. A 10 nF part at the pin runs out of charge during a 30 ns burst (\(\Delta V \approx It/C\)), which is the other way a decoupling capacitor fails, still in the time domain. How a capacitor's own inductance makes it stop acting as a capacitor above some frequency, and the impedance curves that show it, are Module 4. Parallel capacitors can also resonate with each other; Module 12 covers that with placement.

Check your understandingMoving the decoupling capacitor a centimetre away

A 100 nF capacitor sits beside a chip's power pin and keeps the supply dip small during the chip's fast current bursts. To make room, someone moves that same capacitor 1 cm away along the supply trace.

What happens to the dip?

  1. It gets smaller: a centimetre of trace adds capacitance.
    A centimetre of trace is about 10 nH of inductance. Its capacitance is negligible next to 100 nF.
  2. Nothing: the capacitance did not change.
    The stored charge is the same, but the path from that charge to the pin now includes the trace inductance.
  3. It gets worse. The capacitor still supplies the charge, but the current has to change in the inductance of that centimetre, and \(v = L\,di/dt\) appears between the capacitor and the pin.
    Decoupling fails when inductance sits in the path of the burst, even if the capacitor value is untouched.
  4. It gets worse only for an electrolytic; a ceramic is immune to where it sits.
    Any capacitor moved away puts inductance between its charge and the pin. A larger package adds still more inductance of its own.

The burst lasts tens of nanoseconds. The charge that supplies it has to come from somewhere close, because inductance will not pass a fast change in current without dropping voltage. A centimetre of trace is roughly 10 nH. For a 200 mA edge in 2 ns, \(L\,\Delta i/\Delta t = 10\,\text{nH} \times 0.2\,\text{A} / 2\,\text{ns} = 1\,\text{V}\). The figure's dip is smaller than that bare estimate, because the edge is not a pure step into that inductance alone, but moving the 100 nF part from the pin to 1 cm away makes the dip several times worse. The value of the capacitor did not change. Its location did.

Exercise 1Design · ●●○Sizing a hold-up capacitor

A board runs from a 2S pack through a series diode and a linear regulator. When a motor on the same pack starts, the pack can drop out for up to 5 ms. The diode stops the board's input capacitor from discharging back into the motor, and it also sits in series while the pack is healthy, so the capacitor never charges all the way up to the pack voltage. You want the board to keep running through the dropout. A linear regulator draws the same current at its input as it delivers at its output. Design to the worst case you can actually count on, the way a datasheet's guaranteed minimum is what you design to rather than its typical value (Module 12).

Given Pack, nominal7.4 V (3.7 V per cell). Not the number to design to. Pack while sagged6.6 V Series diode0.7 V (a Schottky would be about 0.3 V; the larger drop is the worse case) Regulator's minimum input3.6 V Board current80 mA Dropout length5 ms

(a) What voltage is the input capacitor actually sitting at, just before the dropout?

That's the nominal pack voltage. The pack is sagged, and the diode drops voltage of its own.

That's the sagged pack, but the diode is still in series with the capacitor.

6.6 − 0.7 = 5.9 V. The nominal 7.4 V never appears on the capacitor in the case you have to survive.

(b) How far can that capacitor's voltage fall before the regulator stops working?

That's 7.4 − 3.6, the nominal pack with the diode ignored. The capacitor doesn't start at 7.4 V.

That's 6.6 − 3.6, which counts the sag and still ignores the diode drop.

5.9 − 3.6 = 2.3 V. A Schottky at 0.3 V would leave 6.3 − 3.6 = 2.7 V of room. The ordinary diode is the tighter case, so ΔV is 2.3 V.

(c) What's the minimum capacitance at the regulator's input?

That's \(I t\) divided by 3.8 V, the nominal pack with no diode. It is too small for the voltage the capacitor actually starts from.

That's \(I t\) divided by 3.0 V: sag counted, diode ignored.

That's what you'd need on the 3.3 V output, where only 0.3 V of droop is allowed. The capacitor is on the input.

\(C = I\,t / \Delta V\), with the ΔV from (b).

\(C = 0.080 \times 0.005 / 2.3 = 174\ \mu\text{F}\), about 175 µF. A 220 µF electrolytic is the smallest common value with margin for tolerance, ageing and cold. Designing from the nominal 7.4 V and forgetting the diode would have specified 105 µF, and that part runs out before the 5 ms is over.

Discussion: worst case, and why the capacitor goes before the regulator

The 7.4 V figure is a nominal, in the same sense as a datasheet "typical" (Module 12): useful as a label, not as a number the circuit is guaranteed to see. The case to design to is a sagged pack and the diode you added to stop the capacitor feeding the motor. Between the two diode drops, 0.7 V and a Schottky's 0.3 V, the larger one leaves less room (2.3 V rather than 2.7 V) and so asks for more capacitance (174 µF rather than about 150 µF). On the 3.3 V output the capacitor can only fall 0.3 V before the microcontroller stops, so the same 80 mA for 5 ms takes \(0.080 \times 0.005 / 0.3 = 1330\ \mu\text{F}\), about eight times the input-side part. The energy view says why the input side still wins: the capacitor delivers \(\tfrac12 C(V_1^2 - V_2^2)\), and swinging from 5.9 V to 3.6 V makes more of the stored energy available than swinging from 3.3 V to 3.0 V. The diode's other job remains: without it, the capacitor would discharge into the starting motor instead of into the board.

Exercise 2Analysis · ●●○Inrush when connecting a 4S pack

A motor controller has 1000 µF across its input. It's connected to a fully charged 4S LiPo with an ordinary connector. Everything in the path, from the battery's internal resistance to the capacitors' ESR, adds up to 0.1 Ω.

Given Battery, full charge4 × 4.2 V = 16.8 V Input capacitance1000 µF Total path resistance0.1 Ω

(a) Peak inrush current?

\(16.8 / 0.1 = 168\ \text{A}\), through a connector rated for perhaps 30 A continuous.

(b) Time constant of the surge?

\(\tau = 0.1 \times 1000\,\mu\text{F} = 100\ \mu\text{s}\). The surge is mostly over within half a millisecond.

(c) Energy stored in the capacitors once they're charged?

\(\tfrac12 \times 10^{-3} \times 16.8^2 = 0.141\ \text{J}\).

Discussion: where else does energy go?

A useful result: charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through any resistance wastes exactly as much energy as ends up stored, here another 0.141 J. With a small resistance it's dumped in a fraction of a millisecond, much of it at the connector contacts as they touch, which is what pits and welds them. A precharge resistor doesn't reduce that energy, but it spreads it over a resistor designed to take it and over a much longer time. Anti-spark connectors build that resistor into the plug.

Exercise 3Design · ●●●Protecting a solenoid's switch

A transistor switches a 12 V solenoid valve. The transistor is rated for 30 V and turns off in about 1 µs.

Given Supply12 V Coil24 Ω, 30 mH Transistor rating30 V Flyback diode forward drop0.7 V

(a) Steady coil current?

\(12 / 24 = 0.5\ \text{A}\). In steady state the inductor is just its 24 Ω of wire.

(b) Without protection, what voltage would the coil generate to stop 0.5 A in 1 µs?

\(v = L\,\Delta i/\Delta t\).

\(0.03 \times 0.5 / 10^{-6} = 15\,000\ \text{V}\). It never reaches that: the 30 V transistor breaks down first and absorbs the coil's energy.

(c) With a flyback diode across the coil, what's the highest voltage the transistor sees?

That's the voltage across the diode (and the coil). The transistor sits between the bottom of the coil and ground, so it also has the 12 V supply above it.

The coil's bottom end rises until the diode conducts, 0.7 V above the 12 V supply: 12.7 V, comfortably inside 30 V.

(d) With the diode, roughly how long is the time constant of the current dying away?

\(\tau = L/R = 0.03 / 24 = 1.25\ \text{ms}\), so the current takes around 5 ms to die away and the valve closes correspondingly later.

Discussion: when a slow release matters

If the valve must close quickly, put a Zener or TVS diode in series with the flyback diode, for example a 15 V Zener. The coil voltage can then rise to about 15.7 V, so the energy is absorbed several times faster, and the transistor sees about 12 + 15.7 ≈ 28 V. That's under 30 V but with little margin, so a 60 V transistor would be the better choice. Module 5 covers Zeners and TVS diodes.

Comparison. Set what you measured next to what you wrote down. A τ within the capacitor's tolerance of RC, a kickback spike that collapses to about a diode drop above the supply once the diode is fitted, and visibly less ringing with the ground spring than with the long lead, are what the predictions were aiming at. If the spike or the ringing is not where you put it, the measurement may be the thing that changed, not the circuit.

Summary & cheat sheet

  1. A capacitor's voltage can't change instantly (\(i = C\,dv/dt\)); an inductor's current can't (\(v = L\,di/dt\)). In settled DC, a capacitor is an open circuit and an inductor is a wire.
  2. RC and RL circuits change exponentially with time constants τ = RC and τ = L/R: 63 % after one τ, within 1 % after five.
  3. Empty capacitors look like a short circuit at switch-on. Inrush current is limited only by the resistance in the path.
  4. Interrupting an inductor's current produces a large voltage spike. Put a flyback diode across every relay, solenoid and motor coil you switch.
  5. Real capacitors have tolerance, ESR, a voltage rating and, for X5R and X7R ceramics, a capacitance that shrinks with DC voltage.
  6. Real inductors have winding resistance, a saturation current beyond which their inductance collapses, and a thermal current rating. Keep peak current below Isat.
  7. A decoupling capacitor next to a chip supplies the current burst locally. Inductance in that path, a long trace or a capacitor placed away from the pin, is what makes the supply dip.
  8. Before the bench, read the oscilloscope section in Module 14: set the scope for the probe you're using, clip ground only to ground, and use the ground spring for fast edges.
